## Recognise

An allergic reaction occurs after contact with something a person is allergic to. The most common causes of a severe reaction are food, insect venom (such as a bee or wasp sting), medicines and latex. Plants, a vaccine, or exertion combined with wheat can also trigger a reaction. Someone who has had a reaction before often knows what they are allergic to and sometimes carries medication. Ask them.

Mild allergic reaction:

- red, itchy eyes, sneezing, a runny nose;
- itching, swelling or hives on the skin, including on the face;
- abdominal cramps, diarrhoea, vomiting.

Severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis). Be alert when someone suddenly becomes unwell:

- breathing problems: shortness of breath, wheezing, tightness in the chest;
- swelling of the tongue, throat or larynx, with hoarseness or noisy breathing. Difficulty swallowing is often the first sign;
- a swollen face, red or pink skin, itching or redness over the whole body;
- headache, prolonged dizziness, nausea, diarrhoea or vomiting;
- anxiety, drowsiness, confusion, pale or grey skin;
- signs of shock, up to loss of consciousness.

A mild reaction can unpredictably turn into a life-threatening reaction within a few minutes. How severe a reaction becomes varies from person to person and even from one time to the next.

## What to do

### Severe allergic reaction

1. Ensure your own safety and that of the casualty. Move the casualty away from what is causing the reaction, or remove it, for example by rinsing it off the skin.
2. Assess the condition: find out what is wrong, check for a response, open the airway if necessary and check the breathing.
3. Call 112 or have someone call and say that it is an allergic reaction. If you cannot get through, see Call 112 if.
4. Also try to reach a GP nearby. They may have adrenaline in stock and are sometimes on the scene sooner.
5. If the casualty has their own adrenaline auto-injector with them, give it to the casualty so that they use it themselves. Help them with it. The best place is the middle of the outer side of the thigh. It may go through clothing, if the clothing is not thick.
6. You must not administer the auto-injector on your own initiative. Only do so if the casualty explicitly asks for it and can no longer do it themselves.
7. Choose the position:
   - Signs of shock: carefully lay the casualty down.
   - If the casualty finds it harder to breathe when lying down: help them to sit or half-sit in a comfortable position.
8. Regularly check the level of consciousness and the breathing until the emergency services arrive.
9. Tell the emergency services whether an auto-injector has been used and when.
10. If the casualty becomes unconscious and is not breathing normally, start CPR (see topic 2).
11. After use, follow the normal rules for disposing of used medicines and medical devices.

After using an auto-injector, the casualty must always go to hospital.

Adrenaline can cause side effects that can also be part of the allergic reaction itself: a fast, pounding or irregular heartbeat, agitation or anxiety, weakness or trembling, nausea and vomiting, throbbing headache, dizziness, paleness and sweating.

### Sting in the mouth or throat

1. Call 112 immediately or have someone call. If you cannot get through, see Call 112 if.
2. Let the casualty suck on ice or rinse their mouth with cold water. That can temporarily reduce the swelling.

### Bee sting without a severe reaction

1. Scrape the sting out with a hard object, such as a fingernail, a bank card or the blunt side of a knife. Start below the sting site and work upwards.
2. Do not use tweezers or fingers: you will squeeze the venom sac empty.
3. Carefully remove jewellery from the stung hand or finger.
4. Cool for no more than 20 minutes with ice in a bag of water or a cold pack, wrapped in a thin towel. If you have no ice, use cold water.
5. Clean the small wound with water, or disinfect it if there is no water.
6. Do not let the casualty scratch.

### Mild allergic reaction

1. Ask about known allergies and prescribed medication.
2. Remove what is causing the reaction or take the person out of that environment.
3. Help the person into a comfortable position and help them take their own medication if they have it with them.
4. Do not let them scratch itchy skin.
5. With hay fever, rinsing the eyes or nose can give relief.
6. Keep monitoring the person closely: a mild reaction can become a severe one.

## If help is a long time coming

- According to the IFRC, a second auto-injector may be considered if the emergency services have not yet arrived and the casualty does not improve within 5 to 10 minutes of the first. This only applies to the casualty's own prescribed auto-injector. Even then the Belgian rule applies: the casualty uses it themselves, and you only administer it at their explicit request if they can no longer do it themselves.
- If the casualty has no auto-injector, try every way of reaching medical help: 112 and a GP nearby.
- A severe allergic reaction can come back after everything seemed to be over, between 1 and 78 hours after the start. This happens more often after a severe reaction or if more than one auto-injector was needed. So monitor someone who has had a severe reaction or received an auto-injector for the 3 days afterwards.
- Keep checking the level of consciousness and the breathing regularly. If the casualty becomes unconscious and is not breathing normally, perform CPR.
- Keep the casualty lying down if they have signs of shock, or sitting if they have difficulty breathing. Keep them warm.
- Avoid any new contact with what caused the reaction.
- Anyone with an allergy should always carry an allergy card and their prescribed medication, and make sure the people around them know where these are. Anyone with a known allergy who travels to a remote place should prepare extra carefully for this. Think in advance about how you will help yourself if you are alone when a reaction starts.

## Call 112 if

- you suspect a severe allergic reaction: immediately;
- the casualty has difficulty breathing or swallowing, or becomes hoarse;
- the casualty feels unwell or gets abdominal cramps after contact with a known allergen;
- there is itching or redness over the whole body;
- the casualty loses consciousness;
- someone has been stung in the mouth or throat;
- an auto-injector has been used.

Consult a doctor after a sting if the sting cannot be removed, with extensive redness or a painful, itchy swelling, if the casualty feels unwell up to 24 hours later, if they have been stung very many times, with signs of inflammation (redness, pain, warmth, swelling) or if they are probably not sufficiently protected against tetanus.

**If you cannot get through**

- Only leave the casualty alone if there is no other option. A mild reaction can become life-threatening within a few minutes. Point directly at a bystander to get help and tell them where it is, what happened, how many casualties there are and what condition they are in.
- Also try to reach a GP nearby (see What to do).
- If you are giving CPR and are alone without a phone, see topic 2 (If you cannot get through).
- See also topic 1 (If you cannot get through: getting help without a phone) and Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call.

## Do not

- Do not administer an adrenaline auto-injector on your own initiative. Only do so at the explicit request of the casualty if they can no longer do it themselves.
- Do not use an auto-injector that has not been prescribed for the casualty.
- Do not lay the casualty down if that makes breathing harder.
- Do not wait if there are signs of an allergic reaction.
- Do not grip a sting with tweezers or your fingers.
- Do not let the casualty scratch.
- Do not leave someone without monitoring during the first days after a severe reaction.
