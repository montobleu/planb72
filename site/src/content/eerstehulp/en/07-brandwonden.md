## Recognise

A burn is caused by heat (fire, steam, hot liquid or objects), chemicals, electricity or radiation, including the sun. Even after the source has gone, the burning continues in the layers of the skin for a while. That is why cooling quickly is so important.

How serious it is depends on the depth and the size:

- First degree: red, swollen, painful.
- Second degree: blisters, painful.
- Third degree: black, parchment-like or white.

A chemical burn hurts immediately. The skin may blister or become irritated after contact with the product.

An electrical burn is mainly at the place where the current entered the body and where it left. There may also be internal burns that you cannot see.

Children younger than 5 years and people older than 60 years are more vulnerable. Even a burn that looks small can be serious for a baby, a child or an older person.

## What to do

### Burn caused by heat

1. Make sure the cause has been safely removed. For example, cover a pan of burning oil.
2. Wash or disinfect your hands and put on disposable gloves.
3. Check the response and the breathing. Open the airway if necessary.
4. Cool as quickly as possible with cool or lukewarm running tap water. Aim the stream just above the burn, so that the water flows over the wound. That is more comfortable than the stream directly on the wound.
5. Cool for at least 10 minutes and preferably 20 minutes. Continue until the pain has eased.
6. Do not use ice-cold water and do not use ice.
7. Remove clothing and jewellery on and around the burn, unless they are stuck to the skin.
8. After cooling, cover the burn according to how serious it is:
   - Small first-degree burn: apply a moisturising product, such as a hydrogel or after-sun, and cover with a plaster or sterile dressing.
   - Small second-degree burn with intact skin: cover with a burn dressing. If you do not have one, apply hydrogel and cover with a dressing.
   - Large second- or third-degree burn: after cooling, apply a wet wound dressing, as sterile as possible, with a triangular bandage, dressing or clean cloth. Cling film can be used temporarily. Lay the film over an arm or leg, not tightly around it, because it may swell. Call 112.
9. Leave blisters intact. Do not burst them.
10. Ask whether the casualty is vaccinated against tetanus.
11. Watch for signs of poisoning after a fire: toxic gases may have been released.
12. Reassure the casualty. Monitor the level of consciousness, the breathing and the temperature, especially with a child or a vulnerable person.

### Large burn: risk of shock

1. Have the casualty lie down.
2. Keep them warm.
3. Prevent the burn from coming into contact with the ground, so that it stays clean.
4. Call 112 (see topic 6).

### Chemical burn

1. Ensure your own safety. Do not touch the product without gloves that are resistant to it. Ventilate if there are fumes.
2. Call 112. Then call the Poison Centre on 070 245 245 for advice on first aid. Keep the packaging at hand.
3. Rinse the skin with running water until the pain eases.
4. Make sure the diluted product does not run over healthy skin or into the other eye.
5. Remove contaminated clothing and jewellery, unless they are stuck to the skin.

### Chemical in the eye

1. Rinse the eye immediately with clean water.
2. Remove contact lenses while rinsing.
3. Hold the head so that the rinsing water does not run into the other eye or over healthy skin.
4. Do not apply any other products without medical advice.
5. The casualty needs help afterwards and must not drive a car themselves.

### Electrical burn

1. Do not touch the casualty as long as they are in contact with the power source. First switch off the power (see topic 13).
2. Call 112.
3. Cool and cover the visible burns as above.
4. Bear in mind that there may be internal injuries.

## If help is a long time coming

The IFRC gives solutions for when there is no tap water or dressing material:

- No running water: cool with another cool liquid, such as juice or milk.
- Little water: put a bowl under the burn and pour the water over the wound into the bowl. That way you can reuse the water. This works better than holding the wound in a bowl of water, because the body warms up still water.
- No dressing: use what is available locally, such as aloe vera, honey or banana leaves. After cooling, petroleum jelly or honey can be beneficial on a burn caused by heat.
- During transport: protect the burn with a clean wet cloth or plastic cling film.

Also:

- Leave blisters intact. A blister that has been burst becomes infected more quickly.
- Monitor the temperature, especially in children and older people. Cooling for a long time with cold water can cause hypothermia.
- Keep a casualty with a large burn lying down and warm. Monitor the level of consciousness and the breathing.
- Watch for infection over the following days: if the skin around the burn becomes warm or painful, or the person develops a fever, seek medical advice as soon as possible.
- A burn is a high-risk wound for tetanus. With a high-risk wound, a booster vaccination may be needed if the last vaccination was more than 5 years ago. Anyone who is not fully vaccinated needs extra protection. Discuss this with a doctor as soon as possible (see topic 17).

## Call 112 if

- the burn is a large second- or third-degree burn;
- the burn is on the eyes, mouth or throat, or goes all the way round the neck, the trunk or a limb;
- the casualty is younger than 5 years or older than 60 years;
- the burn was caused by chemicals or electricity;
- there are serious symptoms, such as unconsciousness or breathing difficulties, or if the condition gets worse;
- you are in doubt.

Consult a doctor for a large first-degree burn, a second- or third-degree burn larger than a 2 euro coin, a burn on the face, hands, feet, joints or genitals, a burn caused by flames, or if the pain persists after 15 minutes of cooling.

With a chemical product, always call the Poison Centre as well: 070 245 245.

## Do not

- Do not put ice on a burn and do not cool with ice-cold water.
- Do not burst blisters and do not remove the skin of a blister.
- Do not pull away clothing or jewellery that is stuck to the skin.
- Do not wrap cling film tightly around an arm or leg.
- Do not touch a chemical product with bare hands.
- Do not try to dilute a swallowed corrosive product. Call the Poison Centre or 112.
- Without medical advice, do not put anything other than clean water into an eye with a chemical product in it.
- Do not touch the casualty of an electrical accident as long as the power is not off.
- Do not use an ointment containing silver sulfadiazine. The IFRC says that it is associated with poorer healing.
