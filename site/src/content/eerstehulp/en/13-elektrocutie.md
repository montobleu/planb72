## Recognise

Terms:

- Electric shock: contact with a harmful electric current.
- Electrocution: an electric shock that leads to cardiac arrest.
- Low voltage: alternating voltage below 1000 volts, such as the household installation of 230 volts. The current passes through the body.
- High voltage: for example a high-voltage pylon or the overhead line of a railway. The current passes mainly over the body. The casualty may be thrown away and always has serious injuries.

With low voltage, the casualty may:

- be unconscious;
- have difficulty breathing or no longer be breathing;
- have heart rhythm disturbances or a cardiac arrest;
- have burns, especially at the place where the current entered and where it left the body, and also internally;
- have severe muscle cramps.

With high voltage, the casualty may also:

- be lying some distance from the power source because they were thrown;
- have torn clothing, or have lost gloves or a helmet because of the blast of air.

Electrical burns increase the risk of blood clots. Signs: a swollen and painful leg (or both legs), discoloured skin on the leg and visibly swollen veins.

## What to do

### Step 1: make sure the scene is safe

Low voltage:

1. Never touch a casualty who is still connected to the power source, unless the power is off.
2. Switch off the power as quickly as possible: pull the plug out of the socket (do not pull on the cable), remove the fuses, or switch off the circuit breaker or the main switch.
3. Make sure the power cannot be switched on again.
4. If you cannot switch off the power, try to separate the casualty and the power source. First insulate yourself from the ground, for example by standing on a dry wooden chair or on dry insulating material. Water conducts electricity: do not stand in or on water.
5. Free the casualty from the electrical object.

High voltage:

1. Do not go near the power source or the casualty. Stay at least 10 metres away. The zone up to 10 metres around the power source is live. High voltage can jump 3 to 5 metres, so you can be electrocuted without touching the conductor.
2. A broken cable on the ground may still be live.
3. Do not try to remove the power source, not even with an object that does not conduct. The voltage is too high.
4. Call 112 and leave the rescue to the emergency services.
5. In a railway accident: if you are on the train, wait for the instructions of the staff. If you are outside, keep a sufficient distance.

### Step 2: assess the condition

1. Make first contact with the casualty by briefly touching them with the back of your hand, and only when the power is off or the casualty is no longer connected.
2. Find out what is wrong.
3. Check for a response: ask loudly "Are you all right?" and gently shake the shoulders.
4. If they do not respond: alert 112, open the airway (tilt the head, lift the chin) and check the breathing: look, listen and feel, for no more than 10 seconds.

### Step 3: get specialised help

- Unconscious: call 112.
- Serious injuries or doubt: call 112.
- If the casualty felt "strange" for a moment after a low-voltage accident: have them examined by a doctor, even if it does not seem serious.

### Step 4: give further first aid

1. If the casualty is not breathing normally: start CPR with 30 chest compressions (5 to 6 cm deep, 100 to 120 per minute) and 2 rescue breaths. Have someone fetch an AED and follow the instructions of the AED (see topic 2).
2. If they are breathing normally but do not respond: place them in the recovery position and keep checking the level of consciousness and the breathing (see topic 5).
3. Give first aid according to the injuries. Cool burns with running water, for at least 10 and preferably 20 minutes, and cover them (see topic 7).
4. If the casualty was thrown by high voltage, assume serious injuries.

## If help is a long time coming

- Also when power cables are lying on the ground after a storm or a disaster: stay at least 10 metres away and keep others away. A cable on the ground may still be live. In any disaster, first assess whether any power lines are exposed.
- CPR alone rarely restarts the heart. Have someone look for an AED and continue CPR in the meantime. Take turns with other rescuers every 1 to 2 minutes.
- Electricity can cause internal injuries that you cannot see, and symptoms may appear later. So keep monitoring the level of consciousness and the breathing even if the casualty feels well.
- Over the following days, watch for signs of blood clots in the legs: swelling, pain, discoloured skin and swollen veins. If so, seek medical help.
- Treat burns as in topic 7. If you have little water, pour it over the wound into a bowl and reuse it. Watch for signs of infection: warmth or pain around the burn, or fever.
- If the casualty felt "strange" for a moment after a low-voltage accident, have them examined by a doctor as soon as possible, even if it does not seem serious.

## Call 112 if

- it is a high-voltage accident;
- the casualty is seriously injured;
- the casualty has difficulty breathing;
- the casualty is or has been unconscious;
- there are electrical burns;
- you are in doubt.

Consult a doctor after a low-voltage accident if the casualty says they felt "strange" for a moment. There may be internal injuries or symptoms that appear later.

## Do not

- Do not touch a casualty who is still connected to the power source, unless the power is off.
- With high voltage, do not come within 10 metres of the power source or the casualty.
- With high voltage, do not remove the power source yourself, not even with an object that does not conduct.
- Do not stand in or on water when you try to switch off the power.
- Do not unplug a plug by pulling on the cable.
- Do not repair electrical appliances yourself and do not work on appliances that are live.
- Do not assume that everything is fine because the casualty feels well.
