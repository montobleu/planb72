## Recognise

### Stroke

The signs of a stroke point to brain injury. The sooner medical help is available, the less damage to the brain. Possible symptoms, usually sudden:

- paralysis, numbness or weakness on one side of the body: face, arm or leg;
- a lopsided mouth, a drooping corner of the mouth;
- difficulty speaking, slurred speech, not finding the words or not understanding others;
- difficulty swallowing;
- suddenly seeing less well with one or both eyes;
- dizziness, loss of balance, difficulty walking or with coordination;
- sudden, severe headache without a known cause;
- nausea, disturbed level of consciousness;
- a seizure in someone without epilepsy.

Do the FAST test. Have the casualty sit down for it.

- F (Face): ask them to smile or show their teeth. Is the mouth lopsided or does one corner of the mouth droop?
- A (Arm): ask them to stretch both arms out in front of them at the same time with the palms facing up. Do they stay at the same height, or does one arm drift down or turn?
- S (Speech): does the casualty speak unclearly or not find the words? Ask the casualty themselves or bystanders who know them. If necessary, have them repeat a simple sentence.
- T (Time): try to find out when the symptoms started, or when the casualty was last seen behaving normally. Call 112.

Mild symptoms that last only a few minutes may point to a small, temporary stroke (TIA). Even then, medical care is needed quickly. Symptoms that resemble a stroke can also be caused by blood sugar that is too high or too low.

### Heart attack (myocardial infarction)

Typical symptoms:

- pressing or cramping pain, or a tight, constricting feeling in the chest. People also describe it as discomfort, a feeling like being in a vice, squeezing or burning;
- pain that spreads to the left or right arm, the right shoulder, the neck, the lower jaw, the teeth, the upper abdomen or between the shoulder blades;
- shortness of breath, pale or grey skin, blue lips and fingernails;
- sweating, coughing, anxiety, nausea, vomiting;
- pain that lasts more than a few minutes, or that comes and goes.

A heart attack can also start with less obvious symptoms: pain in the neck or arms, dizziness, tiredness, headache, hot flushes or loss of consciousness. In women, pain in the arms, shoulder blades or jaw, nausea, vomiting, shortness of breath, coughing, headache and anxiety are more common. In men, mainly chest pain and sweating. In women, older people and people with diabetes, the symptoms may be less noticeable. A heart attack can even occur without symptoms. A heart attack is often mistaken for stomach complaints.

With angina pectoris, the pain is often familiar and improves with rest. With a heart attack, the pain persists. If you are in doubt, assume that it may be a heart attack.

## What to do

### Stroke

1. Make sure the scene is safe. If you have to move the casualty, support them on the paralysed side.
2. Call 112 as soon as you recognise the first symptoms. Say that you suspect a stroke and when the symptoms started.
3. Write down the time at which the symptoms started.
4. Check the response and the breathing. Open the airway if necessary.
5. Let the casualty rest and make no further effort.
6. Help them adopt a comfortable position, lying down or sitting, depending on what they can still do physically and mentally. If they are sitting lopsided, support them so that they do not fall.
7. Do not give anything to eat or drink.
8. Reassure the casualty and keep talking calmly until the emergency services arrive.
9. If they lose consciousness: place them in the recovery position and check the level of consciousness and the breathing every minute. If they are not breathing normally, start CPR.
10. Wash or disinfect your hands after giving first aid.

### Heart attack

1. Call 112. Do this even if the symptoms pass within 5 minutes. Say that you suspect a heart attack, so that the call gets the right priority.
2. Check the response and the breathing.
3. Have the casualty stop all exertion and rest.
4. Help them adopt a comfortable position, for example sitting.
5. Make sure they can breathe freely. Loosen tight clothing.
6. Ask whether they have had this pain before and whether they take medication for it. Let the casualty take their own medication if they want to.
7. Have someone fetch an AED and put it close to the casualty, in case it is needed.
8. Reassure the casualty. Stay with them until the emergency services arrive and monitor the breathing and level of consciousness.
9. If the condition changes, for example if the casualty collapses, report this to 112.
10. If the casualty becomes unconscious and is not breathing normally, start CPR and use the AED (see topic 2).

## If help is a long time coming

- With a stroke and a heart attack, every minute counts. The sooner there is medical help, the less damage to the brain or heart. So look for the fastest way to care.
- In a remote area it can be useful to drive towards the nearest hospital and meet the ambulance on the way.
- If it takes a long time for the emergency services to arrive, have someone fetch an AED and keep it close to the casualty. A heart attack can turn into cardiac arrest.
- Keep the casualty at rest, without exertion.
- Monitor the breathing and level of consciousness continuously. Write down the time at which the symptoms started and every change. Pass this on to the healthcare professionals.
- Even if the symptoms of a stroke disappear, the casualty must see a doctor. The signs point to brain injury.
- If someone has known angina pectoris and takes their own medication, 112 is sometimes not needed. Do call 112 if the pain does not disappear within a few minutes of the medication as it usually does.

## Call 112 if

- you suspect a stroke: immediately at the first symptoms;
- you suspect a heart attack, even if the symptoms pass within 5 minutes;
- the pain is severe, the casualty is short of breath, the skin is pale or grey and clammy, or the lips, ears, fingers or toes turn blue;
- the chest pain persists after taking their own medication;
- the casualty becomes unconscious.

## Do not

- Do not wait to see whether the symptoms pass.
- Do not give anything to eat or drink if a stroke is suspected.
- Do not let the casualty exert themselves.
- Do not move someone with paralysis without supporting them on the paralysed side.
- Do not give oxygen for a stroke, even if you are trained.
