## Recognise

Start CPR immediately if someone suddenly collapses, does not respond and is not breathing normally. If in doubt, start as well.

- Gasping, groaning, gurgling, snoring or sighing is not normal breathing. It is agonal breathing, which is common in the first minutes of a cardiac arrest.
- In someone who collapses during sport or play, breathing may seem laboured and the eyes are often open. Even so, it can be a cardiac arrest.
- Brief jerking movements are possible, especially in younger people. Check the breathing as soon as they stop.
- Feeling for a pulse is not necessary and is unreliable.

Ages: a baby is younger than 1 year, a child is 1 to 12 years, an adolescent 13 to 18 years. If you are unsure whether someone is an adolescent or an adult, follow the procedure for adults.

## What to do

### Adult

1. Make sure the scene is safe. Check for a response: speak loudly and gently shake the shoulders.
2. Shout for help. Point to someone.
3. Open the airway: tilt the head back and lift the chin. Look, listen and feel for no more than 10 seconds to see whether the person is breathing normally.
4. Call 112 or have someone call, with the phone on speaker. If the connection works and nobody has first aid knowledge, the operator will give instructions over the phone. If you cannot get through, send someone for help (see Call 112 if). If you are alone without a working phone and have to leave the casualty to raise the alarm: first go and raise the alarm, come back and then start CPR.
5. Have someone fetch an AED if there is one nearby. An AED is kept in a green cabinet or case. If you are alone, do not leave the casualty, unless the AED is close by and visible.
6. Place your hands in the centre of the chest, on the lower half of the breastbone. Put your strongest hand against the breastbone and the other on top of it. Interlock your fingers.
7. Give 30 chest compressions. Press down about 5 cm, not more than 6 cm. Press 100 to 120 times per minute. Let the chest recoil fully after each compression and do not lean on it.
8. Perform CPR on a firm surface if possible. Do not move the casualty if that takes time, for example from a bed to the floor. On a soft surface, push deeper.
9. After 30 compressions, open the airway. Pinch the nose closed and blow into the mouth for 1 second, just hard enough to make the chest visibly rise. Give 2 rescue breaths. Keep the interruption shorter than 10 seconds.
10. If the chest does not rise, quickly check whether the airway is blocked.
11. Alternate 30 compressions with 2 rescue breaths.
12. If you are not trained or do not want to give rescue breaths: give chest compressions only, without stopping, at least 100 per minute. Rescue breaths are especially useful for someone who was unable to breathe, such as after drowning, choking or suffocation, and for children.
13. When the AED arrives: switch it on and follow the spoken instructions. Stick the 2 pads on the bare chest: one below the right collarbone next to the breastbone, the other on the left below the armpit. Do not stick them on breast tissue and move a bra out of the way. You may quickly remove a lot of chest hair if that does not delay the shock for long.
14. Continue compressions until the AED is ready to analyse. Make sure nobody touches the casualty during the analysis and the shock.
15. Resume compressions immediately after the shock. Do not wait to check the pulse or the heart rhythm. After each analysis, follow the instructions of the AED and resume CPR.

### With two or more rescuers

1. Start compressions yourself and meanwhile give instructions to the other rescuer: call 112, or go and get help if calling does not work, and fetch the AED.
2. When the rescuer returns with the AED, let them attach the pads while you continue compressions. Or the other way round.
3. Change the person giving CPR every 2 minutes, so that the compressions stay effective. Change over without interrupting the compressions.

### Child (1 year to puberty) and baby (younger than 1 year)

In children and babies, cardiac arrest is usually caused by a breathing problem. That is why rescue breaths are more important here.

1. Check for a response. With a baby, tap the sole of the foot or pick the baby up. A baby who does not respond is floppy and has an abnormal colour.
2. Open the airway. With a child, tilt the head back and lift the chin. With a baby, bring the head into a neutral position and lift the chin.
3. Check the breathing for no more than 10 seconds.
4. Have someone call 112 or call yourself with the speaker on. If you have no way of calling while you perform CPR, perform CPR for 1 minute first and then call or go and get help.
5. First give 5 rescue breaths. With a baby, put your mouth over the mouth and nose. With an older child, give mouth-to-mouth. Blow steadily for 1 second until the chest or abdomen rises.
6. Then give 30 chest compressions on the lower half of the breastbone, 100 to 120 per minute.
   - Baby: use two thumbs while your hands encircle the chest. Press down at least one third of the depth of the chest, about 4 cm.
   - Child: press with one or two hands, depending on the size of the child and of yourself. Two hands give better compressions and less fatigue. Press down one third of the depth of the chest, about 5 cm.
   - Adolescent: use the two-handed technique as for adults.
7. Give 2 rescue breaths and alternate 30 compressions with 2 rescue breaths.
8. If you do not want to or cannot give rescue breaths, give compressions only, 100 to 120 per minute. If the connection works, the operator can guide you through this.
9. AED on a child: follow the instructions. Children up to 25 kg (about 8 years) and babies: put the AED in child mode or use child pads if possible. Then stick one pad on the chest, to the left of the centre, and the other on the back between the shoulder blades. Heavier children and adolescents: use the AED as for adults. If the AED has no child instructions, use it in the normal setting.

### When to stop

Continue until the emergency services take over or ask you to stop, until the casualty clearly recovers (coughing, opening the eyes, speaking, moving purposefully and breathing normally), or until you are exhausted. Do not stop when the ambulance arrives, but only when the healthcare professionals take over. If the casualty recovers, keep monitoring them closely: they may stop breathing again.

## If help is a long time coming

- CPR alone rarely restarts the heart. The heart usually needs a shock from a defibrillator. So have someone look for an AED as quickly as possible. Every minute of delay until the shock reduces the chance of survival by about 10 per cent.
- Take turns with other rescuers every 1 to 2 minutes, so that nobody becomes exhausted. Briefly teach bystanders how to give compressions.
- If the casualty has to be transported from a remote area to medical care, CPR must continue without interruption during transport, on a firm surface.
- After drowning or in hypothermia, there is a chance that the person responds to CPR, even without a defibrillator. In hypothermia, continue CPR without interruption until the person has been rewarmed.
- For children and babies: where no emergency service or hospital can be reached and CPR has had no effect after the first 20 minutes, you can stop because continuing CPR is unlikely to help any more. This does not apply after drowning or in hypothermia. For adults in those circumstances, protect the person's dignity.
- If the person recovers, stay with them and keep checking the level of consciousness and the breathing until a doctor is present.
- Taking part in a resuscitation is hard. Make sure that rescuers receive support afterwards. Talk about it and seek help if you are struggling.

## Call 112 if

- someone does not respond, even before you know whether they are breathing normally;
- someone is not breathing normally;
- a child or baby does not respond and is breathing with difficulty or not at all. Say clearly that the child does not respond and is not breathing normally, so that the call is given priority;
- you are using an AED: tell the operator.

Call with the speaker on and keep performing CPR in the meantime. If the connection works, the operator can guide you step by step. If it does not, follow the steps on this page.

**If you cannot get through**

- Only leave the casualty alone if there is no other option. Point directly at a bystander to get help and tell them where it is, what happened, how many casualties there are and what condition they are in. Send someone else to fetch an AED.
- If there are two of you: one performs CPR, the other goes to get help and brings an AED if possible.
- If you are alone without a phone: for an adult, get help first and then start CPR; for a child, perform CPR for 1 minute first and then go and get help.
- If you are alone, do not go to fetch an AED unless it is close by and visible. Start CPR.
- See also topic 1 (If you cannot get through: getting help without a phone) and Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call.

## Do not

- Do not hesitate and do not wait. You can do little wrong for someone in cardiac arrest.
- Do not stop out of fear of doing something wrong.
- Never interrupt the compressions for longer than 10 seconds.
- Do not lean on the chest between two compressions.
- Do not put a pillow under the head.
- Do not move the casualty if that takes time.
- Do not fetch the AED yourself if it is not close by and you are alone.
- Do not touch the casualty while the AED is analysing or delivering a shock.
- Do not stop when the ambulance arrives, but only when the healthcare professionals take over.
