## Recognise

Someone is unconscious if they do not respond to stimuli from their surroundings: not to your voice and not to a touch. In an unconscious person the muscles relax. The tongue can then fall back into the throat and block the airway, even if the person is still breathing. That is why unconsciousness is always life-threatening.

Possible causes: poisoning (carbon monoxide, alcohol), low blood sugar, lack of oxygen, a brain haemorrhage, meningitis, a head injury or a seizure.

The level of consciousness can decrease in stages. The IFRC and the ERC use the AVPU scale:

- A: alert, but possibly confused;
- V: responds only to your voice;
- P: responds only to pain, for example when you squeeze the shoulder;
- U: does not respond at all.

If someone responds only to pain or not at all, you must keep the airway clear.

Fainting is a brief form of unconsciousness. Warning signs: turning pale, dizziness, seeing black spots or stars, sweating with cold, clammy skin, and nausea. Brief jerking movements are possible and do not necessarily point to epilepsy.

## What to do

### Checking response and breathing

1. Ensure your own safety and that of the casualty.
2. Gently shake both shoulders and ask loudly whether they are all right. With a baby, tap the sole of the foot.
3. If the person responds, leave them lying as you found them, if they are not in danger. Shout for help and do not stay alone. Have someone stay with the casualty and have someone call 112, or call yourself. Say that the person is partially conscious but responding, give your name and the exact address. Find out what happened and check regularly that they remain conscious.
4. If the person does not respond, shout loudly for help. Have someone call 112 and fetch an AED. If you are alone, call yourself and say that the person is unconscious, give your name and the exact address.
5. Turn the casualty onto their back and open the airway: hand on the forehead, tilt the head back, two fingertips under the point of the chin and lift the chin. With a baby, bring the head into a neutral position.
6. Look, listen and feel for no more than 10 seconds to see whether the person is breathing normally.
7. If they are not breathing normally, start CPR immediately (see topic 2).

### Breathing normally and no suspected spinal injury: recovery position

Place the casualty in the recovery position if they:

- are breathing normally but do not respond;
- have difficulty breathing because of blood, vomit or other body fluids;
- have to be left alone because you have to go and raise the alarm.

Features of the position according to the ERC and the IFRC:

1. The casualty lies on their side.
2. The arms and legs are bent.
3. The head is tilted back, so that the airway stays open. This also prevents the casualty from choking on vomit.
4. You can hold a baby in this position in your arms.
5. Preferably place a pregnant woman on her left side. That way the blood vessels to the womb are not compressed.

The Red Cross shows the technique step by step in its instruction videos and in the First Aid app. Practise it on a course.

Afterwards:

1. Check every minute that breathing remains normal.
2. Stay with the casualty until the emergency services arrive.
3. If breathing stops or becomes irregular, turn the casualty onto their back and start CPR.
4. If you cannot turn the casualty onto their side, keep the airway open by tilting the head and lifting the chin.

### Breathing normally and you suspect a spinal injury

Think of a spinal injury after a road traffic accident, a fall from height or a dive into shallow water.

1. Leave the casualty in the position in which you found them, provided they are breathing spontaneously.
2. If they are lying on their back: keep the airway open without tilting the head back. Only if there is difficulty breathing, push the jaw upwards. Hold the head gently so that it does not move.
3. Watch the mouth constantly. If vomit comes up, turn the casualty onto their side immediately, no matter how, so that the vomit can drain out.
4. If they are lying on their front: watch the mouth constantly and let vomit drain out.
5. Check the level of consciousness and the breathing every minute.
6. The Croix-Rouge de Belgique stresses: the injury must be clearly and immediately identifiable. If you are in doubt whether there is an injury, do place the casualty in the recovery position.

### Fainting

1. If you see someone about to faint, have them lie down or support them if they fall.
2. Lay them flat on the ground. You may raise the legs.
3. Provide fresh air and ask bystanders to keep their distance.
4. Loosen tight clothing.
5. Put a cold compress or a damp flannel on the forehead.
6. When they come round, let them lie a little longer. Then let them gradually sit up and stand up.
7. If they are unconscious for longer than 2 minutes, place them in the recovery position, check the breathing every minute and call 112.
8. Check whether they were injured when they fell.

## If help is a long time coming

- Keep checking the breathing regularly, even after a long time. Keep the airway open.
- Try to find out the cause, such as diabetes, poisoning or a head injury. Look for open packaging of medicines or products nearby and give it to the emergency services (see topic 12).
- Write down what you see and when: how long the person has been unconscious, whether they respond to voice or pain, and every change.
- If you suspect a spinal injury, help is still far away, and there are at least two rescuers: you may then turn the casualty onto their side while one rescuer keeps the head in line with the back. If help is coming soon, only use head tilt or jaw lift.
- The Croix-Rouge de Belgique explains that with a clear injury to the head, neck or trunk, you can leave the casualty on their back if they are clearly breathing and the emergency services will be on the scene soon. If it takes a long time, an open airway through the recovery position takes priority.
- Keep the casualty warm. Put something between the body and the ground, cover them with dry layers and protect them from the wind.
- Do not give an unconscious or drowsy person anything to eat or drink.
- If the person wakes up, speak calmly to them, explain what has happened and keep monitoring them.

## Call 112 if

- someone is unconscious, even if they are breathing normally;
- someone has a reduced level of consciousness;
- someone remains unconscious for longer than 2 minutes after fainting;
- the fainting comes on suddenly, without any warning signs.

Pass on to the emergency services: the circumstances in which the person became unconscious, known health problems and the medication they take.

Consult a doctor for fainting without a clear cause, for repeated fainting, if heart problems are suspected and for fainting after a fall or a blow to the head.

## Do not

- Do not place anyone in the recovery position who is not breathing normally. That person must lie on their back for CPR.
- Do not tilt the head back if you suspect a spinal injury.
- Do not move a casualty with a suspected spinal injury unless necessary.
- Do not leave an unconscious person alone, unless you have to go and raise the alarm. In that case, first place them in the recovery position.
- Do not give anything to eat or drink.
- Do not put a pillow under the head of someone you are giving CPR to.
