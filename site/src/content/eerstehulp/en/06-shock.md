## Recognise

Shock means that the circulation is failing: organs and tissues do not get enough blood and therefore not enough oxygen. Shock is always caused by something else. The IFRC lists these causes:

- severe bleeding, external or internal (the most common cause);
- very severe burns, where fluid leaks from the blood vessels into the burn;
- a heart attack, where the heart does not pump enough blood around the body;
- a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis);
- an infection such as blood poisoning (sepsis).

Signs of shock. They do not all have to be present at the same time:

- drowsiness or confusion, restlessness or anxiety;
- feeling unwell, weakness, tiredness;
- dizziness on standing up; the faintness does not go away when the person lies down;
- pale or grey skin, bluish lips, ears and fingers;
- sweating, shivering, cold and clammy skin, cold hands and feet;
- nausea and retching;
- shallow, rapid breathing and a rapid heart rate;
- eventually unconsciousness.

You can recognise internal bleeding in the abdomen by a blow or knock to the abdomen, bruises, a hard, tense abdomen, severe pain sometimes with the legs drawn up, and signs of shock. No blood can be seen on the outside. Unconsciousness may be the only sign.

## What to do

1. Make sure the scene is safe. Put on disposable gloves if you have them.
2. Help the casualty to lie down on their back.
3. Assess the condition: find out what is wrong, check for a response and, if necessary, open the airway and check the breathing.
4. Call 112 immediately or have someone call. If you cannot get through, see Call 112 if.
5. Treat the cause if you know it:
   - Severe external bleeding: press on the wound with your hands for 10 minutes, keep the limb raised if possible, then apply a pressure bandage or press on a clean cloth, and keep pressing until help arrives (see topic 3).
   - Severe allergic reaction: help the person use their own adrenaline auto-injector (see topic 15).
   - Large burn: cool and cover it, and prevent the burn from touching the ground (see topic 7).
   - Suspected heart attack: let the person rest (see topic 16).
6. Choose the position according to the situation:
   - Standard: flat on the back.
   - If the person has difficulty breathing or does not feel well on their back: help them into the position that is most comfortable for them, for example sitting and leaning back or leaning forward.
   - With a known heart attack causing shock: on the back with the upper body slightly raised.
   - With a severe allergic reaction with breathing difficulty: sitting or half-sitting.
   - If the person feels better with the legs raised and the legs are not injured: you may raise the legs 30 to 60 degrees.
7. Keep the casualty warm. Cover them with a blanket or clothing. Put something between the body and cold ground.
8. Do not let the casualty exert themselves. Keep them as calm as possible, for example by talking to them.
9. Check the level of consciousness and the breathing every minute.
10. Do not give anything to eat or drink. Do not give painkillers if internal bleeding is suspected, even if the casualty asks for them.
11. If the casualty becomes unconscious and is not breathing normally, start CPR (see topic 2).
12. After giving first aid, take off your gloves and wash or disinfect your hands.

### Children and older people

The signs of shock are the same. In a child with dark skin, look at the palms of the hands and the soles of the feet to see paleness or blue discolouration. In children and older people, even a small burn can be serious.

## If help is a long time coming

Shock is life-threatening and always needs medical care. What you can do while you wait:

- Keep treating the cause. With bleeding: keep pressing. A tourniquet stays in place (see topic 3).
- Prevent heat loss. Seriously injured people cool down quickly and that increases the risk of death. Remove wet clothing, put cardboard, a coat or a blanket between the casualty and the ground, wrap them in dry layers or a foil blanket and protect them from the wind with your body or with objects.
- Keep the casualty lying down and calm. Do not let them stand up or walk around.
- Talk to the casualty, hold their hand and make eye contact. If you have to leave to help another casualty, say so and ask someone to stay.
- Check the level of consciousness and the breathing every minute. Write down any changes.
- If the condition changes and the phone works, report this to 112 again. If someone has to go for help, give that person precise information to take with them about the location and the condition.

## Call 112 if

- you see signs of shock: always and immediately;
- you suspect internal bleeding;
- the casualty has a reduced level of consciousness or is unconscious;
- there is severe bleeding, a large burn, a suspected heart attack or a severe allergic reaction.

Explain clearly to the emergency service what the condition is, which signs of shock you see and what the cause might be.

**If you cannot get through**

- Only leave the casualty alone if there is no other option. Point directly at a bystander to get help and tell them where it is, what happened, how many casualties there are and what condition they are in.
- See also topic 1 (If you cannot get through: getting help without a phone) and Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call.

## Do not

- Do not let the casualty exert themselves or stand up.
- Do not lay someone who is having difficulty breathing flat if that makes their breathing even harder.
- Do not raise the legs if they are injured or if the casualty feels worse because of it.
- Do not give anything to eat or drink.
- Do not give painkillers if internal bleeding is suspected.
- Do not let the casualty get cold.
- Do not leave the casualty alone.
