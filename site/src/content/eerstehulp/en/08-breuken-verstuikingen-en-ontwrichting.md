## Recognise

You do not need to know whether it is a fracture, a sprain or a dislocation. Only an X-ray gives certainty. If you are in doubt, treat the injury as a fracture.

Fracture:

- the bone is in an abnormal position, or the limb is shorter, twisted or bent;
- you hear or feel the bones grating when moved, or there was a crack or snap at the time of the accident;
- pain, swelling and bruising at the site of the fracture;
- the limb is difficult to use;
- open fracture: a bleeding wound, sometimes with visible bone fragments.

A fracture of a large bone, such as the thigh bone, can itself bleed heavily. That can be life-threatening.

Sprain:

- pain and a swollen joint;
- sometimes a bruise at the level of the joint;
- usually the casualty can still bear weight, but cannot roll through the foot. If they cannot bear weight, it may be a fracture.

Dislocation: the two bone ends of a joint have shifted. Ligaments and the joint capsule are stretched or torn, and nerves or blood vessels may be damaged.

- pain in the joint and the limb does not move normally;
- swelling and bruising;
- the joint is in an abnormal position;
- with an open dislocation: a bleeding wound, sometimes with visible bone fragments.

A dislocation can occur together with a fracture.

Signs of compartment syndrome, a dangerous build-up of pressure in a muscle: sudden muscle pain, recurring pain, sensory disturbances, hard muscles or loss of strength.

## What to do

### Closed fracture

1. Have the casualty move the limb as little as possible. Support it in the position in which you find it.
2. Let the casualty adopt a comfortable position. Raise the limb higher than the rest of the body if the casualty can tolerate it. That limits the swelling.
3. Remove rings and bracelets with a fracture of the arm or hand.
4. Cool the injury for no more than 20 minutes, with ice cubes in a bag of water or a cold pack. If you have no ice, use cold water. Wrap the ice in a thin cloth and never put it directly on the skin.
5. Stop cooling if it bothers the casualty. If the pain comes back, you may cool again as soon as the skin is back to a normal temperature.
6. Check whether there is bleeding, external or internal, and watch for signs of shock, especially with a fracture of the thigh bone (see topic 6).
7. Call 112 or have someone call for a fracture of the leg, ankle or foot. If you cannot get through, see Call 112 if. With a closed fracture of the arm, wrist or hand, the casualty may go to hospital on their own.
8. Write down the time of the injury. That helps the doctor.

### Open fracture

1. Press gently on the wound to stop the bleeding, without moving the limb.
2. Moisten a sterile dressing with water or with a water-based, non-staining antiseptic and cover the fracture with it.
3. Do not put ice on an open fracture.
4. Keep the limb still and treat for shock.
5. Call 112 or have someone call.

### Sprain

1. Compare the limb with the other side. Do not test for yourself how far the joint can move.
2. Have the casualty move the limb as little as possible. They should hold an arm or hand still against the chest. They must not put weight on a painful leg.
3. Remove rings and other jewellery with a sprain of the elbow or wrist.
4. With a sprain of the lower body, leave the shoe on.
5. Raise the limb higher than the body if that is comfortable.
6. Cool for no more than 20 minutes at a time, as for a fracture. Cooling for longer can damage the skin.
7. A compression bandage may be used if it makes the casualty feel better. Immobilising is also allowed, if that is comfortable.

### Dislocation

1. Never try to put the joint back yourself. Leave that to a doctor.
2. Remove rings and jewellery with a dislocation of the shoulder, elbow, wrist or finger.
3. Let the casualty adopt the most comfortable position possible. Raise the limb if possible.
4. Closed dislocation: cool for no more than 20 minutes, as for a fracture.
5. Open dislocation: press on the wound without moving the limb and cover with a moist sterile dressing. Do not put ice on it.

### Cramp

With muscle cramp, do not cool, but warm the muscle.

## If help is a long time coming

- If the casualty has to be moved and the transport will be bumpy or jolting, for example over a long distance across rough terrain, protect the limb, especially a leg, by splinting it. Splint in such a way that pain is kept to a minimum, no additional injury occurs and transport can be safe and quick.
- Splint in the position in which you found the limb. Stabilise it so that it moves as little as possible.
- Cool repeatedly if the pain comes back, each time for no more than 20 minutes and only when the skin is back to its normal warmth.
- With a fracture of a large bone, monitor the signs of shock and keep the casualty lying down and warm.
- Watch for sensory disturbances, such as tingling, pins and needles or a foot that has gone to sleep, and for signs of compartment syndrome. These are reasons to consult a doctor.
- Never straighten a joint or limb that is in an abnormal position.
- Dislocations and fractures must always be treated by a healthcare professional. Seek that care as soon as possible.

## Call 112 if

- it is a fracture of the leg, ankle or foot;
- it is an open fracture or open dislocation;
- the casualty cannot move about;
- the casualty has a lot of pain or swelling, or the limb is in an abnormal position;
- you are in doubt.

See a doctor if the swelling is very pronounced, the limb is in an abnormal position or is abnormally mobile or immobile, there are sensory disturbances (tingling, pins and needles, a foot that has gone to sleep), the pain does not go away after 20 minutes of cooling or there are signs of compartment syndrome.

**If you cannot get through**

- Only leave the casualty alone if there is no other option. Point directly at a bystander to get help and tell them where it is, what happened, how many casualties there are and what condition they are in.
- See also topic 1 (If you cannot get through: getting help without a phone) and Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call.

## Do not

- Never straighten a joint or limb that is in an abnormal position yourself.
- Do not try to put a dislocation back.
- Do not test the mobility of a joint yourself.
- Do not let the casualty put weight on a painful leg.
- Do not put ice on an open fracture or open dislocation.
- Never put ice directly on the skin.
- Do not cool for longer than 20 minutes at a time.
- With a sprain of the lower body, do not take the shoe off.
