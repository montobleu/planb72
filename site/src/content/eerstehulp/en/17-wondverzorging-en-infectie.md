## Recognise

Small wounds, such as a cut, a graze or a small puncture wound, are usually not urgent. They can, however, become infected, with bacteria or with tetanus.

A wound is more serious and needs a doctor if:

- the bleeding does not stop;
- the wound cannot be cleaned properly;
- the wound is gaping or deep;
- bones, muscles or subcutaneous tissue are visible;
- the face, eyes or genitals are injured;
- there is something in the wound, such as soil, wood or metal;
- it is a laceration, puncture wound, gunshot wound or bite wound from an animal or a human;
- a graze is larger than half the palm of the casualty's hand;
- the casualty has diabetes or reduced resistance to infection;
- the casualty is not vaccinated, or not sufficiently vaccinated, against tetanus, or you are in doubt.

Signs of wound infection:

- the skin around the wound becomes red, purple or darker, warm and painful;
- swelling, or fluid or pus coming out of the wound;
- fever.

Signs of blood poisoning (sepsis): confusion or disorientation, breathing difficulties, a rapid heart rate, fever or shivering, extreme pain, clammy or sweaty skin.

Tetanus (lockjaw) is a life-threatening infection caused by a bacterium that lives in soil and manure. It enters through wounds, especially deep and dirty ones. High-risk wounds are:

- puncture wounds and deep splinters or thorns;
- bite wounds from animals or humans;
- wounds after stepping on a rusty nail;
- wounds contaminated with soil or street dirt;
- burns;
- wounds that have not been thoroughly cleaned and disinfected after 6 hours.

The first signs of tetanus are stiff and cramped jaw muscles, headache and fever. Later come muscle spasms, often close to the wound, which spread. If the breathing muscles are affected, it can be fatal.

## What to do

### Small wound

1. Remove the cause if possible. If there is an object in the wound, leave it in place (see topic 3).
2. Avoid contact with blood: wash or disinfect your hands and put on disposable gloves. Do not touch the wound with bare fingers.
3. Remove jewellery and clothing around the injured area.
4. Stop the bleeding by pressing on the wound. If it is bleeding heavily, follow topic 3.
5. Check the wound for dirt and foreign objects as soon as the bleeding has stopped.
6. Rinse the wound thoroughly with running tap water, preferably lukewarm. Aim the stream directly at the wound.
7. If necessary, rub lightly with a sterile dressing to remove stubborn dirt.
8. Little or no water: spray a water-based, non-staining antiseptic onto the wound and onto a dressing and dab the dirt away. Take a new dressing regularly. As a last step, disinfect once more with a clean dressing.
9. Pat the skin around the wound dry with a clean towel. Do not touch the wound again after that.
10. Cover the wound with a sterile dressing (if necessary with hydrogel), a paraffin gauze dressing, a wound plaster or a second-skin dressing. A covered wound heals faster in a moist wound environment, provided it has first been cleaned properly.
11. Take off the gloves and disinfect your hands.
12. Ask whether the casualty is vaccinated against tetanus.

### Puncture wound

1. Always have a puncture wound checked by a doctor.
2. You may try to remove a small splinter yourself. Leave a larger object, such as a nail or a knife, in place. Alert the emergency services as quickly as possible.
3. Control the bleeding, rinse and cover as for a small wound.

### Bite wound

1. Have a dog taken away by its owner or a vet. The animal may see the treatment as a threat.
2. Skin not broken: cool the area with ice cubes in a bag of water or a cold pack.
3. Skin broken: first control the bleeding. With severe bleeding, apply a pressure bandage. Rinse the wound thoroughly with water as soon as possible. If you have little water, disinfect.
4. Consult a doctor as soon as possible for every bite wound. Check whether the casualty is vaccinated against tetanus and rabies.

### Tetanus after a wound

1. Clean the wound thoroughly as soon as possible with lukewarm tap water and soap, disinfect it and cover it with a sterile dressing. Disinfecting helps only to a limited extent against the tetanus bacterium.
2. With a high-risk wound: contact your GP. They will check whether a new vaccination is needed.
3. With a high-risk wound, a booster vaccination may be needed if the last vaccination was more than 5 years ago.
4. Anyone who is not vaccinated, or not fully vaccinated, also receives tetanus antibodies for a high-risk wound. These work immediately, whereas the vaccine needs time.
5. Adults routinely receive a booster vaccination every 10 years. In Flanders it is fully reimbursed. Anyone who was not fully vaccinated as a child can complete the schedule or start again.

## If help is a long time coming

- No tap with drinking water: make a small hole in a clean, unused water bottle and use it to squirt a gently pressured stream of water onto the wound. Use bottled water or clean running water. A physiological saline solution is even better if you have one.
- No clean water: clean a simple skin wound with an antiseptic.
- Also carefully clean the skin around the wound with soap and clean water.
- Check the dressing every day. If it is still clean, leave it in place. If there is a little blood or clear fluid on it, remove it, clean the wound again and apply a new dressing. Look at the wound at least every 24 hours.
- Cover a clean, open wound with a waterproof dressing to prevent infection. Stay out of floodwater with an open wound.
- After a disaster, the CDC recommends leaving dirty wounds, bite wounds and puncture wounds open, because a wound that has not been cleaned properly can trap bacteria. The Belgian guideline says that you cover a properly cleaned wound, and that a wound that cannot be cleaned properly must be seen by a doctor. Have such a wound seen by a doctor as soon as possible.
- Never cover an infected wound, and seek medical help.
- Never use wound closure strips yourself. Do not remove stitches or staples yourself.
- Everyone with a wound must check whether their tetanus vaccination is up to date. With a puncture wound or a wound contaminated with manure, soil or saliva, a healthcare professional decides on the basis of the vaccination record whether a booster vaccination is needed. Your vaccinations are recorded in your health booklet and in your medical record.
- Small wounds and grazes usually heal within a few days.

## Call 112 if

- the bleeding is severe or cannot be controlled (see topic 3);
- there is a large object in the wound: alert the emergency services as quickly as possible.

Seek medical care immediately with signs of blood poisoning: confusion, breathing difficulties, a rapid heart rate, fever or shivering, extreme pain or clammy skin.

See a doctor as soon as possible for a wound that looks infected, for every puncture wound and bite wound, for a wound with dirt or an object in it, for a gaping or deep wound, and when in doubt about tetanus protection.

**If you cannot get through**

- Only leave the casualty alone if there is no other option. Point directly at a bystander to get help and tell them where it is, what happened, how many casualties there are and what condition they are in.
- With severe bleeding: keep pressing yourself and send someone else for help (see topic 3).
- See also topic 1 (If you cannot get through: getting help without a phone) and Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call.

## Do not

- Do not remove a larger object from the wound.
- Do not come into contact with blood or body fluids with bare hands.
- Do not touch the wound again after it has been cleaned and covered.
- Do not use wound closure strips yourself.
- Do not remove stitches or staples yourself. Leave that to a nurse or doctor.
- Do not rinse a serious head wound (see topic 9).
- Do not cover an infected wound.
- Do not go into floodwater with an open wound.
