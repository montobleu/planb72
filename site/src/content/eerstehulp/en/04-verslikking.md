## Recognise

Choking usually happens while eating. Ask: "Are you choking?"

Mild choking: the airway is partially blocked.

- The person can still speak, cough, cry and breathe.
- The coughing may be weak or forceful.
- When breathing in, you may hear a high-pitched wheezing sound.

Severe choking: the airway is (almost) completely blocked.

- The person cannot speak, cough, cry or breathe.
- They make coughing movements without sound.
- They clutch at their throat with one or two hands and panic.
- The face may turn red or blue. The lips, ears, fingers and toes turn bluish.
- Consciousness may gradually be lost.

At greater risk are: young children, older people, people with a reduced level of consciousness or with a condition that disturbs swallowing and coughing (such as after a stroke, or with Parkinson's disease, cerebral palsy or dementia), people under the influence of alcohol or drugs and people with poor teeth.

## What to do

### Mild choking (all ages)

1. Encourage the person to cough. With a baby who is coughing: wait and see whether the baby coughs it up by themselves.
2. Do not intervene yet. Someone who is still coughing is often better at clearing the airway themselves.
3. Carefully remove a clearly visible object from the mouth.
4. Stay with the person until they are breathing normally again. Mild choking can turn into severe choking.

### Severe choking in an adult or child

1. Call 112 or have someone call, or do so as soon as the first round does not help. If you cannot get through, have someone else get help (see Call 112 if).
2. Stand to the side and slightly behind the casualty. Have them lean forward. Support the chest with one hand.
3. Give up to 5 sharp back blows with the heel of your hand between the shoulder blades.
4. If the back blows do not help, give up to 5 abdominal thrusts. Stand behind the casualty and put your arms around the upper part of the abdomen. Have them lean forward. Place a fist on the upper part of the abdomen, between the navel and the ribs. Grasp your fist with your other hand and pull sharply inwards and upwards.
5. Alternate 5 back blows with 5 abdominal thrusts until the object is dislodged or until the casualty becomes unconscious.

### Severe choking in a baby (younger than 1 year)

1. Lay the baby along your forearm, face down with the head slightly lower. Support the lower jaw with your hand.
2. Give up to 5 back blows with the heel of your hand between the shoulder blades.
3. If they do not help, turn the baby onto their back. Place both hands around the chest with the thumbs on the breastbone. Give up to 5 chest thrusts: press the breastbone in with your thumbs to at least one third of the depth of the chest.
4. Call 112 or have someone call if the chest thrusts do not help. If you cannot get through, have someone else get help and stay with the baby yourself.
5. Alternate 5 back blows with 5 chest thrusts until the object is dislodged or until the baby no longer responds.

With a baby, give chest thrusts, not abdominal thrusts.

### Severe choking in a pregnant woman

1. Give 5 back blows as above.
2. If they do not help, give 5 chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts. Stand behind her. If she is standing, brace yourself firmly against her. If she is sitting, brace yourself firmly against the chair.
3. Put your arms under her armpits. Have her lean forward.
4. Place a fist on the lower half of the breastbone. Grasp that fist with your other hand and pull quickly and sharply towards you.
5. Alternate until the object is dislodged.

### Severe choking in a wheelchair user

1. Put the brake on the wheelchair.
2. Give 5 back blows as above.
3. If they do not help, give 5 abdominal thrusts. If you can stand behind the wheelchair and put your arms around the abdomen, brace yourself firmly against the wheelchair, have the casualty lean forward and pull your fist on the upper part of the abdomen sharply towards you and upwards.
4. If that does not work, kneel in front of the casualty. Place a fist on the upper part of the abdomen and with your other hand push quickly away from you and upwards.

### If the casualty becomes unconscious

1. Carefully lower the casualty to the ground.
2. Call 112 or have someone call if this has not yet been done. Have someone fetch an AED if there is one nearby. If you cannot get through, see Call 112 if and topic 2.
3. Start CPR immediately, with chest compressions and rescue breaths (see topic 2). The compressions increase the pressure in the airway and can dislodge the object. Continue, even if the object does not come loose, until the emergency services arrive.
4. With a baby: give rescue breaths and chest compressions as in CPR for a baby.
5. If you see the object in the mouth, carefully remove it.

## If help is a long time coming

- Keep alternating: 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts, or for a baby 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts. Only stop when the object is dislodged or when the person becomes unconscious.
- If the person becomes unconscious, perform CPR without stopping until help arrives. Even if the object does not come loose, continue CPR.
- If the object is dislodged and the person is breathing normally again, stay with them and monitor their breathing. After abdominal thrusts there may be internal injuries, and a small piece of the object may be in the lungs and cause symptoms later.
- Have the person examined by a doctor as soon as possible, even if the problem seems solved. This checks that the object or the manoeuvres did not cause any injury.
- Over the following hours and days, watch for persistent coughing, difficulty swallowing or the feeling that something is still in the throat. These are reasons to see a doctor after all.

## Call 112 if

- the choking is severe: the person cannot speak, cough or breathe;
- the back blows and abdominal or chest thrusts do not help;
- the casualty loses consciousness.

Also see a doctor if the casualty keeps coughing, has difficulty swallowing, has the feeling that something is in the throat, or has received abdominal thrusts. Seek medical advice or call 112, even if the object has come out.

**If you cannot get through**

- Only leave the casualty alone if there is no other option. Point directly at a bystander to get help and tell them where it is, what happened, how many casualties there are and what condition they are in.
- If a child is choking severely, stay with the child and do not leave to get help yourself.
- See also topic 1 (If you cannot get through: getting help without a phone) and Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call.

## Do not

- Do not intervene in mild choking as long as the person is coughing effectively.
- Do not do a blind finger sweep in the mouth. You may push the object deeper. Only remove what you can clearly see.
- Do not give a baby abdominal thrusts, give chest thrusts.
- Do not give a pregnant woman abdominal thrusts, give chest thrusts.
- Do not leave the casualty alone until they are breathing normally again.
- Do not hold back out of fear of doing something wrong. Doing nothing can end badly.

Prevention in children: cut round food such as grapes and tomatoes in half. Keep an eye on a baby when they are eating or can put something in their mouth.
