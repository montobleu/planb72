## Recognise

Rode Kruis-Vlaanderen teaches first aid in four steps. You always go through them in the same order and start again as soon as the situation changes:

1. Make sure the scene is safe.
2. Assess the condition of the casualty.
3. Get specialised help.
4. Give further first aid.

A situation is life-threatening if, at the first assessment, you answer one of these four questions with "no" or "yes, severely". The IFRC calls this the ABC check:

- Does the person respond?
- Is the airway open and clear?
- Is the person breathing normally?
- Is there an injury that is bleeding heavily?

If someone can talk or cry, you may assume that the airway is open and that breathing is adequate.

Watch for these signs of cardiac arrest: the person does not respond and is not breathing normally. At first it may look as if the person is still breathing: gasping, groaning, sighing, gurgling or snoring. That is not normal breathing but "agonal breathing". Brief jerking movements at the start are possible and are often mistaken for an epileptic seizure. As soon as the movements stop, check the breathing straight away.

For children and babies, the ERC uses a quick look at three things. If you see any of these signs, call for help immediately:

- Behaviour: does the child respond to your voice or a touch? Is it drowsy, confused, floppy or stiff? Is it crying inconsolably?
- Breathing: is it breathing with difficulty, too fast, too slowly or irregularly? Is it making noises? Is it unable to talk or cry loudly?
- Skin colour: is the skin mottled, blue, grey or abnormally pale? On dark skin, look at the palms of the hands and the soles of the feet.

## What to do

1. First look around. Is there danger to yourself, the casualty or bystanders? Think of traffic, spilled chemicals, power lines or aggressive people.
2. Remove the danger if you can do so safely, or find a safer place nearby. If that is not possible, do not approach the casualty. Alert the fire service or the police, for example in case of fire, gas, chemical, radiological or biological contamination, or violence.
3. Only move the casualty if they are in danger.
4. If possible, approach from the side the casualty is facing. That way they are not startled and do not move unnecessarily.
5. Check for a response. Gently shake both shoulders and ask loudly: "Are you all right?" With a baby, tap the sole of the foot or pick the baby up.
6. If the casualty responds, leave them in the position in which you found them, as long as they are not in danger. Speak calmly. Ask what happened, what they feel, whether they have any serious allergies and what illnesses they have. Ask for permission before you examine them.
7. If the casualty does not respond, shout loudly for help. Point to someone to help you. Turn the casualty onto their back.
8. Open the airway. Kneel beside the chest. Place one hand on the forehead and gently tilt the head back. Place two fingertips of your other hand under the point of the chin. Do not press into the soft part under the chin. Lift the chin and keep it lifted. With a baby, bring the head into a neutral position, no further back. If you suspect a spinal injury, do not tilt the head back (see topic 9).
9. Check the breathing. Keep the airway open. Put your ear and cheek close to the mouth and nose. Look to see whether the chest rises and falls, listen for breath sounds and feel whether air is coming out. Do not do this for longer than 10 seconds. If you are in doubt whether breathing is normal, act as if it is not normal.
10. Call 112, or have someone call and ask that person to let you know afterwards that the call has been made. If you are alone, put your phone on speaker so that your hands stay free. The Red Cross sums up the information in three questions: where, what and who. For an unconscious person, give their condition, your name and the exact address. Answer the operator's questions calmly. The operator will help you with the next steps.
11. If the casualty is not breathing normally: start CPR immediately and have someone fetch an AED (see topic 2).
12. If the casualty is breathing normally but does not respond: place them in the recovery position, unless you suspect a spinal injury. Check every minute that breathing remains normal (see topic 5).
13. Give further first aid in this order: keep the airway clear, control severe bleeding (topic 3), cool burns (topic 7), treat large wounds and cover them, treat injuries to bones, muscles and joints (topic 8), and then treat the less serious injuries.
14. Stay with the casualty. Regularly recheck the response and the breathing, because the condition can suddenly get worse. Then start again at step 1 of the four steps.
15. Do not give a conscious casualty anything to eat or drink. Explain what has happened and what is going to happen.
16. If there are several casualties: call 112 first to report how serious it is and call again as soon as you know more. Help those with breathing or circulation problems first.
17. After giving first aid, wash or disinfect your hands.

The ERC recommends briefly going through the ABCDE sequence with every casualty:

- A (airway): is the airway open? After a fall from height or a serious accident: think of a neck injury.
- B (breathing): is the person breathing normally? Do you hear wheezing? Is the person choking? Are the lips or nails blue? Are the lips, tongue or throat swollen?
- C (circulation): is there pain in the chest, neck or arm? Are there signs of shock: a very fast or very slow heart rate, pale, cool or clammy skin, dizziness or confusion?
- D (disability, level of consciousness): is the person alert, do they respond to your voice, only to pain, or not at all?
- E (exposure): check the whole body for injuries. Stop severe bleeding. Prevent hypothermia by removing wet clothing and using blankets. In case of overheating: start active cooling.

## If help is a long time coming

The IFRC describes special circumstances in which medical help can be hours or days away: remote places and disasters in which roads, telephones or hospitals temporarily do not work. For these, the IFRC gives the following points of attention:

- First do what saves life. Breathing and circulation come first, then the rest. Know why this order applies, so that you also follow it under pressure.
- A lack of equipment is no reason not to help. Use what is available, for example a shirt to press on a bleeding wound.
- Pay extra attention to your own safety in an unfamiliar or unstable environment, for example during aftershocks after an earthquake.
- After a serious accident, transport becomes important. Make sure first aid can continue during transport.
- Sometimes you can continue helping under the guidance of a doctor or nurse by telephone or radio.
- If you have to send someone for help on foot or by car, give that person precise information to take with them: where, how many casualties and in what condition. That way the emergency service can send the right help.
- In remote areas it can be useful to drive towards the nearest hospital and meet the ambulance on the way.
- Keep caring for the person until professional help takes over or until the person and those around them can cope on their own. Keep monitoring the level of consciousness and the breathing and write down any changes.
- Prevent infection and further injury.

Keep the casualty warm, because seriously injured people cool down quickly and that increases the risk of death. FEMA gives these steps:

- Remove wet clothing.
- Put something between the casualty and the ground, such as cardboard, a coat or a blanket.
- Wrap the casualty in dry layers: a coat, a blanket or a foil blanket.
- Protect them from the wind with your body or with objects nearby.

Stay with the casualty. Hold their hand and make eye contact. If you have to leave to help another casualty, say so and ask someone else to stay.

If 112 cannot be reached with a normal call: 112 is free throughout Europe, and the 112 BE app lets you call the fire service, the ambulance or the police with one tap on the right icon. Try again and keep giving first aid in the meantime.

## Call 112 if

- the casualty does not respond, even if they are breathing normally;
- the casualty is not breathing normally or you are in doubt about the breathing;
- there is severe bleeding;
- you see signs of shock;
- there is a danger you cannot remove yourself, such as fire, gas or chemicals;
- you are in doubt.

112 is the number for the ambulance and the fire service. For urgent police assistance it is 101. Do not call 112 for ordinary storm damage.

## Do not

- Do not approach the casualty if the scene is not safe.
- Do not move the casualty unless they are in danger.
- Do not wait to help because nobody else is doing anything. Everyone staying quiet does not mean that nothing is wrong.
- Do not take the pulse as the only test for cardiac arrest. It is unreliable.
- Do not tilt the head back if you suspect a spinal injury.
- Do not check the breathing for longer than 10 seconds.
- Do not give anything to eat or drink.
- Do not film the casualty and do not block the way for the emergency services.
