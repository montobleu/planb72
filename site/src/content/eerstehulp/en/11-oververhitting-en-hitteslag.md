## Recognise

Overheating occurs after long exposure to high temperatures or after heavy exertion in the heat. The IFRC distinguishes two levels. Heatstroke is life-threatening.

Mild to moderate overheating (heat exhaustion):

- a normal or slightly raised temperature;
- cool, pale, clammy skin;
- heavy sweating and thirst;
- headache and muscle cramps;
- a rapid, weak pulse;
- dizziness or fainting.

Heatstroke:

- the casualty is in a hot environment, has just come from one or has just made a heavy effort;
- first heavy sweating, then no more sweating;
- red, dry, hot skin;
- a high body temperature: the Belgian guideline gives 39 °C as the threshold for calling 112, the IFRC refers to more than 40 °C in heatstroke;
- headache, seeing black spots, dizziness, fainting;
- nausea and vomiting, muscle or abdominal cramps, pain all over the body;
- confusion, drowsiness or strange behaviour;
- seizures or unconsciousness.

Heat cramps: after heavy exertion in the heat, the muscles cramp, usually in the calf, arm, abdomen or back, because of loss of salt and potassium through sweat. The fingers may swell. Always watch for signs of heatstroke as well.

Dehydration can occur together with overheating: little or no sweat, a dry mouth and tongue, dark urine, headache, dizziness and tiredness. In babies and young children: crying without tears, sunken eyes, being unusually tired or sleepy, and a dry nappy for 3 hours or longer.

## What to do

### Heatstroke

1. Have the casualty stop all exertion and let them rest. Keep them as calm as possible.
2. Take them to a cool place. Remove excess clothing.
3. Call 112 or have someone call if there are signs of heatstroke. If you cannot get through, see Call 112 if.
4. Cool them down immediately. The more serious the symptoms, the more vigorously you may cool. Choose the best method you have:
   - Best choice: immerse the casualty up to the neck in a bath of lukewarm to cold water. The IFRC gives water of 1 to 26 °C, for about 15 minutes or until the temperature drops below 39 °C. The Belgian guideline says: until the condition improves, for no more than 15 minutes.
   - If immersion is not possible, for example because there is no cold water or because the casualty is confused and refuses: put a cool, wet sheet over the casualty or wet them with cold water.
   - Put ice cubes in a bag of water, cold packs or wet, cold cloths on places with large blood vessels: in the groin, under the armpits and on the neck.
   - Sprinkle or douse the casualty with cold water. A plant spray bottle can help.
   - Point a fan at the casualty. Fanning increases the cooling.
   - Immerse the hands and feet in cold water.
5. Measure the temperature if possible and monitor the level of consciousness. Try to get the temperature below 39 °C.
6. Only give something cool to drink if the symptoms are caused by heavy exertion and the casualty is fully conscious: a sports drink, water or cold tea.
7. Do not give any fever-reducing medicine.
8. If the casualty becomes unconscious and is breathing normally, place them in the recovery position. If they are not breathing normally, start CPR.

### Mild to moderate overheating

1. Have the person stop the exertion and rest in a cool place.
2. Cool them down: hands and feet in cold water, cold packs on the neck and groin, a cold shower or fanning.
3. Give water to drink. A sports drink or cold tea can also help.
4. Monitor the temperature and the level of consciousness. If the condition does not improve or gets worse, seek medical help.

### Heat cramps

1. Stretch the cramped muscle carefully and very gradually.
2. Watch for signs of heatstroke.
3. Do not apply cold to a cramp. Rather, warm the muscle.

### Children, older people and vulnerable people

- Never leave a child, an adult or an animal behind in a parked car in the sun.
- Do not leave a casualty of overheating alone in a hot environment.
- In hot weather, regularly check on older people and babies: are they staying cool and drinking enough?
- A baby who is breastfed should continue to be breastfed, more often than usual.

## If help is a long time coming

- Keep cooling with every means you have: water, wet cloths, shade, fanning. The ERC says: start active cooling with everything available, such as removing clothing, wet cloths, fans and cool water.
- Make sure cooling materials are ready in places where the risk of overheating is high, so that there is no delay.
- Monitor the temperature and the level of consciousness. Try to bring the temperature below 39 °C and keep it there.
- After cooling, keep the person cool and let them rest. Most people with mild to moderate overheating recover fully.
- Dehydration: let someone who is fully conscious drink enough. With mild dehydration, water is sufficient. With more serious dehydration, the IFRC recommends an oral rehydration solution. If you do not have one, give apple juice, coconut water or water.
- Seek medical advice for dehydration if the condition does not improve quickly, with a baby, child or older person, if someone is losing more fluid than they can drink, if someone passes little or no urine and the urine is dark, or with fever or signs of overheating.
- When travelling in remote areas with little water, take enough to drink. Avoid heavy exertion at the hottest time of the day.

## Call 112 if

- the casualty has a disturbed level of consciousness or becomes unconscious;
- the casualty behaves abnormally or is confused;
- the casualty has a seizure;
- the casualty is no longer sweating;
- the casualty cannot drink without vomiting;
- the body temperature is higher than 39 °C;
- the casualty has difficulty breathing.

Consult a doctor if heat cramps persist.

**If you cannot get through**

- Only leave the casualty alone if there is no other option, and certainly not in a hot environment. Point directly at a bystander to get help and tell them where it is, what happened, how many casualties there are and what condition they are in.
- Have the other bystanders help with cooling and with making shade.
- Keep cooling in the meantime with everything you have (see If help is a long time coming).
- See also topic 1 (If you cannot get through: getting help without a phone) and Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call.

## Do not

- Do not let the casualty make any further effort.
- Do not give any fever-reducing medicine.
- Do not give anything to drink to someone who is not fully conscious.
- Do not leave the casualty alone in a hot environment or in a car in the sun.
- Do not give alcohol or drugs.
- Do not apply cold to a muscle cramp.
