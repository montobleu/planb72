## Recognise

### When to suspect a spinal injury

After a road traffic accident (as a driver, passenger, cyclist or pedestrian), a fall from height such as from stairs or a ladder, a head-first dive into shallow water, an explosion or another serious accident. In people older than 65 years or with a bone disease, even a minor fall or a slip can cause a spinal injury.

Possible signs:

- sensory disturbances or paralysis, including in the trunk or arms;
- tingling or reduced sensation in the limbs or around the anus, possibly with involuntary loss of urine or faeces;
- pain in the back or front of the neck, in the back or at the back of the pelvis, spontaneously or on movement;
- a visible deformity of the head, neck or back;
- persistent severe headache, drowsiness, restlessness, memory loss or unconsciousness;
- nausea and vomiting;
- a serious head wound.

There can be a spinal injury even without symptoms. Babies and young children cannot tell you. People under the influence of alcohol or drugs also notice it less.

### Head injury

A head wound often bleeds heavily because the scalp has many blood vessels. It often looks worse than it is. A head wound is serious if it was caused by a heavy blow, if there is an object in it, if the bleeding cannot be controlled, if the face is injured or if the wound goes deeper than the skin.

Signs of a skull fracture: blood or fluid from the nose, mouth or ear, bruising around the eyes or behind the ears, a deformed skull.

Signs of brain injury or concussion after a blow, knock or jolt to the head or body:

- unconscious, even if only for a few seconds;
- changed behaviour: aggressive, slurred speech, loss of balance, a seizure. Children may behave strangely or become very quiet and stop playing;
- frequent yawning, sensitivity to light or noise, blurred or double vision;
- tingling or problems with balance or coordination;
- severe headache lasting longer than 2 hours;
- blood or clear fluid from the nose, ear or mouth;
- vomiting more than once;
- jerking movements or a rigid, twisted posture of the arms or legs.

These signs can appear immediately, but also only after hours or days.

## What to do

### Suspected spinal injury

1. Do not move the casualty, unless there is immediate danger.
2. Check for a response. If necessary, open the airway and check the breathing, but do not tilt the head back.
3. Call 112 or have someone call. If you cannot get through, see Call 112 if.
4. If conscious and breathing normally: calm the casualty and persuade them not to move. Ask them to keep their head and neck still themselves. If that does not work (for example with a child or someone who has been drinking), gently support the head with your hands. Only do this if the casualty cooperates, not with someone who is restless or agitated.
5. Unconscious but breathing normally: leave the casualty as you found them, on their back or on their side. Gently support the head so that it does not move. Check the level of consciousness and the breathing every minute.
6. Difficulty breathing: only push the jaw upwards, without tilting the head back.
7. If the casualty vomits while lying on their back: turn them onto their side immediately, no matter how, so that the vomit can drain out. If they are lying on their front: let the vomit drain out.
8. Unconscious and no longer breathing: start CPR (see topic 2).
9. If the casualty has to be moved after all because of danger, do so with at least two rescuers. One rescuer keeps the head in line with the back.

### Suspected pelvic fracture

Signs: pain in the hip, pelvis, groin or knee that gets worse on movement, being unable to bear weight on the legs, a foot in an abnormal position, tingling in the legs, being unable to pass urine or blood in the urine, and signs of shock.

1. Help the casualty to lie on their back, still, with the legs straight.
2. Put padding between the legs and tie the legs together at the knees and the feet.
3. Call 112 or have someone call.
4. Monitor breathing, circulation and level of consciousness and watch for shock.
5. Do not rock or turn the pelvis. That can restart or worsen bleeding.

### Head wound

1. Make sure the scene is safe. Wash or disinfect your hands and put on disposable gloves.
2. Find out what happened. Check the response and the breathing.
3. Minor head wound: treat it as a skin wound (see topic 17). Then cool for no more than 20 minutes.
4. Bump: cool with ice cubes in a bag of water or a cold pack, for no more than 20 minutes. Wrap the ice in a thin cloth. If you have no ice, use cold water.
5. Serious head wound: do not rinse it with water or antiseptic. Leave a loose flap of skin in place. If it is bleeding severely, press on the wound for 10 minutes and then apply a pressure bandage.
6. If you suspect a spinal injury, do not let the casualty move.

### Blow to the head without a wound

1. Have the person stop their activity, such as sport, and let them rest.
2. Monitor them: watch for the signs of brain injury and for changes in the level of consciousness and the breathing.
3. With signs of a serious head injury: call 112 or have someone call. Reassure the person and ask them to keep their head and neck still. If they are lying down, you may keep the head still with your hands or knees.
4. If there are no symptoms or only mild ones (a bump, a mild headache, slight nausea), let them rest and keep monitoring them.

## If help is a long time coming

- Keep the casualty as still as possible and handle them as little as possible. Keep monitoring the breathing and the level of consciousness.
- An unconscious casualty with a suspected spinal injury who is breathing normally: if help is coming soon, only keep the airway open by lifting the jaw or tilting the head. If help is still far away and there are at least two rescuers, you may place the casualty on their side while one rescuer keeps the head in line with the back.
- The Croix-Rouge de Belgique says that an open airway has priority. You can only leave a clearly injured casualty on their back if they are clearly breathing and the emergency services will be on the scene soon. If you are in doubt about the injury, place them in the recovery position.
- After a blow to the head without warning signs: the person may carry on with their day, but someone must keep an eye on them for the next 24 hours for signs that appear later. Concussion can develop over hours or days.
- Rest is usually the best first treatment. Only let the person drive, work with machinery or play contact sport again once all symptoms have gone.
- Keep the casualty warm by covering them with dry layers and protecting them from the wind.

## Call 112 if

- you suspect a spinal injury, skull fracture or brain injury;
- the casualty is unconscious, has been unconscious (even if only for a few seconds) or has a reduced level of consciousness;
- a violent force has acted on the head or body;
- after a blow to the head, the casualty shows changed behaviour, sensory disturbances, vision problems, persistent severe headache or blood or fluid from the nose, ear or mouth, or vomits more than once;
- you suspect a pelvic fracture.

Consult a doctor for a serious head wound, if the casualty feels unwell after the accident or the condition gets worse (increasing headache, nausea, drowsiness), and when in doubt.

**If you cannot get through**

- Only leave the casualty alone if there is no other option. Point directly at a bystander to get help and tell them where it is, what happened, how many casualties there are and what condition they are in.
- See also topic 1 (If you cannot get through: getting help without a phone) and Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call.

## Do not

- Do not move a casualty with a suspected spinal injury, unless there is immediate danger.
- Do not tilt the head back.
- Do not apply a cervical collar and do not strap down the head or neck.
- Do not try to keep the head and neck still with a restless casualty who is not cooperating.
- Do not rock or turn the pelvis if a pelvic fracture is suspected.
- Do not rinse a serious head wound and do not remove a loose flap of skin.
- Do not put ice directly on the skin and do not cool for longer than 20 minutes.
- Do not let someone with signs of concussion continue playing sport.
