## Recognise

### Hypothermia

Hypothermia begins when the core temperature of the body drops below 35 °C. Many body functions are then compromised. The risk is greater in wet weather or with wet clothing, in unheated homes, for homeless people, with the use of alcohol or drugs, and for people who are moving about or get lost in the cold. Hypothermia can also occur above 4 °C if someone is wet from rain, sweat or cold water.

Early hypothermia:

- uncontrollable shivering;
- cold, pale, dry skin that later turns blue at the lips, ears, fingers and toes;
- slow movements, clumsy hands, difficulty speaking, mild confusion;
- exhaustion, memory loss, drowsiness.

Severe hypothermia:

- the shivering stops;
- confusion, incoherent or strange behaviour;
- the person can no longer walk or use their hands;
- the muscles stiffen;
- breathing first becomes faster and later slow and shallow;
- reduced level of consciousness or unconsciousness.

In babies: bright red, cold skin and very little energy.

### Frostbite

Frostbite is damage to the skin and tissue caused by extreme cold, usually at 0 °C or lower or in strong wind. The nose, ears, cheeks, chin, fingers and toes are the most vulnerable.

- first redness or pain, a burning, prickling or numb feeling;
- then white or greyish-yellow skin;
- the skin feels unusually hard or waxy; blisters are possible;
- numbness: the person often only notices when someone tells them;
- at a later stage the skin turns dark blue or black.

## What to do

### Hypothermia

1. Handle the casualty gently. Avoid sudden, jerky movements. Move them horizontally.
2. Check for a response, open the airway and check the breathing. Look for long enough: breathing can be so slow that it seems as if the person is not breathing.
3. Take the casualty to a warmer place or protect them from further cooling.
4. If the clothing is wet, carefully remove it completely and dry the casualty. With moderate to severe hypothermia, cut the clothing off, so that the casualty moves as little as possible.
5. Insulate the casualty from the ground, for example with a tarpaulin and a sleeping bag.
6. Measure the body temperature if possible.
7. Ask: is the person still shivering and fully conscious?
8. Yes: rewarm passively. Put the casualty in a sleeping bag, or use a blanket (fleece or polyester), if possible with an insulating blanket. Cover the head as well. Dress them in warm, dry clothing. Give a warm, sugary drink or energy-rich food, but only if they can swallow properly.
9. No: rewarm actively, preferably with an electric blanket. If you do not have one, use a cherry-stone heat pillow, heat pads, a hot-water bottle or warm stones. Do not put these directly on the skin, so that the casualty does not get burned. In that case, do not give anything to eat or drink.
10. First rewarm the centre of the body: chest, neck, head and groin. Skin-to-skin contact under loose, dry layers also helps.
11. Call 112 or have someone call. If you cannot get through, see Call 112 if.
12. Reassure the casualty and monitor the breathing and level of consciousness.
13. Once the casualty is warmer, keep them dry and wrapped in a warm blanket, including around the head and neck.
14. If they become unconscious and are not breathing normally, start CPR (see topic 2). Handle them gently and keep rewarming during CPR.

### Frostbite

1. First check whether the person is also hypothermic. Hypothermia is more serious and takes priority.
2. Get the person to a warm room or shelter as quickly as possible. Remove wet clothing and rewarm them under dry blankets and clothing.
3. Carefully remove jewellery if this can be done without damaging the skin.
4. Only rewarm the frozen part if there is no risk of it freezing again. The IFRC only recommends rewarming on the spot if you have the means and medical care is more than 2 hours away.
5. Immerse the frozen part in warm water that feels pleasant on an uninjured area of skin. The IFRC gives 37 to 39 °C. Rewarm until the skin turns red or purple and feels soft and supple again, usually after about 30 minutes.
6. If you have no warm water, rewarm the part with body heat, for example frozen fingers in the armpit.
7. Do not rub or massage and do not handle the skin roughly.
8. Cover the part with sterile gauze. If fingers or toes are frozen, put gauze between each finger or toe.
9. Leave blisters intact.
10. Protect the rewarmed part from freezing again.
11. Always have frostbite checked by a doctor.

## If help is a long time coming

- Provide shelter. In an environment with few resources, you can make a fire to warm the casualty and build a shelter against the cold. Always put a layer, such as a blanket, between the casualty and the ground.
- Keep rewarming and monitor the temperature, level of consciousness and breathing.
- Give a warm, non-alcoholic drink to someone who is fully conscious and can swallow. Give nothing to someone who is unconscious.
- After rewarming, keep the casualty dry and wrapped up, including the head and neck.
- Hypothermic people in cardiac arrest must be given CPR without interruption until they have been rewarmed. The chances of recovery after cardiac arrest caused by mild to moderate hypothermia are excellent. In hypothermia, there is a chance that the person responds to CPR, even without a defibrillator. If you have to transport the casualty, continue CPR without interruption on a firm surface during transport.
- Frostbite: the IFRC recommends only rewarming frozen body parts if you have the necessary means, if medical care is more than 2 hours away and if the part cannot freeze again. If medical care is closer, do not rewarm the part yourself.
- Do not let someone with frozen feet or toes walk, unless it is really necessary. That increases the damage.
- After rewarming, you may apply aloe vera to the frostbitten skin.
- When you go to a cold, remote place, let someone know where you are going and when you intend to be back.

## Call 112 if

- the casualty is no longer shivering;
- the muscles stiffen;
- the level of consciousness decreases;
- the body temperature is below 35 °C;
- the casualty is unconscious or not breathing normally: in that case, also start CPR.

The IFRC recommends seeking medical help for any hypothermia. Always have frostbite checked by a doctor.

**If you cannot get through**

- Only leave the casualty alone if there is no other option. Point directly at a bystander to get help and tell them where it is, what happened, how many casualties there are and what condition they are in.
- If you have to leave yourself, have someone else stay with the casualty if possible. That person keeps the casualty warm in the meantime: wet clothing off, something between the casualty and the ground, dry layers around them, shelter from the wind.
- Have bystanders help to keep off wind and rain, for example with a tarpaulin.
- If you are giving CPR and are alone without a phone, see topic 2 (If you cannot get through).
- See also topic 1 (If you cannot get through: getting help without a phone) and Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call.

## Do not

- Do not make sudden or jerky movements with a hypothermic casualty.
- Do not rub a hypothermic casualty to warm them. That can damage skin and muscles.
- Do not rub or massage frozen skin and do not rub snow on it.
- Do not give anything to eat or drink during active rewarming, and never to someone who is unconscious.
- Do not give alcohol.
- Do not put warm stones, hot-water bottles or heat pads directly on the skin.
- Do not rewarm frozen skin at a stove, open fire, radiator, heat lamp, heating pad or electric blanket, and not with hot water. The numb skin burns quickly.
- Do not rewarm a frozen part if it can freeze again afterwards.
