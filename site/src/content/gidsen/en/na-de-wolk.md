## When sheltering ends

You stay indoors until the authorities announce that it is safe again. In principle, sheltering lasts no more than 24 hours. After that, one of three things follows:

| What the authorities announce | What you do |
| --- | --- |
| It is safe, sheltering is over | You may go outside. Follow the recommendations on food, water and cleaning |
| Evacuation | Follow the instructions on who, where to and how. An evacuation after the cloud is possible if sheltering would have to last longer or if the release is very large |
| Stay indoors | Keep sheltering and keep listening to the radio and the other official channels |

Medical advice may still follow after a nuclear accident. Listen to the radio and look out for police loudspeaker vans; if the network works, also follow BE-Alert and the government websites. The authorities announce when the danger is over, and give instructions on cleaning rooms and eating food.

Do not open the windows, do not switch the ventilation back on and do not leave the building before the authorities announce that it is safe.

### After a nuclear weapon

After a nuclear detonation, stay in the best-protected place for the first 24 hours, unless there is immediate danger or the authorities say you may leave. If you have to evacuate, listen to the information about routes, reception sites and procedures. Once you may leave the shelter, help the injured and people who are trapped.

## Returning after an evacuation

- **How long:** that depends on the situation. It can be after a few hours, but it can also take several days.
- **How you will know:** at the reception centre, the contact person gives the information. If you are staying with family or friends, listen to the radio. If the network works, also check the municipality's website or call the information number.
- **The zone:** the police set up a safety zone. Once it has been cleared, you may not enter that zone until the authorities allow it.
- **Do not go back earlier** than the local authorities say it is safe.
- **If you could not turn off the water, gas and electricity** before you left, you will get support from the competent services on your return if necessary.
- **Pets you had to leave behind:** if you still cannot go back after 24 hours, you will usually get adapted recommendations for your animals.

Source: [National Crisis Center: Evacuating](https://crisiscenter.be/en/what-can-you-do/right-reflex/evacuate) · [Nuclear risk: Evacuation](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/what-can-you-do/evacuation) · [National Crisis Center: brochure Prepared together](https://crisiscenter.be/sites/default/files/documents/files/WEB-Brochure%20Prepared%20Together-EN.pdf) · [Ready.gov: Radiation emergencies](https://www.ready.gov/radiation)

## The car, clothes and belongings

- **A car that was outside when the cloud passed:** avoid your clothes and skin touching the outside; open the doors with a cloth. Keep the windows closed and the ventilation off.
- **Contaminated clothing:** keep it in a closed plastic bag, outside or far from people and pets. Follow the instructions of the authorities on what to do with it.
- **Packaging and surfaces:** wipe them with a damp cloth. Put those cloths in a closed bag, away from people and pets. Then wash your hands.
- **Reception centre:** the authorities may set up a centre where you report for decontamination and a medical check.
- **Cleaning your home:** follow the instructions of the authorities at the time.

## Why food and water matter after the cloud

Contamination of the food chain comes mainly from radioactivity that settles on the ground as the cloud passes. Through food or drinking water, this can lead to internal contamination. People who eat contaminated food take in a large part of the radioactivity; it accumulates in organs and tissues and is gradually excreted. The chance of cancer or genetic abnormalities increases with the dose and with how long the substances remain in the body.

Two substances matter most:

| Substance | Half-life | Consequence for food |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Radioactive iodine | Within 8 days; decayed after a few weeks | Mainly a concern immediately after the accident; in the long term not a problem for consumption |
| Radioactive caesium | 30 years | Can remain in the environment for many years and remain a problem for food production |

Radioactive substances can settle on vegetables and animal feed, with rain or snow. Over time they can get into crops and animals through the soil, and into fish and seafood through rivers and the sea. Food can also become contaminated outside the evacuation zone.

Source: [Nuclear risk: frequently asked questions](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/faq) · [WHO: Radioactivity in food after a nuclear emergency](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/radioactivity-in-food-after-a-nuclear-emergency)

## Restrictions on food and water

The Belgian authorities can take these measures:

- a restriction or ban on the use of fresh crops;
- a ban on the use of drinking water or surface water;
- advice against using rainwater to water crops;
- a ban on milk from non-commercial channels;
- a grazing ban: (dairy) cattle must be kept indoors.

The measures can apply to farms and to people with their own vegetable garden.

### What consumers and producers do

In areas confirmed to be seriously contaminated:

- Do not eat locally produced milk or vegetables.
- Do not eat, harvest or catch fish, shellfish, algae or other aquatic animals and plants.
- Do not hunt and do not pick mushrooms or other wild food.

Food that was packaged and sealed before the accident, such as tins or plastic packaging, is safe. Food that had already been packaged or shipped before the emergency is not affected.

### Early in the emergency, if it can be done safely

Producers can take these measures before the fallout:

- Cover feed and food that is outside with plastic or an impermeable tarpaulin.
- Close the ventilation of greenhouses.
- Bring livestock in from the pasture to a shed.
- Harvest ripe crops and put them under cover before any fallout has been measured.
- Do not harvest after the fallout; wait for instructions.

Source: [Nuclear risk: Protecting the food chain](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/what-can-you-do/protection-food-chain) · [WHO: Radioactivity in food after a nuclear emergency](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/radioactivity-in-food-after-a-nuclear-emergency)

## How the authorities check food

The Federal Agency for Nuclear Control (FANC) monitors the food chain throughout Belgium. It analyses drinking water, milk, foodstuffs and control meals. Milk is a sensitive pathway: iodine passes quickly from grass to cows and ends up in the milk, and because milk is distributed quickly, the population would take it in quickly. In milk, foodstuffs and control meals, the FANC looks for strontium, caesium and iodine, among other things.

After an emergency, national authorities must check food and restrict the consumption and distribution of some products if the levels exceed the permitted limits. Internationally, the guideline levels of the Codex Alimentarius (FAO and WHO) apply to traded food: if food stays below those levels, it is considered safe. Countries can choose other levels for their own use. The authorities should make their measurement results and decisions public.

### Drinking water

European requirements apply to drinking water (Directive 2013/51/Euratom), transposed in Belgium into the royal decree of 31 May 2016 and a technical decree of the FANC of 24 November 2016. Every drinking water supplier must submit a self-monitoring programme to the FANC each year and have radioactivity analyses carried out at its own expense. In a nuclear emergency, the authorities can ban the use of drinking water or surface water. Then follow their instructions. Bottled water and sealed drinks are best; if there is nothing else, use tap water to stay hydrated. You may use tap water to wash, even if the authorities recommend bottled water for drinking.

### Breastfeeding

Mothers are understandably worried about radioactivity in breast milk after a radiation emergency. This transfer is only a concern in extreme situations, such as for the population close to the worst-affected area, and especially with a large release of radioactive iodine. Iodine tablets, together with checks on food and drinking water, are then an important measure. Children, adolescents, pregnant women and breastfeeding women benefit most. The authorities give the necessary guidelines.

## Agriculture, livestock and vegetable garden

- **Livestock indoors:** the authorities can impose a grazing ban. (Dairy) cattle must then be kept indoors, so that no contamination occurs via milk or meat.
- **Evacuation of livestock** is only considered if the livestock in the affected area cannot be housed adequately, the evacuation area is small enough and there is enough time to do it without risk of contamination. People take priority over animals.
- **Vegetable garden:** do not eat vegetables from the garden or anything that was outside uncovered until the authorities say it is safe. Do not use rainwater to water crops.
- **Milk from non-commercial channels:** the authorities can ban its use.
- **Game, mushrooms, fish:** in seriously contaminated areas, do not hunt, do not pick mushrooms or other wild food, and do not harvest fish, shellfish or algae.

The measures can apply to farms and to private individuals. They are meant to prevent any internal contamination from eating contaminated food.

## Measurements: where to find them

PlanB72 does not show live measurements and gives no instructions for measuring or estimating radiation doses yourself. Measurement data are interpreted by specialists. When you have a connection again, you can find the official measurements here:

| Monitoring network | What it is | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Telerad | The Belgian network of about 250 monitoring stations that check radioactivity in the air and in river water, with more stations around the nuclear installations. If a value is abnormal, they automatically send an alarm to the FANC experts | telerad.fgov.be |
| EURDEP | The European map of the European Commission (JRC) with hourly averages of the gamma dose rate, going back up to 35 days, from about 5,500 stations. Most data have not been validated | remap.jrc.ec.europa.eu |

In addition to Telerad, a radiological surveillance programme regularly takes samples of water, air, soil and plant and animal products throughout Belgium, especially around nuclear sites.

If too high a dose is measured in the air, the authorities advise going indoors or staying indoors. Follow the instructions of the authorities, not your own interpretation of a map.

Source: [Nuclear risk: frequently asked questions](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/faq) · [Telerad](http://telerad.fgov.be) · [National Crisis Center: Incident at a nuclear power plant](https://crisiscenter.be/en/risks-belgium/technological-risks/nuclear-plant-incident) · [EURDEP Advanced Map](https://remap.jrc.ec.europa.eu/Advanced.aspx)

## Health

- **Long-term risks:** the risks to the population are mainly long-term risks, such as cancer and genetic abnormalities. The probability, not the severity, increases with the dose.
- **Acute symptoms** such as vomiting, burns and massive hair loss only occur after massive exposure, which in principle is only expected among workers at the installation and possibly emergency responders. The general public is unlikely to receive a dose high enough for acute effects.
- **Medical advice:** medical advice may still follow after an accident. Listen to the radio and follow the other official channels.
- **Sick or injured:** listen to the instructions on where to get medical help. If you are in a public shelter, tell the staff immediately. Call 112 in a medical emergency, or have someone call. If you cannot get through, see Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call.
- **Children:** they are more sensitive to radiation. Have them followed up medically after the emergency as soon as the authorities say it is safe.
- **Pregnant women who took iodine:** tell your doctor after the emergency, so that it is recorded in your medical file.
- **Newborns who were given iodine:** have their thyroid levels monitored.

## Psychological support

Going through a radiation emergency can be hard and traumatic. Take care of yourself and talk to someone if you feel bad. A terrorist attack can lead to post-traumatic shock. Thinking about emergencies causes stress that can make you feel anxious, angry or depressed. Stress, worry and fear are normal.

### What you can do yourself

- Talk about your feelings with family, friends, someone you trust or your GP.
- Eat, drink and sleep enough.
- Regularly take a break from the news and social media.
- Find distractions: play a game with your children, read a book, listen to music.
- Move regularly. Only go outside again when the authorities say it is safe.
- Breathe calmly and deeply when you notice the tension rising.

### Supporting each other on the spot

Psychological first aid is help from person to person, on the spot. Anyone can give it:

- Provide safety and calm. Speak calmly and softly.
- Listen without forcing people to talk.
- Help with practical things: water, warmth, correct information, finding family.
- Stay together and keep children with their parents or carers.
- Do not leave anyone who is very distressed or could harm themselves alone. Look for help from people nearby or medical help on the spot.

More in the guide on mental resilience and psychological first aid.

### Children

- Create a calm atmosphere.
- Listen to their questions and feelings and take them seriously. Tell them it is normal to be afraid, sad, angry or unsure.
- Give a simple, honest explanation that suits their age. Say so if you do not know something and look for answers together from reliable sources.
- Explain who they can turn to for help.
- Keep to the daily routine as much as possible.

## Correct information and rumours

After an emergency, a lot of information circulates, especially on social media. It can be wrong, misleading or out of date. The Crisis Center asks you to be critical:

- Check whether the information comes from an official source.
- Check whether several channels or sources confirm it.
- See whether the message looks reliable.

Only share messages from the authorities or emergency services yourself. Follow the communication from the authorities, the emergency services and the municipality, even if the worst seems to be over. If the network goes down, listen to a battery-powered radio or the car radio. The authorities can also use police loudspeaker vans, information boards, leaflets and door-to-door communication.

Source: [National Crisis Center: brochure Prepared together](https://crisiscenter.be/sites/default/files/documents/files/WEB-Brochure%20Prepared%20Together-EN.pdf) · [National Crisis Center: Information during an emergency](https://crisiscenter.be/en/what-can-you-do/stay-informed/get-informed-emergency-situation)

## Helping each other

- Regularly check whether vulnerable people in your neighbourhood need help, without putting yourself in danger.
- Share reliable information from the authorities with people who are less well informed.
- If the network works, send a message via a messaging app or social media rather than calling, so that the telephone network does not become overloaded.
- Offer to be a contact person for family and friends who had to evacuate.
