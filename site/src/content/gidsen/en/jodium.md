## About this guide

This guide explains what iodine tablets do, who in Belgium should keep them at home and when to take them. It contains **no dosages**. The dose depends on your age and is given on the packaging and in the package leaflet of your box. At the time itself, the authorities say who must take the tablets. Read the package leaflet and follow the instructions of the authorities.

Source: [Nuclear risk: When to take them](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/iodine-tablets/when-should-you-take-them) · [Nuclear risk: frequently asked questions](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/faq)

## What iodine tablets do

The thyroid needs iodine to work properly. You normally get these small amounts from your food. In a nuclear accident, radioactive iodine may be released. The thyroid makes no distinction between ordinary and radioactive iodine and absorbs both. Radioactive iodine in the thyroid irradiates it from the inside and increases the risk of thyroid cancer, especially in children.

Radioactive iodine enters the body in three ways:

| Route | What helps |
| --- | --- |
| Breathing in air containing radioactive iodine | Largely avoidable by staying indoors with windows and doors closed. That is why sheltering is the best measure |
| Eating or drinking contaminated food | The authorities take measures, for example a ban on milk or vegetables |
| Absorption through the skin | Usually negligible |

Iodine tablets contain ordinary, non-radioactive iodine: according to the Belgian authorities, about a thousand times the amount needed daily. If you take them at the right moment, your thyroid is saturated and no longer absorbs radioactive iodine. You quickly pass the excess iodine in your urine. The WHO calls this measure iodine thyroid blocking.

## What iodine tablets do not do

- **They only protect the thyroid.** They offer no protection against other radioactive substances.
- **They do not protect against radiation from outside**, for example from radioactivity on the ground, on surfaces or on food (WHO).
- **They do not keep radioactive iodine out of your body.** They only prevent it from accumulating in the thyroid (WHO).
- **They are not an antidote to radiation** (WHO).
- **Not every nuclear accident releases radioactive iodine.** Iodine is only one of the possible components of a radioactive cloud. An accident may still be in an early phase without any release.
- **They do not make it safe to go outside.** Even after taking them, you stay indoors.

That is why sheltering always takes priority over taking iodine, especially if you have no tablets at home. The recommendation to take iodine always coincides with the recommendation to shelter. Finland also says: iodine only protects the thyroid and is only a supplement to sheltering indoors.

## Why you may also have to take iodine indoors

Closing windows and doors keeps most of it out. Even so, a limited amount of dust particles always gets in, for example through ventilation systems and gaps. That is why the authorities may recommend taking iodine tablets during sheltering as well. Only take them if the authorities actually recommend it.

Source: [Nuclear risk: frequently asked questions](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/faq)

## Who in Belgium should keep iodine tablets at home

| Group | Advice of the Belgian authorities |
| --- | --- |
| Residents of a municipality within 20 km of a nuclear site (10 km around IRE Fleurus) | Get iodine tablets in the house, even if your municipality lies only partly in the zone. Even more important if you have or want children |
| Children and young people up to 18 years, throughout Belgium | Collect a box |
| Pregnant women and women who are breastfeeding, throughout Belgium | Collect a box |
| Institutions with children (crèches, day nurseries, nursery, primary and secondary schools), throughout Belgium | Keep a supply as a precaution |
| Institutions within 20 km (10 km around Fleurus): schools, companies, shops, hospitals, sports and cultural centres | Keep a supply for staff and visitors |
| Adults between 18 and 40 years | Less sensitive; may also be advised to take iodine if the authorities explicitly recommend it |
| Adults over 40 years | Discuss its use with your GP, separately from any emergency. If you live in the zone, do collect tablets, for grandchildren or neighbours who visit |

Nobody is excluded. Grandparents, for example, can keep tablets at home for grandchildren who come to visit.

### Why the age limit of 40 years

- The risk of thyroid cancer from radioactive iodine decreases with age.
- After 40 years, the thyroid more often functions abnormally, especially in iodine-poor regions such as Belgium. The risk of side effects is then higher. With a severely disordered thyroid, the disadvantages, such as heart problems, may be greater than the benefits.
- The limit of 40 years follows the knowledge and recommendations of the World Health Organization. The WHO says that iodine thyroid blocking is generally not indicated for adults over 40 years, with the exception of emergency workers.

### Difference with other countries

Finland recommends iodine for people aged 40 years or younger, and in the event of a shortage gives priority to children, pregnant women and women who are breastfeeding. In Finland you buy the tablets without a prescription at the pharmacy. In Belgium they are free. In Belgium, follow the Belgian advice.

## Where to get them

1. Collect them in advance as a precaution: go to a pharmacy. In every municipality within the zone, all pharmacists have iodine tablets. Outside the zones, pharmacists throughout the country also have boxes.
2. Take your identity card with you.
3. The pharmacist gives you one box per four people.
4. The tablets are free.

For an institution, there is an online calculation tool on nucleairrisico.be to determine the number of boxes. Take into account the total number of people who may be present, with a reasonable estimate of external visitors. If the pharmacy's stock is too small, the boxes sometimes have to be ordered first.

In addition, Belgium has strategic stocks: centrally in a pharmaceutical warehouse and spread across the units of the Civil Protection. In a nuclear emergency, these can quickly be brought to the affected zone.

## Storage and shelf life

- Keep the tablets in their original packaging (blister). It protects against light and moisture.
- Store them at room temperature, dry and dark, like other medicines. Above all, protect them from heat.
- Only take them out of the packaging when the authorities recommend it. Outside the packaging they deteriorate quickly.
- Keep them in a place that everyone in the house knows, for example in the medicine cabinet. The Crisis Center includes them in the emergency kit list.
- Only the production date is printed on the packaging. The tablets keep for at least 10 years. Like table salt, the iodine hardly degrades.
- The most recent boxes in circulation have production date 2017.
- If you still have boxes with production date 2010 or 2011, exchange them free of charge at your pharmacy for a newer box. Leave the tablets at the pharmacy; the empty box can go in the paper recycling.
- Yellow or yellowish-brown spots on the tablets are caused by slow oxidation. This does not affect how they work.

Sciensano regularly tests the older tablets for their effectiveness.

## When to take them

**Never take iodine tablets on your own initiative.** Only take them when the competent authorities explicitly advise you to.

- **Why the timing matters:** iodine only works if you take it at the right moment, ideally just before or as soon as possible after a release of radioactive iodine. The authorities choose the moment at which the tablets are most effective, on the advice of experts. Finland says: taking them too early or too late reduces the protection.
- **What the WHO says about the time window:** the best period is from less than 24 hours before to 2 hours after the expected start of exposure. Up to 8 hours afterwards is still reasonable. Later than 24 hours after exposure it no longer offers protection. In Belgium, the authorities decide when.
- **How you hear about it:** the authorities announce it via radio and TV. When you shelter, you are inside with windows closed: so keep a battery-powered or wind-up radio ready. Police loudspeaker vans also drive through the streets, so that you receive the information even without a landline or mobile phone. If the network works, the recommendation also comes via BE-Alert. It may be limited to a certain zone or a certain group, for example pregnant women or young children.
- **Accident in a neighbouring country:** only take the tablets if the Belgian authorities explicitly recommend it.
- **How long you are protected:** after taking them you remain protected for at least 24 hours. Even in a serious accident, it is very likely that taking them once is enough. Keep sheltering in the meantime.

## How to take them

- For each family member, check the dose on the packaging and in the package leaflet. It contains drawings per age group. Different doses apply to adults, children, babies, pregnant women and women who are breastfeeding.
- The Belgian authorities recommend not simply swallowing the tablets, but dissolving them in a large glass of lukewarm water. Milk or fruit juice masks the taste. The tablets do not dissolve easily: break or crush them and stir.
- Iodine slightly irritates the stomach. Do not take it with alcohol.

Source: [Nuclear risk: frequently asked questions](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/faq) · [Nuclear risk: When to take them](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/iodine-tablets/when-should-you-take-them)

## Babies, pregnancy and breastfeeding

- **Babies:** the younger the child, the more important the protection. The recommendation certainly applies to babies. If your baby refuses to drink it, try again later. If the baby keeps refusing: staying indoors removes most of the risk, and part of the iodine the mother takes passes into the breast milk (Belgian authorities). The WHO says that the iodine in breast milk is not enough to protect a baby and that the baby also receives iodine itself. Follow the instructions of the authorities and the package leaflet.
- **Newborns younger than one month:** the WHO recommends having their thyroid levels monitored after taking iodine, with a consultation with the paediatrician within the first week.
- **Breastfeeding:** normally there is no reason to stop breastfeeding. For children younger than one month, it is sometimes recommended to express the milk and throw it away for 24 hours, giving formula during that period, and then to resume breastfeeding (Belgian authorities).
- **Pregnancy:** pregnant women only take the tablets when instructed. In this way they protect their own thyroid and that of the unborn child. After the emergency, tell your doctor, so that it is recorded in your file (WHO).

## Side effects and who had better not take them

- Iodine tablets have low toxicity. If a child takes several, do what you would do with any medicine: consult your doctor or pharmacist, or call the Poison Centre (070 245 245) if the network works.
- Side effects are usually temporary and harmless, such as vomiting or a bad taste. In exceptional cases there are hypersensitivity reactions: red skin, fluid retention, pain in the neck, watering eyes, cold symptoms, swollen salivary glands, fever. These normally disappear by themselves. Do not take iodine again. If the problem persists or the condition worsens, contact your doctor.
- The small risk of hypersensitivity is not a reason not to take the tablets.
- A small minority had better not take them: people who are allergic to iodine, people with a rare skin disease and people who have or have had thyroid problems. The package leaflet gives the full list. If in doubt, talk to your GP or specialist now.
- Iodine tablets have very few interactions with other medicines and no effect on the pill or other contraceptives.
- If you are allergic or ill at the time of the accident and cannot take anything: staying indoors is the best protection. Do not leave when you are in the middle of a radioactive cloud. Do not take another medicine as a substitute, unless your doctor explicitly tells you to.
- If you refuse for religious or other reasons, nobody can force you. The consequences are at your own risk.

Source: [Nuclear risk: frequently asked questions](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/faq) · [WHO: Use of potassium iodide](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/use-of-potassium-iodide-for-thyroid-protection-during-nuclear-or-radiological-emergencies)

## If you have no tablets

- **Stay indoors.** Sheltering offers the fastest and most effective protection.
- The authorities use the time between the first signs of a problem and the release to issue warnings, distribute stocks and, if necessary, ask the population to collect tablets from the pharmacy. Listen to the recommendations on the radio.
- If you have no tablets, you can also get them from the emergency services: there are stocks throughout the country, and in an accident the emergency services can distribute them to the population at risk.
- If at the time of the accident you are at a football match, in a cinema, shop or petrol station: institutions in the zones are asked to keep tablets and distribute them when the authorities recommend it. The Civil Protection also has a stock that it can hand out.
- Finland says: do not go to the pharmacy to collect tablets if a recommendation to take them has already been issued.

## What is not a substitute

- **Iodised salt.** The salt you normally use does not contain enough iodine to block radioactive iodine. Eating a lot of salt is itself dangerous (WHO).
- **Sea air and fish.** Iodine that you breathe in at the seaside or eat in fish and shellfish offers some protection, but not enough in a nuclear accident (Belgian authorities).
- **Other medicines,** unless your doctor explicitly recommends them (Belgian authorities).

Iodine-rich food such as fish, shellfish and iodised salt is generally good, though, because our diet usually contains too little iodine.

## Why children get priority

The younger you are, the more vulnerable you are to radioactive iodine. In children in the area around Chernobyl, who took in radioactive iodine through contaminated milk and food, the increase in thyroid cancer was only observed four to five years after 1986 (WHO). The WHO says that iodine reduces the risk of thyroid cancer in people aged 0 to 18 years who have been exposed to radioactive iodine. Radioactive iodine loses half its radioactivity within 8 days and has decayed after a few weeks. According to the WHO, radioactive iodine in food is therefore mainly a concern immediately after an accident, and not a long-term problem for consumption.

## In brief

1. Get tablets in the house if you belong to a target group or live in a zone.
2. Keep them in the original packaging in a fixed place.
3. Read the package leaflet now and discuss any doubts with your GP.
4. In an accident: go indoors first and stay indoors.
5. Only take the tablets if the Belgian authorities say so, at the moment they say. You will hear it on the radio, via police loudspeaker vans or, if the network works, via BE-Alert.
6. After that, stay indoors until the authorities say it is safe.
