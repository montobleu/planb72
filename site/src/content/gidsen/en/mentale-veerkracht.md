Thinking about emergencies and risks can cause stress, worry and anxiety. That is normal. Good preparation helps in practical terms and brings peace of mind: if you know what to do, you feel less powerless and less anxious.

During a disaster, telephone and internet may not work. Mental support is then what people on the spot do for each other: psychological first aid. Helplines are listed at the bottom, for after the crisis.

## What you may notice in yourself

These reactions to a crisis, during and afterwards, are common:

- Physical complaints: shaking, headaches, feeling very tired, loss of appetite, pain.
- Crying, sadness, low mood, grief.
- Anxiety, being on guard or jumpy, fearing that something very bad is going to happen.
- Insomnia and nightmares.
- Irritability and anger.
- Guilt and shame, for example because you survived or could not help or save others.
- Being confused, emotionally numb, feeling unreal or dazed.

Unexpected reactions of body and mind are normal. Some people are only mildly upset, or not at all. Most people recover well, especially once their basic needs are met again and they receive support from the people around them. The great majority get through a disaster without professional help.

## What helps before, during and after an emergency

- Talk about your feelings and problems with family, friends, your GP or someone you trust. The feeling of not being alone with your fears can already help. Coming to terms with things together works better than alone: seek contact with people who went through the same thing.
- Think in small, achievable steps. Keep up daily routines and make a plan for the day. After a disaster, do not only look at the damage, but plan and discuss the next steps together.
- Get enough sleep if possible, but do not sleep all day. Drink enough and eat regularly and healthily. Exercise and go outside where possible.
- Breathe calmly and deeply when tension rises. Take breaks and time to rest. Look for distraction and relaxation: read, listen to music, walk, sing, pray or play with children.
- Do not follow more news and social media than necessary, and do so at fixed times.
- Help others in a safe way, for example by listening or by helping them to prepare. This gives the feeling of doing something meaningful, so that you feel less helpless.
- Seek professional help if you have severe anxiety. If insomnia and nightmares persist, see When to seek more help later.

**Avoid** drugs, smoking and alcohol, working all the time without rest, isolating yourself from loved ones, neglecting basic hygiene, and violence.

For sleeping warm without heating, see the guide on heat and cooling.

## Psychological first aid on the spot

Psychological first aid is a humane, supportive response to someone who is suffering and may need support. Anyone can do it, including ordinary citizens. It is not professional counselling and does not require a detailed discussion of what happened. It usually concerns the first contact, during or shortly after the event, wherever it is safe enough. It is often a single conversation of 30 to 60 minutes.

The basis is look, listen and link. Recovery rests on safety, calm, self-efficacy (believing that you can cope, on your own and together), connectedness and hope. Preferably help together with others, such as a neighbourhood group: anyone who acts alone may put themselves in danger. Follow the instructions of the authorities and do not hinder rescue work.

**1. Ensure safety**

- Check whether the place is safe. If you are not sure, do not go, and try to arrange help or speak to people from a distance.
- Look for who urgently needs something and who is reacting very strongly. With injuries, medical help always comes first. See the first aid cards.
- Move the person away from immediate danger if this can be done safely. Shield them from the media.

**2. Bring calm and listen**

1. Introduce yourself and ask whether you can help. Approach people with respect and in line with their culture. If possible, find a quiet place and offer water, for example.
2. Sit or stand at the same height.
3. Ask what someone needs and what they are worried about, even if it seems obvious.
4. Stay close, at a distance that suits age, gender and culture. Do not force anyone to talk, but listen if someone wants to tell their story. Simply staying quietly with someone is also support.
5. Let people react in their own way and at their own pace. Do not say "stop" or "calm down" when someone is crying or shouting.

| Do | Do not |
| --- | --- |
| Respect privacy | put pressure on or rush someone, for example by looking at your watch or phone |
| Show that you are listening; allow silence | judge what someone did or feels |
| Acknowledge feelings, losses and strengths | make false promises or give false reassurance; make things up |
| Use simple words | tell your own worries or someone else's story; speak negatively about people |

**Helping someone calm down**

- Stay calm yourself: that calms others. Speak softly, in short, clear sentences. Remind them that you are there to help, and that they are safe if that is true.
- If someone feels unreal, ask them to feel their feet on the floor, to tap their fingers on their lap, or to say what they see and hear that is not distressing.
- Help them breathe slowly: set the example and let the person follow your rhythm.
- Chest pain and shortness of breath can also be caused by a heart attack or asthma. If in doubt, get medical help as quickly as possible.
- Only touch someone if you have first asked permission.

**3. Help with practical things**

- Provide water, clothing or a blanket as quickly as possible: people who are cold or thirsty find it hard to concentrate.
- Help decide what needs doing now and what can wait. Ask how someone got through difficult situations in the past.
- For example, fetch someone who can stay with the person, make sure the children are picked up, help with transport to a safe place, or wait with them until more help arrives.
- Let the person do small things and make decisions themselves where possible.
- Only give information you have, say what you do not know and where your information comes from. Keep it simple and preferably give it to a group at once. Say whether and when you will come back with news, and then do come back.
- Make sure vulnerable people are not overlooked.

**4. Stay together**

- Keep families together. Contact with family or friends takes priority, including in person.
- Bring affected people together so that they help each other, for example by looking after older people. Put people without family in touch with others from the neighbourhood.
- During a power cut you can come together to share light and warmth.

**5. Keep children with their parents**

- Do not let children become separated from their carers. Bringing a child to its carers takes priority.
- Ask parents for permission first before you talk to their child, and help them to care for their child themselves.
- If a parent is taken away for medical care, keep the children with that parent, or note where the parent is being taken.
- If you find a child on its own: do not leave it alone. Note its name and where it comes from. Take it to a child protection organisation or emergency service if there is one, or otherwise look for the carers or family yourself.

**6. Closing**

Explain that you are leaving and introduce whoever takes over. Say goodbye on a positive note. If someone is very distressed, see Who needs more help immediately; in case of loss, see Grief and loss.

## Who needs more help immediately

Some people need more help immediately than psychological first aid: those with serious, life-threatening injuries, those so upset that they cannot look after themselves or their children, and those who could harm themselves or others.

- Know your limits and bring in others: medical staff, other helpers, people from the neighbourhood, the local authorities.
- Do not leave someone who is severely distressed alone. Keep them safe until the reaction has passed, or until you find help.
- Physically injured: medical help first. See the first aid cards.

**If someone could harm themselves**

- Ask directly whether they are thinking of harming themselves and whether they have a concrete plan. Such a question brings relief and does not put ideas into anyone's head.
- The risk is higher with a clear plan (how, where, when), if the means are at hand, after a previous attempt and with the use of alcohol or drugs.
- If there is a concrete threat, do not leave the person alone and stay with them until they get the help they need. Remove objects they could use to hurt themselves, as far as possible in agreement with them.
- Say that you cannot carry the responsibility alone, and seek help from a care provider or people around them.
- Do not try to persuade them to go on living for the sake of others: that can reinforce feelings of guilt. Do not promise secrecy.

**Aggression.** Do not stay alone with the person: bring in others immediately. Keep your distance and an open posture, do not touch the person, speak calmly, and bring others to safety if necessary.

**If life is in danger.** Call 112 or have someone call. If you cannot get through: try another phone or have neighbours or bystanders call. If calling does not work at all, ask for help in your neighbourhood. Stay with the person yourself and send someone else to get help (see Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call).

## Children before, during and after an emergency

Children quickly notice that something is wrong and deserve extra attention. They do better when a stable, calm adult is with them.

**Talking with children**

- First create a calm atmosphere: children sense what adults feel.
- Take their questions and worries seriously. Tell them it is normal to be scared, sad, angry or unsure. If you are shaken yourself, explain why.
- Answer honestly, simply and rather briefly, adapted to their age, without unnecessary details. If you do not know something, say so.
- Do not force a conversation. Some children express themselves better through drawing or in their play. Re-enacting what happened is normal, as long as it stays safe.
- Give positive messages: we are looking after you, we are here for you, we are well prepared. Explain who they can turn to.
- Keep the daily routine as much as possible: familiar habits give something to hold on to.
- Beforehand: practise simple safety rules in a playful way. Involve children in the emergency kit and let them choose a cuddly toy to put in it.

**What you may see and do**

Every child reacts differently: some not noticeably at first, others with concentration problems, nightmares, anxiety or irritability. Show understanding.

| Age | Possible reactions | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Babies | | keep warm and safe; keep away from noise and chaos; cuddle; regular feeding and sleeping times if possible |
| Young children | regressing to earlier behaviour such as bedwetting or thumb-sucking; clinging; repeatedly playing about the event | often repeat that they are safe; explain that it is not their fault; let them stay close to you; be patient with regression |
| School-age children | thinking that they are to blame; new fears; feeling alone; being preoccupied with saving people | explain what happened and what is happening now; listen without judging |
| Adolescents | feeling nothing; feeling cut off from friends; risky behaviour | allow grief and do not expect them to be brave; clear rules; discuss how to avoid dangers; give opportunities to help |

**Protecting.** Keep children with their carers. Protect them from gruesome images, distressing stories, the media and people who want to interview them.

## People with a disability, dementia or a mental health condition

- People with severe mental health conditions, vulnerable older people, pregnant women and people with sight or hearing problems may need extra help. Help them to a safe place, ask about their condition and medication, and stay with them or make sure someone else stays with them.
- With dementia: do not leave the person alone; even someone who does not normally wander off may do so in an unfamiliar environment. Take a familiar object along, such as a pillow or blanket. In the shelter, find a quiet corner away from the exits. When the person is restless, respond to the emotion they are expressing.
- For people who struggle with an unfamiliar or chaotic environment: noise-cancelling headphones, a small pop-up tent or sheets to limit stimuli, familiar snacks, charged devices with videos and activities.

Source: [Ready.gov: People with Disabilities](https://www.ready.gov/disability) · [WHO: Psychological first aid](https://psychiatry.uw.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/1.-WHO_PFA_2011.pdf)

## Grief and loss

- Sorrow and grief are among the normal reactions to a crisis. Everyone grieves differently and there is no fixed timetable. Most people adjust over time.
- Acknowledge the loss, such as of a home or the death of a loved one, and say that you are concerned. Say, for example, that you are sorry and that you can imagine it is very sad.
- Give time and space. Listen without judging and do not look for solutions too quickly. Do not tell someone how they ought to feel, for example not: you should be glad you survived.
- Small gestures can offer support, such as a bowl of soup. Ask what you can do and keep your promises.
- If someone does not want to talk, respect that, but stay in touch. Encourage them to talk with people close to them. Get in touch again after a few weeks and months: support from those around them then decreases, while those affected still need it.
- Young children do not always understand yet that someone who dies does not come back, and may blame themselves. Explain that it is not their fault.
- If you have lost track of someone after a disaster, the Restoring Family Links service of Rode Kruis-Vlaanderen can help search (see the guide on evacuation).

## Looking after yourself when you help others

- Consider honestly whether you are ready to help, given your own health and worries.
- Take time to eat, rest and relax, even if only briefly. Share the work, work in shifts and rest regularly.
- You are not responsible for solving every problem. Help people to help themselves.
- Limit alcohol, caffeine and nicotine.
- Watch for your own signs of stress and accept your own reactions. Talk with other helpers and people you trust. Being part of a team, keeping your daily routines and being able to let go of the work protect you.
- Afterwards, take time to rest and look back. Acknowledge what you were able to do, even in small things, and the limits of what was possible.

## When to seek more help later

- If you keep worrying or need to talk to someone, do not hesitate to seek help.
- If after the disaster you have distressing thoughts or memories, feel very nervous or sad, sleep badly, or drink a lot of alcohol or take drugs, seek support from someone you trust. If the complaints last longer than a month, speak to a care provider.
- People with severe or prolonged stress reactions may need more support than psychological first aid, especially if they can no longer function in daily life or are a danger to themselves or others.
- If the reactions to a loss are unchanged about six months later, it may be prolonged grief and specialist help is advisable.
- If you are very worried about your child, seek advice from a paediatrician or a care professional.
