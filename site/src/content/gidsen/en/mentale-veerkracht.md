Thinking about emergencies and risks can cause stress, worry and anxiety. The Crisis Center says that this is normal. Good preparation does not only help in practical terms, it also brings peace of mind: if you know what to do, you feel less powerless. The BBK says the same: in danger or in an extreme situation it is normal to feel helpless, and being prepared helps to reduce fear and worry.

This guide is about yourself, about children and about how to support others. During a disaster, telephone and internet may not work. Mental support is then what people on the spot do for each other: psychological first aid. Helplines are listed separately, at the bottom, for after the crisis.

## Before the emergency: if you are worried

**What the Belgian brochure recommends**

- Talk about your feelings with family, friends or your GP.
- Make sure you get enough sleep and healthy food.
- Move regularly, exercise and get outside enough.
- Breathe calmly and deeply when you notice the tension rising.
- Take time for an activity that demands your attention: read a book, listen to music, play a game.
- Take regular breaks from the news and social media.

**What other sources add**

- Think in small, achievable steps. Choose one thing to start with (Denk vooruit).
- See whether you can help others, for example by listening to them (Denk vooruit). The MCF says that helping out, for example by helping others to prepare, can give you a sense of purpose.
- Limit how much news you follow. Find a level that works for you and spend more time on things that make you feel good (MCF).
- Seek professional help if you have severe anxiety (MCF).

## During and after: what you may notice in yourself

The WHO lists these reactions to a crisis. They are common:

- Physical complaints: shaking, headache, feeling very tired, loss of appetite, aches and pains.
- Crying, sadness, low mood, grief.
- Anxiety and fear.
- Being on guard or jumpy.
- Fearing that something very bad is going to happen.
- Insomnia and nightmares.
- Irritability and anger.
- Guilt and shame, for example because you survived or could not help or save others.
- Being confused, emotionally numb, feeling unreal or in a daze.

Some people are only mildly upset, or not at all. Most people recover well over time, especially when their basic needs are met again and they receive support from the people around them (WHO). The Red Cross brochure also says that unexpected reactions of body and mind are normal after such an event. The vast majority of those affected get over a disaster without professional help (Impact).

## Psychological first aid: what it is

**What it is.** According to the WHO, psychological first aid (PFA) is a humane, supportive response to someone who is suffering and who may need support. It involves:

- Providing practical care and support, without being intrusive.
- Assessing what someone needs and what they are worried about.
- Helping to meet basic needs, such as food, water and information.
- Listening, without pressuring someone to talk.
- Comforting and helping someone to feel calm.
- Helping to connect with information, services and social support.
- Protecting from further harm.

**What it is not.** It is not something that only professionals can do. It is not professional counselling. It does not require a detailed discussion of what happened (WHO).

**Who and when.** Anyone can do it: volunteers, first responders and ordinary citizens (IFRC). It is usually about the first contact, during or shortly after the event, wherever it is safe enough (WHO). It is often a single conversation of 30 to 60 minutes (IFRC).

**What helps recovery.** The IFRC and the Dutch guideline from Impact name five principles on which experts agree: safety, calm and reassurance, self-efficacy (believing that you can cope, on your own and together), social connectedness and hope.

## During the crisis: psychological first aid on the spot

The WHO builds psychological first aid on three principles: look, listen and link. The sources describe it as help from person to person, on the spot. Preferably do it together with others, for example with a neighbourhood group or an aid organisation: anyone who acts alone can put themselves in danger. Follow the instructions of the authorities and do not get in the way of search and rescue work (WHO).

**1. Provide safety**

- Check whether the place is safe. If you are not sure, do not go. Try to arrange help or speak to people from a safe distance (WHO, IFRC).
- Look for who urgently needs something, such as someone with serious injuries, and who is reacting very strongly (WHO). If someone is physically injured, medical help always comes first (IFRC). See the first aid cards.
- Move the person away from immediate danger if it is safe to do so. Shield them from the media (WHO).

**2. Provide calm and listen**

1. Introduce yourself by name. Ask whether you can help. Approach people respectfully and in keeping with their culture. If possible, find a safe, quiet place. Offer water, for example (WHO).
2. Sit or stand at the same height. If someone is sitting on the ground, kneel (IFRC).
3. Ask what someone needs and what they are worried about, even if it seems obvious (WHO).
4. Stay close. Do not pressure them to talk. Listen if someone wants to tell their story. If someone does not want to talk, quietly staying with them or offering something practical can already be support (WHO).
5. Let people react in their own way and at their own pace. Do not say "stop" or "calm down" when someone is crying or shouting (IFRC 2020).

According to the WHO, listen with your eyes (undivided attention), your ears (truly hearing what is on someone's mind) and your heart (with care and respect).

**Helping someone to calm down**

- Stay calm yourself. Being calm yourself makes others calm (Rode Kruis-Vlaanderen).
- Speak calmly and softly, in short, clear sentences (WHO, IFRC).
- If it is culturally appropriate, keep some eye contact (WHO).
- Remind them that you are there to help, and that they are safe if that is true (WHO).
- If someone feels unreal or disconnected from their surroundings, ask them to put their feet on the floor and feel it, to tap their fingers on their lap, or to say what they see and hear that is not distressing (WHO).
- Help them breathe slowly and calmly. Set the example and let the person follow your rhythm (WHO, Gezondheid en Wetenschap).
- Note: chest pain and shortness of breath can also be caused by a heart attack or asthma. If in doubt, get medical help as soon as possible (IFRC).
- Only touch someone if you have asked permission first (IFRC).

**3. Help with practical things**

- See whether someone needs water, clothing or a blanket and provide it as quickly as possible. People who are cold or thirsty find it hard to concentrate on problems (IFRC).
- Help choose what has to be done now and what can wait. Ask how someone coped with difficult situations in the past (WHO).
- Practical help is, for example: fetching someone who can stay with the person, making sure the children are picked up or get something to eat, helping with transport home or to a safe place, accompanying someone to a hospital or another service, or waiting with them until more help arrives (IFRC).
- Give the person a sense of control. Let them do small things themselves and make their own decisions where possible (Gezondheid en Wetenschap, IFRC 2020).
- Only give information that you have and say what you do not know. Say where your information comes from. Keep it simple, repeat it, and preferably give it to a group at once, so that everyone hears the same thing. Say whether and when you will come back with news, and then do come back (WHO).
- Make sure vulnerable people are not overlooked (WHO).

**4. Stay together**

- Keep families together (WHO, Impact).
- Contact with family or friends takes priority, also in person (IFRC 2020). Help people get in touch with their loved ones (WHO).
- Bring those affected together, so that they help each other. For example, ask people to look after older people. Put people who have no family in touch with others from the neighbourhood (WHO).
- Preferably help people in groups that already exist, such as a family or a class (IFRC).
- During a power cut, several people can get together to share light and warmth (Crisis Center).

**5. Keep children with their parents**

- Keep children with their parents, caregivers and loved ones. Do not let them become separated (WHO). Bringing a child to their caregivers takes priority (IFRC 2020).
- If a child is with their parents, first ask their permission before you talk to the child, and help the parents to care for their child themselves (IFRC).
- If a parent is taken away for medical care, keep the children with that parent, or write down where the parent is going, so that they can be reunited (WHO).
- If you find a child on their own: do not leave them alone. Speak at eye level. Write down the child's name and where they come from. Take them to a child protection organisation or emergency service if there is one. If there is none, look for the caregivers or other family who can care for the child yourself (WHO).

More about children further on in this guide.

**6. Grief and loss**

Acknowledge the loss and listen. See the section Grief and loss.

**7. Who needs more help**

Do not leave anyone who is very distressed or could harm themselves alone, and look for help from people nearby or medical help on the spot. See the section Who needs more help immediately.

## What to say and not to say

From the WHO guidelines:

| Do | Do not |
| --- | --- |
| Find a quiet place with few distractions | pressure someone to tell their story |
| Respect privacy and keep the story confidential if appropriate | interrupt or rush someone, for example by looking at your watch or talking fast |
| Stay close, at a distance that suits age, gender and culture | touch someone if you are not sure it is appropriate |
| Show that you are listening, for example by nodding | judge what someone did or feels |
| Be patient and calm | make up things you do not know |
| Give factual information if you have it; be honest about what you do not know | use overly technical words |
| Give information simply and understandably | tell someone else's story, or your own worries |
| Acknowledge feelings and losses | give false promises or false reassurance |
| Acknowledge the person's strengths | act as if you must solve all the problems |
| Allow silence | take away someone's strength and ability to care for themselves; speak negatively about people |

The IFRC adds: do not use your phone while you are talking to someone.

**Ending.** Explain that you are leaving. If someone else is taking over, introduce that person. If you have referred someone, make sure they know what to expect and have the details. Say goodbye in a positive way (WHO).

## Who needs more help immediately

The WHO names people who immediately need more help than psychological first aid:

- People with serious, life-threatening injuries who need urgent medical care.
- People who are so upset that they cannot care for themselves or their children.
- People who may harm themselves.
- People who may harm others.

**What you do**

- Know your limits and call in others: medical staff if they are there, other helpers, people from the neighbourhood, the local authorities, community leaders (WHO).
- Do not leave someone who is seriously upset alone. Keep them safe until the reaction has passed, or until you find help from care providers, local leaders or other people nearby (WHO).
- Physically injured: medical help first (IFRC). See the first aid cards.

**If someone may harm themselves**

- Ask directly whether they are thinking of harming themselves and whether they have a concrete plan. Such a question brings relief and does not give anyone ideas (IFRC).
- Assess how serious it is. The risk is higher with a clear plan (how, where, when), if the means are at hand, after a previous attempt and with the use of alcohol or drugs (Gezondheid en Wetenschap).
- If there is a concrete threat: ensure safety first. Do not leave the person alone. Stay with them until they get the help they need (IFRC, Gezondheid en Wetenschap).
- Remove objects that the person could use to hurt themselves, as far as possible in agreement with them (Gezondheid en Wetenschap).
- Say that you cannot carry the responsibility alone. Ask whom from their surroundings you can call in (Gezondheid en Wetenschap).
- Do not try to persuade them to go on living for the sake of others: that can increase feelings of guilt. Do not promise secrecy (Gezondheid en Wetenschap).
- Look for help from a care provider or other people nearby, and stay until they arrive (WHO, IFRC).

**Aggression.** Do not stay alone with the person: bring in others immediately. Keep your distance and an open posture, do not touch the person, speak calmly, and bring others to safety if necessary (IFRC).

**If life is in danger.** Call 112 or have someone call. If you cannot get through: try another phone or have neighbours or bystanders call. If calling does not work at all, ask for help in your neighbourhood. Stay with the person yourself and send someone else to get help (see Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call).

## What helps during and after an emergency

The BBK, the WHO and the Crisis Center largely mention the same things.

| Helps | Source |
| --- | --- |
| Keeping in touch with others and sharing experiences | BBK, WHO |
| Keeping up daily routines and making a plan for the day | BBK |
| Going outside where possible | BBK |
| Moving, exercising, physical activity | BBK, WHO, Crisis Center |
| Taking time to withdraw, rest and take breaks | BBK |
| Seeking distraction; doing things that also help with ordinary stress | BBK |
| Using relaxation techniques; relaxing activities such as walking, singing, praying, playing with children | BBK, WHO |
| If possible, sleeping enough, drinking enough and eating regularly | BBK, WHO, Crisis Center |
| Not following more news than necessary; fixed times for news | BBK |
| Discussing problems with someone you trust | WHO |
| Helping others in a safe way and taking part in the community | WHO |

**What you had better avoid (WHO)**

- Drugs, smoking or alcohol.
- Sleeping all day.
- Working all the time without rest or relaxation.
- Isolating yourself from friends and loved ones.
- Neglecting your basic hygiene.
- Violence.

**Together.** The BBK says that the feeling of not being alone with your fears can already help. Do not only look at the damage, but plan and discuss the next steps together. Helping each other gives the feeling of doing something meaningful, which makes you feel less helpless. The Red Cross brochure puts it this way: coming to terms with it together works better than alone. Seek contact with people who went through the same thing.

## Sleep and daily routine

- If possible, make sure you get enough sleep (Crisis Center, BBK, MCF). The WHO recommends resting enough, but not sleeping all day.
- Keep up daily routines and make a plan for the day (BBK). For children: keep the daily routine as much as possible, because familiar habits give something to hold on to (Crisis Center).
- Set fixed times for following the news (BBK).
- Insomnia and nightmares are common reactions (WHO). If they persist, see the section When to seek more help later.

For practical help with sleeping warm without heating, see the guide on heat and cooling.

## Talking with children about emergencies

Children quickly notice when something is wrong. They hear the news, overhear conversations or sense that adults are worried. They deserve extra attention before, during and after an emergency (Crisis Center).

**What the Belgian brochure recommends**

1. First create a calm atmosphere. Children pick up on what adults feel.
2. Listen to their questions and feelings. Take their worries seriously. Say that it is normal to be afraid, sad, angry or unsure. You may also say how you feel.
3. Speak simply and be honest. Give explanations that suit their age. If you do not know something, say so, and look for answers together from reliable sources.
4. Explain who they can turn to for help, so that they understand that they are never on their own.
5. Practise simple safety rules in a playful way.
6. Keep the daily routine as much as possible.
7. Involve children in the emergency plan and the emergency kit. Let them choose a small toy or cuddly toy for the kit.

**What the BBK and the MCF add**

- Every child reacts differently. Some children have trouble concentrating or have nightmares, some at first do not react noticeably at all, others seem anxious or irritable. Show understanding (BBK).
- Offer to do something together. Some children express themselves better by drawing or writing; younger children work through their feelings in their play (BBK).
- Listen carefully when your child asks questions. Answer openly, honestly, factually, simply and clearly. Do not force a conversation if the child does not want to talk (BBK).
- If you are upset yourself, explain why you are worried or sad. That way the child understands that it is normal (BBK).
- Give positive messages: we are looking after you, we are here for you, we are well prepared (BBK).
- Only talk about verified information and avoid unnecessary details. Plan activities with the child to shift their attention (MCF).

## Children during and after a disaster, by age

**What you may see (WHO)**

| Age | Possible reactions |
| --- | --- |
| Young children | falling back into earlier behaviour such as bedwetting or thumb-sucking; clinging to their caregivers; playing less, or repeatedly playing the same game about the event |
| Schoolchildren | believing that they are to blame for what happened; new fears; being less affectionate; feeling alone; being preoccupied with protecting or rescuing people |
| Adolescents | feeling nothing; feeling different from or isolated from friends; risk-taking behaviour and negative attitudes |

Children can also have the same reactions as adults. In general, children cope better when a stable, calm adult is with them (WHO).

**What you do (WHO)**

| Age | What caregivers can do |
| --- | --- |
| Babies | keep them warm and safe; keep them away from loud noise and chaos; cuddle them; if possible, regular feeding and sleeping times; speak calmly and softly |
| Young children | extra time and attention; repeat often that they are safe; explain that it is not their fault; do not separate them from caregivers, brothers, sisters and loved ones; regular routines; simple answers without frightening details; let them stay close to you when they are afraid; be patient with regression; let them play and relax |
| Older children and adolescents | give time and attention; help them keep up routines; explain what happened and what is happening now; allow sadness and do not expect them to be brave; listen without judging; clear rules and expectations; discuss what dangers there are and how they can avoid them; give them opportunities to help |

**Protecting (WHO)**

- Keep children together with their caregivers and family. Do not let them become separated.
- Protect them from gruesome sights, such as injured people or severe destruction, and from distressing stories.
- Protect them from the media and from people who want to interview them and who are not part of the response.
- Speak calmly, softly and kindly, at their eye level, with words they understand.

**What the Red Cross brochure adds**

- Make sure your child can turn to you or another adult.
- Answer honestly and clearly, and keep it rather short. You may say that you do not know something.
- Do not force a child to talk. Re-enacting what happened in play is normal, as long as it stays safe.

Source: [WHO: Psychological first aid](https://psychiatry.uw.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/1.-WHO_PFA_2011.pdf) · [Rode Kruis-Vlaanderen: How do you cope with a shocking event?](https://www.rodekruis.be/storage/main/2018-031-brochure-schokkende-gebeurtenissen-lr.pdf)

## People with a disability, dementia or a mental health condition

- With dementia: do not leave the person alone; even someone who does not normally wander off may do so in an unfamiliar environment. Take a pillow, blanket or other familiar object. In the shelter, find a quiet corner away from the exits. If the person becomes agitated, respond to the emotion they are expressing (Ready.gov).
- For people who have difficulty with an unfamiliar or chaotic environment: noise-cancelling headphones, a small pop-up tent or sheets to limit stimuli, familiar snacks, charged devices with videos and activities (Ready.gov).
- The WHO names people with severe mental health conditions, vulnerable older people, pregnant women and people with visual or hearing impairments as groups that may need extra help to get to a safe place, to be protected and to receive care.
- Help someone with a condition or disability to a safe place. Ask about their condition and medication. Stay with them, or make sure someone else stays with them if you have to leave (WHO).

Source: [Ready.gov: People with Disabilities](https://www.ready.gov/disability) · [WHO: Psychological first aid](https://psychiatry.uw.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/1.-WHO_PFA_2011.pdf)

## Grief and loss

- Sadness and grief are among the normal reactions to a crisis (WHO). Everyone grieves differently and there is no fixed timetable. Most people adapt over time (IFRC).
- Acknowledge the loss and say that you are concerned (Gezondheid en Wetenschap). Acknowledge a person's feelings and losses, such as the loss of a home or the death of a loved one. The WHO gives as an example: say that you are sorry and that you can imagine it is very sad.
- Give time and space. Listen without judging and let the person tell it in their own way. Do not look for solutions too quickly (Gezondheid en Wetenschap, Rode Kruis-Vlaanderen).
- Do not tell someone how they ought to feel. For example, do not say: you should be glad you survived (WHO).
- Small gestures can offer support, such as a bowl of soup. Ask what you can do and keep your promises (Gezondheid en Wetenschap, Rode Kruis-Vlaanderen).
- If someone does not want to talk, respect that, but keep in touch. Encourage them to talk with people who are close to them (Gezondheid en Wetenschap).
- Help people get in touch with loved ones and social support (WHO). Get in touch again after a few weeks and months: support from the people around them decreases after a few weeks, while those affected still need it then (Rode Kruis-Vlaanderen, Impact).
- Young children do not always understand yet that someone who dies does not come back, and may blame themselves. Explain that it is not their fault (IFRC, WHO).
- If you have lost track of someone after a disaster, the Restoring Family Links service of Rode Kruis-Vlaanderen can help search (see the guide on evacuation).
- If you keep worrying or need to talk to someone, seek support from people you trust. See also When to seek more help later.

## Looking after yourself when you help others

The WHO gives helpers, including volunteers and neighbours, this advice:

- Consider honestly whether you are ready to help, given your own health and any personal or family concerns.
- Think about what helped you to keep going in the past.
- Take time to eat, rest and relax, even if only briefly.
- Keep reasonable working hours; share the work, work in shifts and rest regularly.
- You are not responsible for solving all the problems. Help people to help themselves.
- Limit alcohol, caffeine and nicotine.
- Ask other helpers how they are doing and let them do the same for you.
- Talk with friends, loved ones or people you trust.
- Afterwards, take time to rest and reflect. Acknowledge what you were able to do, even in small things, and the limits of what was possible.

The IFRC adds: watch for your own stress signals and accept your own reactions. Being part of a team, keeping your daily routines and being able to let go of the work protect you. In disasters you come across death more often; take your own well-being into account then as well.

## When to seek more help later

- If you keep worrying or need to talk to someone, do not hesitate to seek help (Crisis Center).
- The WHO gives helpers these signals: if after the disaster you have distressing thoughts or memories, feel very nervous or sad, sleep badly, or drink a lot of alcohol or take drugs, seek support from someone you trust. If the complaints last longer than a month, speak to a care provider.
- The WHO also says that people with severe or long-lasting stress reactions may need more support than psychological first aid, especially if they can no longer function in daily life or are a danger to themselves or others.
- If the reactions to a loss are unchanged about six months later, it may be prolonged grief and specialised help is advisable (IFRC).
- If you are very worried about your child, seek advice from the paediatrician or a care professional (BBK).
