When you put together your emergency kit, the Crisis Center explicitly asks you to take into account the medication you use every day. The BBK explains why: in a crisis, ambulance services, hospitals and pharmacies may be temporarily unavailable. This guide is about your own medicines and aids, and about caring for people who depend on others or on devices that run on electricity.

This guide gives no dosages. Discuss any change to your treatment with your doctor or pharmacist. About iodine tablets in a nuclear incident: only take them when recommended by the authorities. See the nuclear guides.

## How much to keep in stock

| Source | Supply of regular medicines |
| --- | --- |
| National Crisis Center (Belgium) | a reserve supply for a minimum of 72 hours in your emergency kit; essential medication for at least three days in the grab bag |
| BBK (Germany) | if you regularly need medicines, a supply for at least ten days |
| MCF (Sweden) | if you need prescription medicines or disposable supplies, for example for diabetes, a supply for one month |
| Ready.gov (United States) | prescription medicines for several days |

**How to build up a supply**

- Discuss with your doctor or pharmacist how to build up an emergency supply (Ready.gov, FDA).
- Know how much you have at home. If you expect the pharmacy to become hard to reach, ask for a new supply in good time (FDA).
- Pay attention to the shelf life and the storage instructions (BBK).
- Ask your doctor or pharmacist whether your medicines may have an adverse effect in hot weather (Crisis Center, heatwave).

## The medication list

Ask your GP or pharmacist for a medication list and keep it with your important documents (Crisis Center). The BBK speaks of a medication plan.

What belongs on the list according to the FDA and Ready.gov:

- The name and strength of each medicine.
- What it is for.
- How much you take and when.
- Your conditions or diagnosis.
- Medical supplies you need.
- Allergies.

Also keep a copy of your prescriptions with your emergency supply (FDA), and copies of your insurance cards (Ready.gov). The BBK recommends an emergency card with information about illnesses, allergies, aids and emergency contacts; many smartphones allow a digital emergency card that you can also open from a locked screen. Ready.gov recommends medical identification, such as a bracelet.

## Storage

- Put bottles and packs in waterproof containers, such as plastic boxes with lids, and store them high up if there is a risk of flooding (FDA).
- Organise and protect your prescription medicines, your over-the-counter products and your vitamins (Ready.gov).
- Keep a cool box and cold packs or ice ready for medicines that must be kept refrigerated (Ready.gov, FDA).

## The home medicine cabinet

**Belgian list (Crisis Center).** A basic first aid kit: bandages, plasters, compresses, scissors, tweezers, safety pins, painkillers, antiseptics. In addition, tissues, wet wipes, toilet paper, sanitary towels, disinfectant gel and iodine tablets (free at the pharmacy).

**German list (BBK)**

- Personal medicines.
- Painkillers and fever reducers.
- A clinical thermometer.
- Remedies for diarrhoea, vomiting and nausea.
- Remedies for cold symptoms.
- Electrolytes to replace lost fluids.
- Antiseptic for wounds and for the skin.
- Plasters and dressings, a burn dressing.
- Disposable gloves.
- A cold pack.
- Remedies for sunburn and insect bites.
- An anti-swelling gel for minor sports injuries.
- Nose drops or nasal spray for congestion.
- Tweezers and scissors.
- Burn, wound and healing ointment.

Ready.gov also mentions antacids for heartburn and laxatives, and spare glasses and contact lens solution.

Discuss with your pharmacist which products to keep at home and how much.

## Insulin without power

The FDA gives these guidelines. They are based on the labels of the US manufacturers. Always follow the package leaflet of your own product and your doctor's instructions.

**Normal storage.** In the fridge, about 2 to 8 °C (36 to 46 °F). Unopened and stored in this way, insulin remains effective until the expiry date.

**Without a fridge**

- Insulin in the manufacturer's vials or cartridges, opened or unopened, may be kept out of the fridge for up to 28 days at 15 to 30 °C (59 to 86 °F) and will still work.
- Insulin that has been diluted or removed from the original vial should be thrown away within two weeks.
- Keep insulin as cool as possible.
- If you use ice, do not let the insulin freeze. Do not use insulin that has been frozen.
- Keep insulin away from direct heat and sunlight.
- Extreme temperatures make insulin less effective. The longer it is exposed to them, the less it works, which makes it harder to control your blood sugar. In emergency conditions you may have to use insulin that was stored above 30 °C after all.
- Some products have a different storage time out of the fridge. As an example, the FDA mentions an insulin given once a week that may be stored for up to 12 weeks below 30 °C. Check the package leaflet of your own product.

**Insulin pump**

- Throw away insulin in the infusion set (reservoir, tubing, catheter) after 48 hours, unless the instructions for your pump or your insulin say otherwise.
- If the insulin in the pump was exposed to more than 37 °C (98.6 °F), throw it away.

**Afterwards.** As soon as you have correctly stored insulin again, throw away vials that were exposed to extreme temperatures and replace them as soon as possible.

**Switching to another insulin product** must, according to the FDA, always be done under a doctor's supervision, with close monitoring of blood sugar. This guide gives no instructions on this.

Source: [FDA: Information Regarding Insulin Storage and Switching Between Products in an Emergency](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/emergency-preparedness-drugs/information-regarding-insulin-storage-and-switching-between-products-emergency)

## Other medicines that must be kept cool

**Beforehand**

- Keep a cool box and ice or frozen cool packs ready and follow the storage instructions on the label (FDA).
- Ask your doctor how long your medicine may be stored at a higher temperature, and ask for specific guidelines for medicines that are vital (Ready.gov).

**After an interruption: the sources differ**

- The FDA: if a medicine was not stored at the recommended temperature, check the storage instructions or ask a pharmacist, doctor or the manufacturer whether you may still use it. If the medicine is vital, such as insulin, and no replacement is immediately available, you may use it until you have a new supply. Replace it as soon as possible.
- Ready.gov: if the power was off for more than a day, throw away medicines that must be refrigerated, unless the label says otherwise, and ask your doctor or pharmacist immediately for a new supply.

Both sources ask you to seek advice from your pharmacist or doctor as soon as possible. According to the FDA, you may continue to use a vital medicine until you have a replacement.

Source: [FDA: Safe Drug Use After a Natural Disaster](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/emergency-preparedness-drugs/safe-drug-use-after-natural-disaster) · [Ready.gov: Power Outages](https://www.ready.gov/power-outages)

## Medicines after a fire or flood

The FDA:

- High temperatures in a fire can make medicines less effective or destroy them. Consider replacing them.
- Medicines that came into contact with floodwater or possibly contaminated water may become unsafe. Throw them away, even if they were in their original packaging with a screw cap, snap lid or dropper.
- If you need such a medicine for a life-threatening condition and no replacement is immediately available, you may use it if it looks unchanged. Replace it as soon as possible.
- Do not use a medicine that is wet or otherwise changed. It is contaminated.
- Medicines that you have to make up with water should only be mixed with purified water or bottled water. Do not use any other liquid.

Source: [FDA: Safe Drug Use After a Natural Disaster](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/emergency-preparedness-drugs/safe-drug-use-after-natural-disaster)

## Devices that run on electricity: home oxygen, ventilation and others

**Beforehand**

- Discuss with your care providers what to do if important electrical machines break down, such as an oxygen machine or an electric wheelchair (Crisis Center).
- Ask your doctor or care provider what you can do to keep a medical device at home working during a power cut (Ready.gov).
- Ask your energy supplier whether you can be put on a list for priority when power is restored (Ready.gov).
- Keep a reserve supply of oxygen (Ready.gov).
- Make a list of the type and serial number of your medical devices, with any operating instructions (Ready.gov).
- Buy an extra battery for battery-powered devices and keep them charged (Ready.gov).
- Do not wait for a power cut. Find out how long the fully charged internal battery of your device lasts. If that is shorter than the interruption could last, you will need to be looked after somewhere else: look into those options in advance (FPS Economy).
- If you use a device intermittently, as with home dialysis, make sure it is not connected at the time of an announced load-shedding cut (FPS Economy).

**What the BBK says about home nursing care.** During a power cut, devices used in home nursing care that may be vital, such as ventilators, can fail. Services such as personal alarm systems and meals on wheels may be disrupted or stop. If you use such services, ask whether and how they will continue during a longer power cut.

See also the guide on light and energy for batteries, power banks and generators, and below for what to do if you cannot call your care provider.

## Dialysis and other treatments at a centre

- If you are on dialysis or another vital treatment, know the location and availability of more than one place that can help you (Ready.gov).
- If you regularly receive treatment at a clinic or hospital, ask about their emergency plan and find a back-up address together (Ready.gov, older adults).
- Plan adapted transport in advance, in case you cannot get to your treatment yourself in a disaster (Ready.gov).

The Belgian sources consulted give no separate guidelines for dialysis. Ask your dialysis centre about its own emergency procedure.

## If you cannot call your care provider

During a power cut, mobile masts last one to two hours, mobile internet drops out quickly and most landlines stop working (FPS Economy). Personal alarm systems can also fail (BBK). If you depend on oxygen, dialysis, home ventilation or another device, arrange in advance what will happen if nobody can be reached.

**Agree in advance**

- Discuss with your care providers what to do if your device breaks down (Crisis Center). Also agree how you will reach each other in an emergency and think of alternatives if you have no phone or internet (Crisis Center).
- If you use home nursing care or a personal alarm system, ask whether and how those services will continue during a long power cut (BBK).
- If you receive treatment at a clinic or hospital, ask about their emergency plan and find a back-up address together (Ready.gov). Know the location of more than one place that can help you (Ready.gov).
- Find out how long the battery of your device lasts and where else you could be looked after (FPS Economy).
- Ask your municipality what arrangements it has made for a power cut, for example an information and reception point for the public (FPS Economy). In Germany, the BBK recommends asking the municipality where you can report an emergency if the telephone network fails.
- Agree with your neighbours how you will reach each other without phone or internet, and who needs extra help (Crisis Center, plan for your neighbourhood).
- Make sure at least one person in your support network has a key, knows where your supply is and knows how to operate your life-saving devices (Ready.gov). See below under people who depend on care.

**On paper**

- A list with the name, address and telephone number of your care providers and contacts. That way you can find each other again, even if your mobile is flat (Crisis Center).
- A list of the nearest medical facilities and hospitals and of adapted transport (Ready.gov).
- The type and serial number of your devices, with the operating instructions (Ready.gov), and your medication list.
- Keep your contact list in waterproof packaging with your emergency kit (Ready.gov).

**When it happens and you cannot get through**

1. Follow the arrangements you made in advance.
2. Ask for help in your neighbourhood (FPS Economy), from neighbours, family and the people in your support network (Ready.gov). The BBK recommends talking to your neighbours: they may need help or be able to help you.
3. Follow the information from the authorities on a battery radio or the car radio (FPS Economy). The municipality can open an information and reception point for the public (FPS Economy).
4. If the battery of a life-sustaining device lasts less long than the interruption, you will need to be looked after somewhere else (FPS Economy). Go to the place you agreed in advance.
5. If there is a danger to life: call 112 or have someone call. If that does not work, see Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call. The map in the app (Nearest button) shows hospitals, fire stations, police and town halls.

Good to know: general and psychiatric hospitals are among the priority users. During load shedding, they are in principle not cut off, or are reconnected with priority. Residential care homes and care centres are not among them (FPS Economy). In Germany, the BBK recommends that, if the emergency numbers do not work, in an emergency you go to the nearest police station or hospital if possible.

## If your medication runs out

**Normal operation: the pharmacy on duty.** Outside opening hours, in Belgium you can always find a pharmacy on duty nearby (APB, apotheek.be):

| When | On-duty service |
| --- | --- |
| Weekdays | from 19:00 to 9:00 |
| Saturday | from 13:00 to 9:00 the next morning |
| Sundays and public holidays | from 9:00 to 9:00 the next morning |

- **Without internet.** Every pharmacy displays an on-duty list with the name, address and phone number of the pharmacies on duty nearby. You can read it from outside. So go to the nearest pharmacy first.
- **If the network works.** You can then also look up the pharmacy on duty on apotheek.be. In some regions, after 22:00 you have to call a free phone number to find out which pharmacy is on duty.
- At night, many pharmacists serve customers through a night hatch.
- With a prescription, the pharmacist may not charge an on-duty surcharge; without a prescription, they may ask a reasonable surcharge that is visibly displayed.

**The GP on duty** can be reached on 1733 (Crisis Center), if the network works. Write the number down in advance on your paper list.

**In a shortage of medicines** (Crisis Center)

- Ask your GP or pharmacist for advice about your treatment.
- As preparation, while the internet works: register with Farmastatus to receive automatic emails when the availability of a medicine changes, and read what you can do yourself on farmainfo.be.
- The Federal Agency for Medicines and Health Products (FAMHP) monitors the supply; manufacturers must report unavailability.

**If care is temporarily unavailable.** See the section If you cannot call your care provider. In Germany, the BBK writes: if the emergency numbers no longer work, in an emergency go to the nearest police station or hospital if possible. The WHO lists care for chronic conditions among the things people often need after a disaster (see the guide on evacuation).

**Consultation.** For insulin, the FDA explicitly says that switching to another product should only be done under a doctor's supervision.

## People who depend on care

**At home**

- Prepare to care for family members who need nursing care yourself for a while if necessary (BBK).
- Build a support network of family, friends and others who can help. Make sure at least one person has a key to the home, knows where the emergency supply is and knows how to operate life-saving devices or administer medicines (Ready.gov, older adults).
- Agree on how you will reach each other in an emergency, even if phone and internet are not working (Crisis Center). People around you can register your address in BE-Alert, so that they also receive a message for as long as the network works (Crisis Center).
- If you receive help from the municipality or home care, talk with your family, neighbours, assistants or care coordinator about what help you will need, and make a plan with your medicines and aids (MCF).

**In a residential care home.** Ask about emergency power planning or a connection for emergency power (BBK).

**In hot weather.** People with a spinal cord injury, severe mental health conditions whose medication interferes with cooling, or dementia are at extra risk. Ask your doctor how heat affects your medicines, and have someone visit regularly (Ready.gov). See the guide on heat and cooling.

**For those responsible for a school, company or care institution**, the Crisis Center recommends a basic first aid kit and a supply of iodine tablets, which organisations can collect free of charge from the pharmacy.

## Checklist

- [ ] Supply of regular medicines for at least 72 hours, preferably longer, discussed with your doctor or pharmacist.
- [ ] Medication for at least three days in the grab bag.
- [ ] Medication list from your GP or pharmacist, with your documents.
- [ ] Copy of your prescriptions.
- [ ] Medicines in waterproof boxes, stored high up.
- [ ] Cool box and cool packs for refrigerated medicines.
- [ ] Package leaflet of your insulin read for storage out of the fridge.
- [ ] Plan for every medical device that runs on electricity, discussed with your care provider, including for when you cannot call.
- [ ] Battery life of every life-sustaining device known, and a place where you can go otherwise.
- [ ] On paper: care providers and contacts with address and telephone number, and the nearest hospitals.
- [ ] Spare batteries and chargers for aids.
- [ ] For dialysis or another treatment: a second address and the centre's emergency plan.
- [ ] Knowing where to find the pharmacy on duty, including without internet.
- [ ] The number of the GP on duty (1733) on your paper list.
