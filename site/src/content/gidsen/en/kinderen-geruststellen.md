Children quickly notice that something is wrong. They overhear conversations and sense the worries of adults. What helps them most is a calm adult nearby, staying with their family, honest explanations at their level, and a day that is as much like an ordinary day as possible. Most children recover within a few weeks after a distressing event.

This guide is about children. Your own stress and psychological first aid for adults are covered in the guide on mental resilience and psychological first aid.

## What helps children most

1. **A calm adult.** Children take on emotions. Stay as calm as you can, and pay attention to your voice and your body language. With young children in particular, how the parent reacts matters.
2. **Staying together.** Keep children with their parents, brothers and sisters. Separate them as little as possible. If you have to, make sure they know where you are and when you will be back.
3. **Showing safety.** Say often that you will stay with them and look after them. Give extra attention, cuddles and time.
4. **Honest, short explanations.** A child's imagination is often worse than reality. Staying silent does not make it better.
5. **A fixed routine.** Fixed times for getting up, eating, playing, learning and sleeping. Familiar habits give something to hold on to.
6. **Playing and moving.** Play is how children process things. Make time for it every day.
7. **Being able to do something.** Let children help, such as laying the table, carrying water or reading to a younger child. That gives them the feeling that they can do something.
8. **Away from distressing images and stories.** Do not let children keep seeing the same images or hearing adults talk about it.

## Talking about the situation

**Before the conversation**

- Choose a calm moment, for example at the table. Not just before bedtime.
- First ask what the child already knows or has heard. Young children sometimes find it easier to tell you through a drawing or a story.
- Very young children who have not noticed anything do not need to be told about it.

**During the conversation**

- Say in simple words what has happened and what is happening now. Also say what may still happen, without frightening details.
- Be honest. "I don't know" is allowed. Then say what you do know or what you think.
- Correct wrong information. Do not brush worries aside, even if they seem exaggerated.
- Let the child repeat your explanation in their own words. That way you know whether they have understood you.
- You may say that you are worried or sad too, and why. That way the child understands that these feelings are normal. Do not let them overwhelm you. If that does not work, come back to it later.
- Talk about the people who are helping: emergency workers, neighbours, volunteers.
- Avoid words such as "bad people" about a group. Watch out for bullying.
- Do not keep a death secret. That damages trust.

**After the conversation**

- End with care. Pay attention to how the child looks, speaks and breathes. Say that they can always come to you.
- Do not force a conversation. A child sometimes asks one question and then wants to go straight back to playing. That is normal.
- Come back to it. A fixed moment, for example once a week, when children can ask questions, helps.
- Give information in advance and in small pieces rather than all at once.

## By age

| Age | What you say and do |
| --- | --- |
| Babies | keep them warm and safe, away from noise and bustle; carry and cuddle them often; fixed feeding and sleeping times if possible; speak calmly and softly |
| Toddlers and pre-schoolers (1 to 6 years) | very simple explanations, without details; repeat often that they are safe and that it is not their fault; keep them close when they are afraid; fixed routines; patience if they fall back into younger behaviour; lots of play |
| Primary school children (6 to 12 years) | explain what happened and what is happening now; say that it is not their fault; answer questions honestly; give small tasks; let drawing, writing or play help them express what they feel |
| Teenagers (12 to 18 years) | talk honestly and as with an adult; ask what they have heard or read; allow sadness without expecting them to be brave; listen without judging; clear agreements; discuss what dangers there are and how they can avoid them; give real responsibility; allow them to talk with people their own age |

## Recognising stress

Children react in different ways. Many reactions are normal after a distressing event and pass by themselves.

| Age | Possible signs |
| --- | --- |
| 0 to 3 years | clinging more; falling back into younger behaviour; sleeping or eating differently; irritable, restless or anxious; crying more often |
| 4 to 6 years | clinging; falling back, such as bedwetting or thumb-sucking; sleeping or eating differently; anxious, less able to concentrate; quieter or, on the contrary, more restless; stopping playing or talking; taking on an adult role |
| 7 to 12 years | withdrawing; worrying about others; sleeping or eating differently; anxious, irritable or aggressive; poorer memory and concentration; physical complaints; talking about the event all the time or acting it out; feeling guilty |
| 13 to 17 years | intense sadness; excessive concern for others; guilt and shame; resisting authority; taking more risks; aggression; hopelessness; feeling different from friends |

**Physical**, at any age: tiredness, stomach ache, headache, dizziness, shaking, shortness of breath. This can also point to an illness. Have it checked by a doctor or nurse if possible.

**Serious signs.** A child who is almost always very quiet or withdrawn, hides, no longer responds or speaks, is constantly extremely worried, wants to hurt others, or is confused, needs more help (see the end of this guide).

## What to do about stress

- **Name what you see.** Wait until the child is a little calmer. Say what you notice, also with a quiet child: "You're quiet today."
- **Help them calm down with belly breathing.** Breathe slowly together five times: count to five as you breathe in and to five as you breathe out. Let the child imagine that their belly is a balloon that fills up and empties. Make a game of it for young children, for example once every hour together.
- **A safe place in your head.** Imagine a calm place together, such as a beach or a forest. Ask what the child sees, hears and smells there.
- **Acting it out is allowed.** Children play out what they went through. That is normal, as long as it stays safe.
- **Drawing, writing and playing.** Many children do not yet have words for what they feel. A drawing or a story helps. So does reading aloud from a book about feelings.
- **Anger with limits.** Anger and unreasonable behaviour are normal. Stay calm and say what is and is not allowed: being angry is allowed, throwing things is not.
- **Patience with regression.** Bedwetting, thumb-sucking or no longer wanting to sleep alone usually pass by themselves. Do not punish it.
- **Do something nice together.** A game, a walk, cooking together.

## With loud noises, sheltering or leaving

- **Explain in advance.** Say that there may be loud noises and what you will do then: go to a safe place and stay there. Practise it as a game, just like the other steps of the family emergency plan.
- **While waiting.** Keep the child close to you. Talk about happy memories, sing softly or read together. Do not sit in silence listening to the noise.
- **If you have to leave.** Explain the steps in order: first this, then that. Let the child bring their own cuddly toy or toy. Involve children in the emergency kit beforehand: let them choose something to put in it themselves.
- **In a new place.** Set up fixed habits as quickly as possible: a fixed place to sleep, a fixed time for eating and for playing.

## Keeping a daily routine

A more or less predictable day, with clear agreements, is a basic need of a child. Fixed times for sleeping and eating connect the child with life before the emergency.

- **The same start and end every day.** Getting up, getting dressed, having breakfast together and going over the day. In the evening, the same ritual in the same order.
- **A daily plan you can see.** Draw or write the day on a sheet of paper: eating, playing, learning, outside, rest. Young children understand drawings better than clock times.
- **Learning and playing.** Plan time for learning, for safe play and for relaxation. See the guide on home schooling without electricity for a daily routine by age.
- **Tasks.** Give each child a fixed small task.
- **News.** Keep the news away from young children. Follow it yourself at fixed times, not all day.
- **Weekly rituals.** A fixed games time, a story on Sunday, a moment to ask questions.

**Sleeping**

- Wind the evening down calmly: little light, a soft song, a story, a cuddly toy. Do the same things in the same order every evening.
- Keep the same bedtime as before, as far as possible.
- A cuddly toy or a familiar object helps a child fall asleep. The bed is a place of rest, never of punishment.
- Plenty of movement during the day helps a child sleep in the evening.
- After a move or a distressing event, children often sleep worse for a while. Comfort them and stay nearby.
- Guideline values, naps included: babies from 4 to 11 months 12 to 16 hours, toddlers from 1 to 2 years 11 to 14 hours, pre-schoolers from 3 to 5 years 10 to 13 hours. Above all, look at your own child.

## In a reception centre

- Stay together as a family. Ask for places next to each other.
- Make your own corner with familiar things: a blanket, a cuddly toy, a photo.
- Keep your own habits from home, even in a busy room.
- Ask whether there is a safe, supervised space for children, or help set one up with other parents. How to do that is explained in the guide on games and activities without electricity.
- Ask children what they need and what they want to do.
- **A child without parents.** Never leave the child alone. Report it immediately to the person in charge of the centre or to the emergency services. Do not try to trace the family yourself if there is a service that does this.

## Looking after yourself as a parent

A stressed parent reads a child's signals less well, and the child takes on the unrest. Looking after yourself is therefore also looking after your child.

- Take a short moment for yourself now and then, and pay attention to your breathing.
- If you feel anger rising, take a ten-second pause before you react.
- Talk with another adult you trust. Accept help from others.
- Look for distraction, for example a game with your children.

## When a child needs more help

Seek help from a GP, a nurse or the care workers at the reception centre if:

- the reactions do not lessen after a few weeks;
- you see the serious signs described above;
- the child went through something very distressing, such as the loss of a parent or violence;
- you are very worried, or can no longer look after your children yourself.
