An emergency supply is what you keep at home to get through a period without shops, electricity, gas or tap water. It is something different from the grab bag, which you take with you if you have to leave (see the guide on the emergency kit and grab bag). This guide is about what stays at home.

Source: [National Crisis Center: An emergency kit at home](https://crisiscenter.be/en/what-can-you-do/build-emergency-kit/emergency-kit-home)

## How long: 72 hours as a minimum

The National Crisis Center says that throughout the European Union it is recommended to have an emergency kit at home that lets you manage on your own for at least 3 days (72 hours). Other countries go further.

| Country and source | Period |
| --- | --- |
| Belgium, National Crisis Center | at least 72 hours |
| Netherlands, Denk vooruit | the first 72 hours |
| Germany, BBK | preferably 10 days; at least 3 days already helps a lot |
| Sweden, MCF | at least one week |
| United States, Ready.gov | several days |

Why 72 hours: Denk vooruit explains that in the first three days of an emergency a lot is still unclear and the emergency services cannot be everywhere at once. During that time the authorities can organise help. The Crisis Center adds that people who are prepared give the emergency services room to help the most vulnerable people first.

How to start: the BBK recommends starting with 3 days and building up step by step. This guide therefore gives quantities for 72 hours and for 2 weeks.

## Water

The Crisis Center asks for 3 to 10 litres per person per day. An adult drinks 1.5 to 2 litres per day. 3 litres is just enough for drinking and cooking; the rest is for washing and flushing the toilet.

| Household | 72 hours, minimum (3 litres) | 72 hours, generous (10 litres) | 2 weeks, minimum (3 litres) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 person | 9 litres | 30 litres | 42 litres |
| 2 people | 18 litres | 60 litres | 84 litres |
| 4 people | 36 litres | 120 litres | 168 litres |

This table multiplies the Belgian advice per person per day by the number of days and people.

Other sources for comparison: Denk vooruit and the MCF say at least 3 litres per person per day; the BBK 2 litres per person per day, of which 0.5 litres for cooking; the German Bundeszentrum für Ernährung counts 2 litres of drinks per day for people aged 65 and over and 1 litre of drinks per day for children up to 12 years (not infants). How to store water is in the guide on water.

Other drinks also count. For an interruption of the water supply, the Crisis Center also mentions soft drinks and juice. The MCF mentions coffee, tea, hot chocolate, juice and milk.

## What kind of food

The Crisis Center asks for food that keeps for a long time and that you do not need water, gas or electricity to prepare. Choose sufficiently varied and nutritious food that you like. Do not forget the tin opener.

The BBK gives four questions for choosing your supply:

1. **Long-life.** Food that does not need to be refrigerated does not spoil during a power cut.
2. **Ready to eat.** Food that does not need to be cooked can be eaten if the cooker does not work.
3. **Short cooking time.** Food that needs little energy makes the fuel for your camping stove or barbecue last longer.
4. **For whom.** Allergies, eating habits, young children and pets.

The MCF says: choose food that is filling, gives a lot of energy, can be stored at room temperature, is quick to prepare, needs little water or can be eaten straight away.

## Quantities per person per day

The Crisis Center and Denk vooruit do not give weights. The German Bundeszentrum für Landwirtschaft und Ernährung (BZL) does: its supply calculator assumes 2,200 kilocalories per person per day and gives these quantities per food group.

| Food group | Per person per day | 72 hours (3 days) | 2 weeks (14 days) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grain products, bread, potatoes | 0.33 kg | 1 kg | 4.6 kg |
| Vegetables, pulses, mushrooms | 0.40 kg | 1.2 kg | 5.6 kg |
| Fruit and nuts | 0.25 kg | 0.75 kg | 3.5 kg |
| Milk and dairy products | 0.25 kg | 0.75 kg | 3.5 kg |
| Eggs, meat, sausage and fish | 0.12 kg | 0.36 kg | 1.7 kg |
| Fats and oil | 33 g | 0.1 kg | 0.46 kg |
| Drinks | 2 litres | 6 litres | 28 litres |
| Other (sugar, honey, chocolate, salt, flour, ready meals) | as desired | as desired | as desired |

The columns for 3 and 14 days are the daily values multiplied, as the calculator also calculates them. The source itself says that these are general recommendations, which you should adapt to your own nutritional needs. For vegetables, you may replace beans and peas with other pulses such as chickpeas and lentils. The quantity of drinks includes 1.5 litres for drinking and 0.5 litres for cooking pasta, potatoes and rice.

Examples from the calculator for one person and one day: 71 g packaged wholemeal bread, 71 g crispbread, 54 g rolled oats, 28 g pasta, 18 g rice; tinned vegetables such as beans, peas and carrots, red cabbage, sauerkraut, sweetcorn; tinned fruit, raisins, prunes, hazelnuts.

## Two example lists

These lists combine the Belgian list with the German quantities. They are an example, not a prescription.

**72 hours for 1 adult**

- 9 to 30 litres of water (Crisis Center), of which at least 6 litres for drinking and cooking.
- 1 kg of grain products that you can eat cold: crackers, crispbread, rusks, rolled oats.
- 1.2 kg of tinned vegetables and pulses.
- 0.75 kg of tinned fruit, dried fruit and nuts.
- 0.75 kg of long-life milk or milk powder.
- 0.36 kg of tinned meat or fish.
- A small bottle of oil.
- Energy bars, chocolate, honey or jam as desired.
- Tin opener.

**2 weeks for 1 adult**

- 42 litres of water as a minimum (Crisis Center, 3 litres per day).
- 4.6 kg of grain products, bread and potatoes, including pasta and rice if you can cook.
- 5.6 kg of vegetables and pulses.
- 3.5 kg of fruit and nuts.
- 3.5 kg of milk and dairy products.
- 1.7 kg of eggs, meat, sausage and fish, mainly tinned.
- About half a litre of oil or fat.
- Sugar, salt, herbs and spices, stock, flour and ready meals as desired.
- An alternative means of cooking and fuel (see the guide on food without electricity).

## Babies and young children

- The Crisis Center: a reserve supply of baby food and nappies at home. For those who need extra help it adds: formula powder, disposable nappies and baby wipes, the medicines your children need and extra toys.
- The OFF ON brochure of the Crisis Center recommends vacuum flasks with hot water when a power cut has been announced, for example for baby bottles.
- Ready.gov: use ready-to-feed formula. If you have to make up powder, use bottled water, or boiled water as a last resort. Ready.gov also mentions bottles, nappies, wipes and nappy rash cream.
- The MCF mentions porridge, formula, oatmeal and baby food for children.
- The BZL counts an average of 1 litre of drinks per day for children up to 12 years (not infants).

## Diets, allergies and illness

- The BBK asks: are there allergies or eating habits that your supply needs to suit.
- Ready.gov: take special dietary needs into account and choose food that your family eats.
- The BZL: take into account the age, work and any dietary needs of the members of the household. The source also offers a vegetarian supply table.
- The MCF: if you need prescription medicines or disposable supplies, for example for diabetes, make sure you have a supply for a month. See the guide on medication and care.

## Pets

- The Crisis Center: food, water and bowls, enough for 72 hours.
- The BZL: also cover your pets' needs with a supply of tinned pet food.
- The MCF: make sure you have a supply for at least a week: dry food and water in containers, and the animal's medicines.
- Diereninrampen.nl, to which the Dutch information booklet refers, gives 50 ml per kilogram of body weight per day as a general guideline for drinking water.

## The rest of the supply at home

In addition to food and drink, the Belgian list for at home contains:

- **Health.** A basic first aid kit: bandages, plasters, compresses, scissors, tweezers, safety pins, painkillers, disinfectants. Tissues, wet wipes, toilet paper, sanitary towels and disinfecting gel. Iodine tablets: free of charge from your pharmacy, take them only when the authorities recommend it.
- **Light, heat and electronics.** A torch, preferably a wind-up one with a crank; otherwise spare batteries. A wind-up or battery-powered radio. A lighter or matches. Candles or tealights. Blankets.
- **Children.** Baby food and nappies.
- **Pets.** Food, water and bowls for 72 hours.

The BBK adds an alternative means of cooking (camping stove, gas barbecue) and fuel, sleeping bags, a heat source that works without mains power using fuel, and hygiene products such as soap, toothpaste, bin bags (including large, strong ones to hang in the toilet), kitchen roll, household gloves and a camping toilet. See also the guides on heat, light and energy, hygiene and food without electricity.

Rough guide values from the BZL. For purchased goods, the date on the packaging always applies.

| Product | Storage | Keeps for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tinned vegetables | dry, airy, preferably below 20 °C, glass jars in the dark | 1 to 2 years |
| Tinned fruit, jam, jelly | dry, airy, preferably below 20 °C | 1 year |
| Tinned fish and meat | dry, airy, preferably below 20 °C | several years |
| Dried fruit and dried vegetables | dry, dark, in glass or tins, preferably below 20 °C | 1 year |
| Dried potato products | dry, preferably below 20 °C | 1 to 2 years |
| Semolina, rolled oats, rice, pasta, cornflakes | tightly closing jars or boxes, dry | 1 year |
| Whole grains | dry, air regularly | 2 years |
| Crispbread, rusks | dry | 1 year |
| Fresh wholemeal bread | bread bin | 10 to 12 days |
| Mineral water in glass | dark, cool, dry | usually 2 years on the label |
| Mineral water in PET | dark, cool, dry | usually 1 year on the label |
| Cabbage, leeks, beetroot, carrots | cellar, 1 to 4 °C, humid | 3 to 4 months |
| Apples | not above 5 °C, dark, airy | 3 to 5 months |

## Rotating: a supply you actually use

The Crisis Center recommends a rolling supply:

- Buy one extra product each time you do the shopping.
- Choose foods that you normally use as well.
- If a product is about to expire, use it in your daily meals and replace it later.
- Check regularly that everything is still usable.

Denk vooruit: check the supply every six months and put that in your diary. Agree who gets the supply in and who checks it. At the same time, check whether your situation has changed, for example because of a baby or new medicines.

The BZL gives this method:

1. Keep a supply list and cross off what you use.
2. Always put new stock at the back or at the bottom. Use the older stock first.
3. Write the name, quantity and date on products you have preserved or frozen yourself.
4. Remove rotting fruit and rotting vegetables straight away.
5. Remove mouldy products straight away. If in doubt, do not eat it.
6. Never eat from a bulging tin (bottom or lid curved outwards). The contents are spoiled.
7. If there is no date on a product, write the date of purchase on it.
8. Do not eat the contents of opened jars of home-preserved food any more.

Ready.gov adds: store tins in a cool, dry place and boxed food in tightly closing plastic or metal containers. Replace what has expired and review what your family needs every year.

## Where to store it: house, cellar, flat

**General**

- Keep all items together in a fixed, accessible place and make sure everyone in the house knows where they are (Crisis Center).
- Store water and food in a cool, dark place (Crisis Center) and keep them dry.
- The BZL gives the ideal conditions: store cupboard 15 to 20 °C, up to 70 per cent humidity, ventilated and dark; cellar 4 to 12 °C, 85 to 90 per cent humidity, ventilated and dark.

**House with a cellar.** The cellar is cool and dark, but the Crisis Center warns that cellars and garages are more vulnerable to flooding. So do not store everything there, and certainly not on the floor if you live in a flood-prone area.

**House without a cellar.** Denk vooruit mentions a shed or hall cupboard as a cool and dark place.

**Flat.** The Crisis Center suggests making one joint emergency kit with the residents of a block of flats, student accommodation or other form of shared living instead of one per household unit, for example in a cellar or communal area. Discuss who has the keys as well. High up in a building, your own water supply is especially important: Denk vooruit describes that on the third floor no more water comes out of the tap during a power cut.

**Little space.** The Crisis Center and Denk vooruit say that you do not need to buy an expensive kit and that most items are already in the house. You can share some things with neighbours, such as a radio. The BBK says the same: it can make sense to buy things together with others and share them.

## During a shortage

On a disruption of the food supply, the Crisis Center says:

- Be sparing with food.
- Make sure vulnerable people, for example children and older people, have enough nutritious food.
- Do not hoard. Buy a limited quantity, so that everyone has the chance to buy food.
- Afterwards, supplies will start up again. For some products this may take longer.

The Crisis Center and Denk vooruit recommend providing some extra water and food to share with vulnerable people around you.

## Checklist

- [ ] Water: at least 3 litres per person per day for 72 hours, preferably more for washing and the toilet.
- [ ] Food for 72 hours that you can eat without water, gas or electricity.
- [ ] Tin opener.
- [ ] Baby food, nappies and toys if there are young children.
- [ ] Food, water and bowls for the pets.
- [ ] A supply for anyone who follows a diet or has an allergy.
- [ ] A fixed, dry, cool place, not everything in the cellar.
- [ ] A reminder in the diary to check every six months.
- [ ] After that: expand step by step towards 10 days or 2 weeks.
