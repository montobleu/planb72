An emergency supply is what you keep at home to get through a period without shops, electricity, gas or tap water. It is something different from the grab bag, which you take with you if you have to leave (see the guide on the emergency kit and grab bag). This guide is about what stays at home.

Source: [National Crisis Center: An emergency kit at home](https://crisiscenter.be/en/what-can-you-do/build-emergency-kit/emergency-kit-home)

## How long: 72 hours as a minimum

Make sure you have a supply that lets you manage on your own for at least 3 days (72 hours). This is recommended throughout the European Union. Longer is better.

Why 72 hours: in the first three days of an emergency a lot is still unclear and the emergency services cannot be everywhere at once. During that time the authorities can organise help. People who are prepared give the emergency services room to help the most vulnerable people first.

Start with 3 days and build up step by step. This guide therefore gives quantities for 72 hours and for 2 weeks.

## Water

Count on 3 litres per person per day: enough for drinking and cooking. Of this, an adult drinks 1.5 to 2 litres per day. If you have the space, set aside up to 10 litres per person per day, so that you can also wash yourself and flush the toilet.

| Household | 72 hours, minimum (3 litres) | 72 hours, generous (10 litres) | 2 weeks, minimum (3 litres) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 person | 9 litres | 30 litres | 42 litres |
| 2 people | 18 litres | 60 litres | 84 litres |
| 4 people | 36 litres | 120 litres | 168 litres |

The table multiplies the amount per person per day by the number of days and people. How to store water is in the guide on water.

Other drinks also count, such as soft drinks, juice, milk, coffee, tea and hot chocolate.

## What kind of food

Choose food that keeps for a long time and that you do not need water, gas or electricity to prepare. Choose sufficiently varied and nutritious food that is filling, gives a lot of energy and that you like. Do not forget the tin opener.

Four questions help you choose:

1. **Long-life.** Food that does not need to be refrigerated does not spoil during a power cut.
2. **Ready to eat.** Food that does not need to be cooked can be eaten if the cooker does not work.
3. **Short cooking time.** Food that needs little energy makes the fuel for your camping stove or barbecue last longer.
4. **For whom.** Allergies, eating habits, young children and pets.

## Quantities per person per day

Count on about 2,200 kilocalories per person per day. That comes down to these quantities per food group.

| Food group | Per person per day | 72 hours (3 days) | 2 weeks (14 days) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grain products, bread, potatoes | 0.33 kg | 1 kg | 4.6 kg |
| Vegetables, pulses, mushrooms | 0.40 kg | 1.2 kg | 5.6 kg |
| Fruit and nuts | 0.25 kg | 0.75 kg | 3.5 kg |
| Milk and dairy products | 0.25 kg | 0.75 kg | 3.5 kg |
| Eggs, meat, sausage and fish | 0.12 kg | 0.36 kg | 1.7 kg |
| Fats and oil | 33 g | 0.1 kg | 0.46 kg |
| Water (see above) | 3 litres | 9 litres | 42 litres |
| Other (sugar, honey, chocolate, salt, flour, ready meals) | as desired | as desired | as desired |

The columns for 3 and 14 days are the daily values multiplied. These are general guide values: adapt them to what your family needs. For vegetables, you may replace beans and peas with other pulses such as chickpeas and lentils.

Examples for one person and one day: 71 g packaged wholemeal bread, 71 g crispbread, 54 g rolled oats, 28 g pasta, 18 g rice; tinned vegetables such as beans, peas and carrots, red cabbage, sauerkraut, sweetcorn; tinned fruit, raisins, prunes, hazelnuts.

## Two example lists

An example, not a prescription.

**72 hours for 1 adult**

- 9 litres of water for drinking and cooking, preferably up to 30 litres so that you can also wash yourself and flush the toilet.
- 1 kg of grain products that you can eat cold: crackers, crispbread, rusks, rolled oats.
- 1.2 kg of tinned vegetables and pulses.
- 0.75 kg of tinned fruit, dried fruit and nuts.
- 0.75 kg of long-life milk or milk powder.
- 0.36 kg of tinned meat or fish.
- A small bottle of oil.
- Energy bars, chocolate, honey or jam as desired.
- Tin opener.

**2 weeks for 1 adult**

- 42 litres of water as a minimum (3 litres per day).
- 4.6 kg of grain products, bread and potatoes, including pasta and rice if you can cook.
- 5.6 kg of vegetables and pulses.
- 3.5 kg of fruit and nuts.
- 3.5 kg of milk and dairy products.
- 1.7 kg of eggs, meat, sausage and fish, mainly tinned.
- About half a litre of oil or fat.
- Sugar, salt, herbs and spices, stock, flour and ready meals as desired.
- An alternative means of cooking and fuel (see the guide on food without electricity).

## Babies and young children

- Keep a reserve supply of baby food and nappies at home: formula powder, disposable nappies and baby wipes, bottles, nappy rash cream, the medicines your children need and extra toys.
- Preferably use ready-to-feed formula. If you have to make up powder, use bottled water, or boiled water as a last resort.
- When a power cut has been announced, prepare vacuum flasks with hot water, for example for baby bottles.
- Porridge and oatmeal are handy for children.

## Diets, allergies and illness

- Adapt your supply to the allergies, eating habits, age, work and diet of everyone in the household. Choose food that your family eats.
- If you need prescription medicines or disposable supplies, for example for diabetes, make sure you have a supply for a month. See the guide on medication and care.

## Pets

- Provide food, water and bowls for at least 72 hours, preferably longer: dry food or tinned pet food, water in containers and the animal's medicines.
- For drinking water, count on about 50 ml per kilogram of body weight per day.

## The rest of the supply at home

In addition to food and drink, you should have at home:

- **Health.** A basic first aid kit: bandages, plasters, compresses, scissors, tweezers, safety pins, painkillers, disinfectants. Tissues, wet wipes, toilet paper, sanitary towels and disinfecting gel. Iodine tablets: free of charge from your pharmacy, take them only when the authorities recommend it.
- **Light, heat and electronics.** A torch, preferably a wind-up one with a crank; otherwise spare batteries. A wind-up or battery-powered radio. A lighter or matches. Candles or tealights. Blankets.
- **Children.** Baby food and nappies.
- **Pets.** Food, water and bowls for 72 hours.

Also useful: an alternative means of cooking (camping stove, gas barbecue) and fuel, sleeping bags, a heat source that works without mains power using fuel, and hygiene products such as soap, toothpaste, bin bags (including large, strong ones to hang in the toilet), kitchen roll, household gloves and a camping toilet. See also the guides on heat, light and energy, hygiene and food without electricity.

Rough guide values. For purchased goods, the date on the packaging always applies.

| Product | Storage | Keeps for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tinned vegetables | dry, airy, preferably below 20 °C, glass jars in the dark | 1 to 2 years |
| Tinned fruit, jam, jelly | dry, airy, preferably below 20 °C | 1 year |
| Tinned fish and meat | dry, airy, preferably below 20 °C | several years |
| Dried fruit and dried vegetables | dry, dark, in glass or tins, preferably below 20 °C | 1 year |
| Dried potato products | dry, preferably below 20 °C | 1 to 2 years |
| Semolina, rolled oats, rice, pasta, cornflakes | tightly closing jars or boxes, dry | 1 year |
| Whole grains | dry, air regularly | 2 years |
| Crispbread, rusks | dry | 1 year |
| Fresh wholemeal bread | bread bin | 10 to 12 days |
| Mineral water in glass | dark, cool, dry | usually 2 years on the label |
| Mineral water in PET | dark, cool, dry | usually 1 year on the label |

## Rotating: a supply you actually use

Keep a rolling supply:

- Buy one extra product each time you do the shopping.
- Choose foods that you normally use as well.
- If a product is about to expire, use it in your daily meals and replace it later.
- Check the supply every six months and put that in your diary. Agree who gets the supply in and who checks it. At the same time, check whether your situation has changed, for example because of a baby or new medicines.

This is how you keep the supply in order:

1. Keep a supply list and cross off what you use.
2. Always put new stock at the back or at the bottom. Use the older stock first.
3. Write the name, quantity and date on products you have preserved or frozen yourself.
4. Remove rotting fruit and rotting vegetables straight away.
5. Remove mouldy products straight away. If in doubt, do not eat it.
6. Never eat from a bulging tin (bottom or lid curved outwards). The contents are spoiled.
7. If there is no date on a product, write the date of purchase on it.
8. Do not eat the contents of opened jars of home-preserved food any more.

Store tins in a cool, dry place and boxed food in tightly closing plastic or metal containers. Replace what has expired.

## Where to store it: house, cellar, flat

**General**

- Keep all items together in a fixed, accessible place and make sure everyone in the house knows where they are.
- Store water and food in a cool, dark and dry place.
- Ideal is a store cupboard at 15 to 20 °C, up to 70 per cent humidity, ventilated and dark, or a cellar at 4 to 12 °C, 85 to 90 per cent humidity, ventilated and dark.

**House with a cellar.** The cellar is cool and dark, but cellars and garages are more vulnerable to flooding. So do not store everything there, and certainly not on the floor if you live in a flood-prone area.

**House without a cellar.** A shed or hall cupboard is a cool and dark place.

**Flat.** Make one joint emergency kit with the residents of a block of flats, student accommodation or other form of shared living instead of one per household unit, for example in a cellar or communal area. Agree who has the keys as well. High up in a building, your own water supply is especially important: during a power cut, no more water may come out of the tap even on the third floor.

**Little space.** You do not need to buy an expensive kit: you already have most items at home. Some things, such as a radio, you can buy together with neighbours and share.

## During a shortage

During a disruption of the food supply:

- Be sparing with food.
- Make sure vulnerable people, for example children and older people, have enough nutritious food.
- Do not hoard. Buy a limited quantity, so that everyone has the chance to buy food.
- Afterwards, supplies will start up again. For some products this may take longer.

Provide some extra water and food to share with vulnerable people around you.

## Checklist

- [ ] Water: at least 3 litres per person per day for 72 hours, preferably more for washing and the toilet.
- [ ] Food for 72 hours that you can eat without water, gas or electricity.
- [ ] Tin opener.
- [ ] Baby food, nappies and toys if there are young children.
- [ ] Food, water and bowls for the pets.
- [ ] A supply for anyone who follows a diet or has an allergy.
- [ ] A fixed, dry, cool place, not everything in the cellar.
- [ ] A reminder in the diary to check every six months.
- [ ] After that: expand step by step, for example to 2 weeks.
