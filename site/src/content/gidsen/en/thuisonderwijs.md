If schools stay closed for a long time and there is no electricity or internet, distance learning also falls away. You do not need to imitate school. The aim is for children and young people to keep a fixed routine, feel safe and keep reading, writing and doing arithmetic. International standards for education in emergencies say that education itself helps: structure, routines and something to look forward to reduce stress and give hope.

The learning activities by level are in two separate guides: nursery and primary school, and secondary school and adults. How to talk with children about the situation is covered in the guide on reassuring children and giving structure.

Source: [INEE: Minimum Standards for Education (2024)](https://inee.org/minimum-standards)

## What comes first

You cannot do everything. Choose in this order:

1. **Safety and basic needs.** Water, food, warmth, sleep and a safe place come first.
2. **Well-being and a fixed routine.** A day with fixed moments gives something to hold on to. Play, movement, music and drawing belong to it, for older children too.
3. **Reading, writing and arithmetic.** These are the skills on which everything else rests. INEE names them, together with social and emotional skills, as the core to start with.
4. **Practical skills for the situation.** Hygiene, first aid, handling fire and water safely, map and compass, the weather.
5. **The rest of the curriculum.** World studies, science, history, languages, music and art, as much as you can manage.

The Flemish step-by-step plan for distance learning says the same to schools: choose deliberately, focus on the essential subject matter and limit the amount. Parents are not teachers and cannot take over that role completely.

## How to teach at home

- **Work on paper and with your hands.** Writing, drawing, sorting, cutting, counting with objects. This works without a screen and keeps children active.
- **Use what is in the house.** Pebbles, buttons, dried beans or rice for counting, a stick to draw shapes in the sand, packaging to read and weigh. UNICEF gives tasks such as this as an example: find objects shaped like a triangle.
- **Read a lot.** Learning at home relies on reading even more than at school. Read aloud to those who cannot yet read well, and let older children read aloud to younger ones.
- **Let children work independently.** A reading book, a workbook or a project they can spend a few days on. A strong structure and tasks that children can do on their own help parents most.
- **Check the work briefly.** Looking together once or twice a week at what has been done is enough. Praise the effort.
- **Let older children help younger ones.** Brothers and sisters, grandparents and neighbours can take over part of it. Children who explain something learn from it themselves too.
- **Watch for stress.** Do not force it. If a day is not working, choose reading aloud, games or exercise outside.
- **Weak readers and children with a disability.** Make tasks visual, with drawings and examples, and explain them out loud. Many parents find reading difficult themselves: then written instructions do not always help.
- **Language.** Young children learn best in a language they understand, preferably their mother tongue.

## What you need

Build up a small stock in advance, in the emergency kit or in a dedicated box:

- For each child, a lined exercise book and a squared one, pencils, a rubber, a sharpener, colouring pencils, a ruler.
- A pile of blank paper and card, scissors with rounded tips, sticky tape.
- A few reading books for each age, a dictionary, an atlas or a paper map of the area, a compass.
- A pack of cards and dice: you can practise arithmetic with them.
- Printed worksheets and learning materials (see below: downloading in advance).
- A folder for each child to keep the work they have done. That way the school can see later what the child did.

Learn by daylight: put the table by the window. See the guide on light and energy without mains electricity for safe light in the evening.

Source: [UNICEF: Guidance on distance learning modalities (2020)](https://www.allinschool.org/media/1626/file/Guidance-distance-learning-modalities-UNICEF-2020.pdf)

## Daily routine by age

A fixed day gives something to hold on to. UNICEF advises parents to plan a daily routine. Keep fixed times for getting up, eating, learning, moving and sleeping. The times below are an example. Adjust them to the daylight, the meals and the tasks in the house.

| Time | Nursery (3 to 5 years) | Primary school (6 to 12 years) | Secondary school (12 to 18 years) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Morning, after breakfast | go over the day together, get dressed, tidy up | daily plan, a task in the house | daily plan, a task in the house |
| First block | games with counting, shapes, colours (short, with breaks) | arithmetic (30 to 45 minutes) | maths or science (45 to 60 minutes) |
| Break | play or move outside | play or move outside | exercise |
| Second block | reading aloud, songs, talking about picture books | reading and writing (30 to 45 minutes) | language: reading, writing, a foreign language |
| Midday | eat, quiet time or afternoon nap | eat, quiet reading | eat, quiet reading |
| Afternoon | crafts, free play, outside | world studies, a practical skill, crafts | project or assignment, practical skill, helping in the house |
| Late afternoon | moving, outside | exercise, games | sport, free time |
| Evening | eat, story, sleep | eat, story or game, sleep | eat, game or reading, diary |

**By age**

- **Nursery children** learn by playing and talking. Do not plan lessons sitting down, but short games and lots of repetition. A quiet time after lunch is still needed for many nursery children.
- **Primary school.** Two to three short learning blocks a day are enough. Alternate sitting and moving.
- **Secondary school.** Young people can work independently for longer. Let them make their own daily and weekly plan and share responsibility, for example for a younger child or a task in the house.
- **Adults.** Also plan a fixed moment for yourself to read, learn something new or pass on a skill.

Movement counts too. According to the health guidelines, children and young people should be active for a total of an hour every day (see the guide on games and activities without electricity).

## Weekly plan

The Flemish step-by-step plan advises schools to offer material per week, with a weekly schedule. You can do that at home too. Choose a theme for each week, for example "water", "the weather", "our street" or "the old days". That way reading, arithmetic and world studies fit together.

| Day | Morning | Afternoon |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | arithmetic; reading and writing | start of the theme: what do we already know, what do we want to know |
| Tuesday | arithmetic; reading and writing | world studies or science around the theme |
| Wednesday | arithmetic; reading and writing | free: outside, games, visiting neighbours |
| Thursday | arithmetic; reading and writing | practical skill: cooking, first aid, map and compass |
| Friday | revise what was difficult | music, drawing, drama; look together at the week's work |
| Weekend | no lessons | reading aloud, games, exercise, rest |

- On Friday, look together at what has been done. Put it in the child's folder.
- Keep a simple list for each child: what they did this week and what they found difficult.
- Plan rest days too. A week without lessons is fine, for example during a move or an evacuation.

## Following the curricula in Flanders

**Structure.** Nursery education (kleuteronderwijs) is for children from 2.5 to 6 years, primary education (lager onderwijs) for 6 to 12 years, usually in six school years. Secondary education has three stages (graden) of two school years each; the third stage can have three school years. Compulsory education starts on 1 September of the year in which a child turns 5 and lasts until 18. In the guides with learning activities we divide primary education into three parts of two school years, as many schools do.

**Educational goals.** The government sets out what pupils must know and be able to do as a minimum. In primary education (basisonderwijs) they are called minimum goals (minimumdoelen). They apply at three key points: the end of nursery education, the end of the 4th year and the end of the 6th year. They become compulsory step by step from 2026-2027 and are divided into ten subject disciplines:

- Dutch
- Mathematics
- Science and technology
- Geography
- History
- Arts education
- Physical education
- ICT
- Attitudes, such as learning to learn and social skills
- French

Schools may offer these subject disciplines together, for example as world studies.

In secondary education the goals are called attainment targets (eindtermen). They are built around sixteen key competences, including Dutch, other languages, mathematics, exact sciences and technology, historical and spatial awareness, citizenship, health, sustainability, economic and financial competences and learning competences. Part of them is called basic literacy (basisgeletterdheid): every pupil must reach this by the end of the first stage.

**Where to find them.** All educational goals are on onderwijsdoelen.be. The curricula (leerplannen), which work out the goals for each school or network, are published by GO!, Katholiek Onderwijs Vlaanderen (Zill for primary education), OVSG and provincial education (POV). Ask your child's school which curriculum it follows, and download or print the goals for your child's school year in advance.

**Home education as a choice.** If parents choose home education themselves in normal times, they report it to the Department of Education (Departement Onderwijs) every school year, at the latest on the 3rd school day. For the primary education certificate (getuigschrift basisonderwijs) and the certificate of the first stage, the child sits exams before the Examination Board (examencommissie). There are no compulsory books or methods, but for a certificate the educational goals must be achieved.

## Following the curricula in the Fédération Wallonie-Bruxelles

**Tronc commun.** The common core pathway runs from the first nursery class to the 3rd year of secondary school. It is being introduced year by year and reaches the 3rd year of secondary school in 2028-2029. Here too, compulsory education runs from 5 to 18. The Tronc commun has eight domains:

1. French, ancient languages, cultural and artistic education
2. Modern languages
3. Mathematics, sciences and technology
4. Human sciences, philosophy and citizenship, religion or ethics
5. Physical education, well-being and health
6. Creativity, engagement and entrepreneurial spirit
7. Learning to learn and making choices
8. Learning to find your direction

**Référentiels.** The goals for each subject are set out in référentiels, which you can download as PDFs from enseignement.be. There is one référentiel for nursery school and one per subject for primary school: French and ancient languages, cultural and artistic education, modern languages, mathematics, sciences, manual, technical, technological and digital education, historical, geographical, economic and social education, philosophy and citizenship, and physical education and health.

**Enseignement à domicile as a choice.** Parents who choose home education themselves make a declaration every year, at the latest on 5 September. They draw up a learning plan. The inspectorate checks the level compulsorily at 8 and at 10 years, using the référentiels as the standard. The child sits the external tests (CEB, CE1D, CE2D).

**German-speaking Community.** There, Rahmenpläne (framework curricula) apply for subjects including German, French, mathematics, science and technology, history and geography, music and art, and sport.

## Downloading and printing learning materials in advance

Without electricity, paper is the only form that always works. Download and print the most important material while you can. Also keep a digital copy on a USB stick or on a device you can charge with a power bank.

**Licence and printing.** For your own use at home you may print almost anything you download legally. The licence only matters if you pass material on, for example in a reception centre or to other families. You may do that without asking under a Creative Commons licence (CC), crediting the author and the licence. Use material marked "all rights reserved" only for yourself.

**Choose paper over screen.** Interactive exercises do not work without internet. Choose worksheets and PDFs.

In order of preference:

1. **The school's materials.** Your child's textbooks, workbooks and course notes, and whatever the teacher can give in advance. Also ask for printed work packs. In the Fédération Wallonie-Bruxelles, the e-classe platform is only accessible to teaching staff: ask the teacher to give you material with a CC licence in advance.
2. **KlasCement** (Flanders). A platform of the Departement Onderwijs en Vorming (Department of Education and Training) on which teachers share learning materials. The licence differs per item: the author chooses a Creative Commons licence, from CC0 to CC BY-NC-ND. Some items have a different licence or no open licence. You need a free account; parents may register too. This is how to download: log in, search by level and subject, set the Licentie (licence) filter to CC0, BY or BY-SA (for home use NC is also fine), choose downloadable learning material, check the licence on the item and download the file.
3. **Wikiwijs** (the Netherlands, managed by Kennisnet). Lesson series with an open licence: CC BY 4.0 or CC BY-SA 4.0. Downloading a lesson series as a PDF for printing is possible without an account, and interactive questions are then converted to paper. The material follows the Dutch curriculum, not the Belgian one.
4. **Books to read and to read aloud.** Wikisource contains texts that are free of rights, under CC BY-SA 4.0, and you can download them as PDFs. The DBNL (Digitale Bibliotheek voor de Nederlandse Letteren, the digital library of Dutch literature) allows downloading and printing for private use, not for passing on. Project Gutenberg offers books that are free of copyright in the United States; in Belgium a work only becomes free 70 years after the author's death, so check this for each book.
5. **OpenStax.** Free English-language textbooks for secondary and higher education, including mathematics and sciences. Since April 2026 under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Downloading as a PDF is possible without an account. They follow an American curriculum.
6. **Kiwix with Wikipedia offline.** Kiwix is free software (GPL-3.0) that reads Wikipedia and other reference works without internet, on Windows, macOS, Linux, Android and iOS. The text of Wikipedia is under CC BY-SA 4.0. You download a ZIM file in advance via the app or library.kiwix.org. Start small: Dutch-language Wikipedia without images ("nopic") was 5.8 GB on 18 November 2026, the version with only the introduction of each article ("mini") 2.6 GB. There are also files with Wikisource, Wiktionary, Wikibooks, Project Gutenberg and, in French, the children's encyclopaedia Vikidia (CC BY-SA 3.0). Sizes change with every new version. Kiwix does need a charged device: so print the most important material as well.
7. **The goals themselves.** Print the minimum goals (minimumdoelen) or attainment targets (eindtermen) for your child's school year (onderwijsdoelen.be), or the référentiels of the Fédération Wallonie-Bruxelles (enseignement.be). They carry no open licence: use them for yourself.

**First aid.** The first aid learning pathway (Leerlijn eerste hulp) and the teaching packs "Eerste hulp bij moeilijke gevoelens" (first aid for difficult feelings, 8 to 10 years) from Rode Kruis-Vlaanderen can be downloaded free of charge. All rights are reserved: use them for yourself and refer others to the official link.

**PlanB72 itself.** All the guides in this app, including this one, are stored offline on your device as soon as the package has been downloaded. You can print them.

## If you have prepared nothing

- Start with what is there: packaging, a calendar, a newspaper, a cookbook, a manual, a map. Anything with text is reading material, anything with numbers is arithmetic material.
- Write tasks on paper yourself. Let children keep a diary: that is writing, and later a memory.
- Ask in the neighbourhood or at the reception centre for books, exercise books and pencils. Teachers, students and retired people nearby can help.
- Learning also happens in daily work: measuring water, counting supplies, planning a meal, recording the weather. See the guides with learning activities.
