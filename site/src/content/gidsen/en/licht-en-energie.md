During a power cut, a great deal fails at the same time: lighting, fridge and freezer, electric cookers, TV and computer, boilers and circulation pumps, lifts, automatic doors and garage doors, cash machines and petrol stations, and trains, trams and metros. The telephone network can also be disrupted: expect calls and the internet to stop working after a short time (see The phone: charge it, but do not rely on it). This guide covers light and power for the devices you really need.

Make a list beforehand of everything you need that runs on electricity, and plan for each device with batteries or another source.

| Light source | Advantages | Disadvantages and risks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wind-up torch with a crank | no batteries needed; the best choice | |
| Battery torch | | spare batteries needed |
| Head torch | hands free | batteries needed |
| Camping lamp on batteries or gas | lights a room | batteries or gas cartridges needed; gas is an open flame |
| Battery lantern | makes signing and lip-reading possible in the dark, for deaf and hard-of-hearing people | batteries needed |
| Candles and tea lights | cheap, keep for a long time | fire risk, use up oxygen, ventilation needed |

During a power cut, use a torch rather than candles whenever possible. Have a torch for every member of the household, and keep a torch or head torch with full batteries in a fixed, easy-to-reach place.

## Using candles safely

Candles appear on every emergency list, but they are also the light source with the most risk.

1. Put candles in a candle holder and place it on a fireproof surface.
2. Never leave them burning unattended.
3. Put them out before you go to sleep.
4. Ventilate regularly, because candles use up oxygen.
5. Make sure you have a working smoke alarm and keep extinguishing equipment to hand: fire extinguisher, garden hose or fire blanket.
6. Store candles and matches or a lighter in a safe, dry place.

During a power cut, the fire risk rises due to incorrect or unattended use of gas appliances, candles and the like.

## Batteries and power bank

- Have rechargeable batteries and spare batteries.
- Keep a charged power bank ready, in your emergency kit and in your grab bag.
- Make sure the batteries of laptops and phones are charged, or keep charged spare batteries and spare chargers ready.
- If you know a power cut is coming, charge your mobile phone and make sure you have a full portable charger.
- For an electric wheelchair or other battery-powered aid: buy an extra battery and always keep it charged. If you cannot buy one, ask which organisations can help.

## The car as a charger and as a radio

- Keep a car charger for your mobile phone ready, and a car with fuel in the tank or a charged battery.
- You can follow the news on the car radio.
- A full tank keeps you mobile if the fuel supply fails. During a power cut, petrol stations may be out of service.
- Car exhaust fumes contain CO and are a risk in a small space such as a garage. So do not let the engine run in an enclosed or partly enclosed space.
- Limit car journeys, because traffic lights may be out of order.

## Using a generator safely

**Before you buy one**

- Think about which devices you want to run. Anything that produces heat, such as kettles, heating and hobs, needs a lot of power: easily 1,500 to 2,000 watts per device.
- You can only connect sensitive devices such as a computer or the heating controls if the generator keeps voltage and frequency stable enough. Otherwise they will be damaged.
- Choose a quiet model. In densely populated areas and blocks of flats, the noise can disturb neighbours.
- Take into account the rules for storing fuel: only in suitable jerrycans, and no gas in the cellar.
- Get advice from a specialist dealer or qualified electrician.

**When using it**

1. Always outdoors. Never in the home, the garage or the cellar: the exhaust fumes can suffocate you. Place the generator at least 6 metres from windows, doors and an attached garage.
2. In a dry, well-ventilated place. Keep the generator dry and protected from rain and flooding. Touching a wet generator or the devices connected to it can give you an electric shock.
3. Connect devices with heavy-duty extension cables. You need an opening in the building for the cables; a door or window left open lets the home cool down and makes burglary easier.
4. Let the generator cool down before you refuel it. Fuel spilled on hot engine parts can catch fire.
5. Install CO alarms on every floor.
6. Follow the manufacturer's instructions.

**Never connect it to the house wiring yourself**

- Never connect a generator yourself to your home's electrical installation.
- If you want to power the house wiring with a generator, always get advice from a qualified electrician. Work on the house wiring may only be done by an approved electrical contractor, with a manual or automatic changeover switch. Also inform your grid operator and your supplier. In a block of flats, inform the owner.

## The phone: charge it, but do not rely on it

**What you need to know**

- Mobile masts can run for one to two hours without electricity. Mobile internet (4G and 5G) drops out quickly, probably immediately.
- Most landlines no longer work without electricity, because the modem or line equipment needs power. A traditional telephone line with a handset that has no power supply of its own still works for about four hours. With internet telephony, the router, fibre connection and handset all need power.
- Even if you have power yourself, the mobile network can drop out, because the mast is in an area without power. The reverse is also possible.
- Mobile networks have only partial, short-lived emergency power and quickly become overloaded.
- When the power comes back, it can take several hours before you can make mobile calls again.
- Check beforehand whether your landline works during a power cut and how long its backup battery lasts.

**Charge it anyway**

- Charge your mobile phone and keep it charged, so you stay reachable as long as there is a signal. Bear in mind that the mobile and fixed telephone networks may be disrupted.
- Turn down the screen brightness and close apps.
- Always keep one phone switched on at home, also at night, to receive messages such as BE-Alert as long as the network works.

**While the network works: save it**

- Call 112 only in immediate danger and limit your phone use, so as not to overload the network.
- Only phone when it is really necessary and preferably use text messages.
- In a major emergency, use messaging apps instead of calling. They need mobile internet, and that drops out quickly during a power cut.

**When calls and the internet no longer work**

- Follow the news on a battery radio, a wind-up radio or the car radio.
- The authorities can also inform you with police loudspeaker vans, information boards, leaflets and door-to-door.
- If calling does not work: try another phone or have neighbours or bystanders call. If calling does not work at all, ask for help in your neighbourhood or go, if possible, to the nearest police station, fire station or hospital (the map in the app shows them, with the Nearest button).
- In a major outage, the municipality may open an information and reception point. Ask beforehand what your municipality has planned.

More in the guide on communication and alerts.

## During the power cut

1. Unplug sensitive electrical devices, such as TV, computer, electric bikes and cars, to prevent damage from a power surge when the power is restored.
2. Switch off electric heating appliances: cooker, oven, iron, coffee maker, kettle, electric blanket, fan heater.
3. For the same reason, set the central heating thermostat as low as possible.
4. Leave one switch on, so you know when the power is back.
5. Use torches instead of candles.
6. Follow the news on a battery radio or the car radio.
7. Keep the fridge and freezer closed (see the guide on food without electricity).
8. Check whether vulnerable relatives and neighbours are all right, and look after anyone who needs extra help.
9. Gather with neighbours or family to share light and warmth. Agree beforehand whose home you can go to and which things you will share.

A power cut in itself is not an emergency: do not call the emergency numbers to ask for information, as this overloads the emergency call centres. Call 112 only in immediate danger. If calling does not work, see Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call.

## When the electricity comes back

- Do not switch all electrical devices on at the same time. Wait until the grid has been restored, to avoid overloading it.
- Restock your emergency kit after use.

## Saving electricity during shortages

During an electricity shortage, municipalities can be cut off for a while to prevent a general blackout (the load-shedding plan). You can help reduce the risk:

- Switch off unnecessary lighting. Leave the room, switch off the light.
- Turn thermostats down.
- Switch off devices on standby.
- Use the iron, washing machine, tumble dryer, electric cooker, microwave, dishwasher and vacuum cleaner only when necessary and preferably after 22:00.
- Use as few hobs as possible, and the microwave instead of the ordinary oven.
- Register with BE-Alert beforehand, so you know in good time that your municipality will be cut off.
- The government announces the day before which zones may be cut off. That information is also given on TV, radio and in the press.

## People who depend on electricity

- If you use devices that run on electricity, such as an oxygen machine or an electric wheelchair, or refrigerated medicines, discuss with your doctor and care providers what to do in a breakdown.
- Ask your energy supplier whether you can be put on a list for priority when power is restored.
- In home care, life-sustaining devices such as ventilators can fail, and services such as personal alarms and meals on wheels can stop. Ask your care service whether and how these services continue during a long power cut. If a family member lives in a care home, ask about the emergency power plan.
- If you use oxygen equipment, pump systems, home dialysis or another powered device at home: do not wait for an outage. Find out how long the fully charged internal battery of your device lasts, and look at where else you could be taken in.
- If you use the device intermittently, as with home dialysis, make sure it is not connected at the time of an announced power cut.

What to do if you cannot call your care provider is in the guide on medication and care.

## Electric doors, gates and lifts

- Know how to unlock electronic gates, roller shutters or doors during a power failure.
- Without electricity you cannot use the lift. Anyone with limited mobility should practise whether they can leave the building without the lift, and agree who will help.

## Checklist

- [ ] A torch per person, preferably wind-up, or with spare batteries.
- [ ] Head torch.
- [ ] Radio on batteries, crank or solar panel, preferably with DAB+ and FM.
- [ ] Rechargeable and spare batteries.
- [ ] A charged power bank at home and in the grab bag.
- [ ] A mobile phone charger for the car.
- [ ] Candles or tea lights, candle holders, matches or a lighter, stored dry.
- [ ] Smoke alarm and CO alarm, tested.
- [ ] Fire extinguisher or fire blanket.
- [ ] Knowing how to open gates and roller shutters without electricity.
- [ ] A plan for every powered device someone depends on.
- [ ] A paper list of important phone numbers.
- [ ] Arrangements with your neighbours: how you will reach each other without phone or internet, and where you will gather to share light and warmth.
