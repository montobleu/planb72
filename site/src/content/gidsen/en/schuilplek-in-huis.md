## Why you choose the place in advance

With many threats you have little time. In an air threat there is very little time between the alarm and the actual danger. That is why you usually shelter where you are at that moment. Those who know in advance which room in the house is best lose no time.

Make an emergency plan with the people you live with. Discuss the three steps of sheltering in advance with family, neighbours or colleagues and practise the plan regularly. Learn how to switch off the ventilation, at home and in buildings where you often are. Also agree with your neighbours how you will reach each other if the telephone or internet does not work.

A shelter at home does not replace a real civil defence shelter. Where there are no or too few civil defence shelters, an improvised shelter is the solution. The two-wall rule does not give complete safety.

## One home, several places

Do not choose one place, but two or three, because the best place depends on the threat.

| Threat | Best place in your home | Not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Air attack, explosion | Cellar without windows; otherwise an inner room with two walls between you and the outside | Attic, rooms with windows, crawl space |
| Nuclear accident | Central room on the ground floor | Rooms along the outer wall, near windows |
| Nuclear weapon | Cellar; otherwise the middle of the building | Rooms along the outer wall, near windows, under the roof |
| Chemical cloud | Central room on the ground floor, well sealed | Cellar: most gases sink downwards |
| Flood | Highest floor | Cellar, basement |
| Storm, tornado | Inner room away from windows and outer walls | Near windows, under the roof |

In Belgium the general advice for sheltering is: a central room on the ground floor, away from windows and outside doors. The cellar is mainly an option for explosions, air attacks and a nuclear weapon.

## The criteria

These criteria are mainly about protection against explosions and, in the case of a nuclear weapon, against radiation. In a nuclear accident or a chemical cloud, the Belgian advice is a central room on the ground floor.

### Depth and location

- **Underground is best.** A cellar offers effective protection because of its sheltered position. Against radiation, the shielding in cellars is particularly high because of the surrounding earth and the storeys above.
- **As deep as possible in the ground and in the middle of the building.** Choose a cellar in the central part of the building, dug in as deep as possible, with vaulted or reinforced concrete ceilings and thick outer walls.
- **The floor above the groundwater level.**

### Mass and construction

A good room has three properties: a strong structure (thick walls, columns, floors and ceilings, often in concrete), robust walls (thick, few openings, little glass) and a sheltered position. The minimum for the outer walls of a cellar shelter is 45 cm of masonry or 30 cm of reinforced concrete. Frame construction in reinforced concrete or steel and solid buildings in brick or stone are the most suitable. Buildings with a lot of combustible material and buildings with only one or two storeys are unsuitable.

### Number of walls between you and the outside

Make sure there are two walls between you and the outside. If the outer wall is damaged, the second wall protects you against fragments and debris. The deeper the room lies in the building, the better.

### Windows and glass

Inner rooms with as few outer walls and doors as possible and without windows protect well, because shards of glass cannot then cause injuries. Avoid rooms with a direct line of sight to the outside.

### Exits

- Make sure there are at least two exits or escape routes, or the possibility of making a second exit or route. That way you do not get trapped if the main entrance is buried under debris.
- Keep the exits usable, clear and not permanently locked.
- The entrance door must open outwards.

### What is above and next to it

Do not set up a shelter:

- under rooms where more than 1,000 kg per square metre is stored;
- under production or storage areas with tanks of dangerous chemical liquids or molten metal;
- within 100 metres of reservoirs of dangerous substances or heavy gases;
- within 50 metres of companies or storage facilities with a risk of explosion or highly flammable substances;
- above pipes carrying high-pressure steam, high voltage or compressed air;
- in a room where fuel, gas cylinders, chemicals or combustible materials are kept.

Also keep your distance from gas, electricity and technical rooms.

### What you had better not choose

- **Attic and top floor:** a roof offers little protection against blast waves, glass and debris.
- **Crawl space.**
- **Kitchen:** glass, heavy appliances and other hazards.
- **Bathroom** only as a last resort, and only without a gas boiler or gas water heater. Tiles and mirrors can break.
- **Rooms with windows, balconies or loggias**, or directly opposite windows and large mirrors.

## Per type of home

### House with a cellar

1. **Best choice:** a cellar room without windows, under the middle of the house, directly accessible from the stairs.
2. **Good choice:** a cellar room with a small window that you shield (see below).
3. **If the cellar is not an option** (flood, gas leak, chemical cloud): an inner room on the ground floor or, in the case of water, the upper floor.

### House without a cellar

1. **Best choice:** an inner room without windows: a hallway, understairs cupboard or storeroom in the middle of the house.
2. **Good choice:** the room with the fewest outer walls and the least glass.
3. **Emergency solution:** behind a robust object, such as a kitchen unit or the parapet of an outer wall.

### Flat

1. **Best choice in an air attack:** the cellar or underground car park of the building, if you can reach it quickly.
2. **Good choice in the flat:** the hallway, entrance hall or cloakroom, if it lies more towards the middle of the flat, or a storeroom without windows. A hallway is better than a bathroom.
3. **Stairwell:** a fallback option if there are no large windows or glass walls and you can stay against an inner wall. If the stairwell has a lot of glass, go back to the hallway of your flat.
4. **Position:** sit or crouch by a load-bearing inner wall, away from windows, balconies, mirrors and glass. Stay as low as possible, preferably lying down, and protect your head and vital parts with your arms or blankets.

The cellars of some older blocks of flats are small and narrow, often contain pipes and cannot take any extra load. Old cellars can leak, be damp, contain hazardous substances or be poorly ventilated. Unfinished buildings are also not recommended: weak ceilings, no doors, poor ventilation.

## How much space per person

In an improvised shelter, allow 3 to 5 m² of floor per person without mechanical ventilation, and 1 to 3 m² with mechanical ventilation. The room is preferably at least 2.3 m high. Make sure that at least a third of the people can lie down and the others can sit: one bed for every two seats.

Source: [Sebeochrana ukrytím](https://mv.gov.cz/clanek/sebeochrana-ukrytim.aspx)

## Shielding windows and doors

### Against glass and debris (explosion, air attack)

- Close windows, doors and shutters. Cover them, for example with curtains.
- Use mass as extra shielding: tables, mattresses or other sturdy furniture.
- If you have doubts about the protection, for example with thin walls, reinforce them with sandbags, bags of earth or concrete blocks.

### Against radiation (cellar)

Block cellar windows with bricks, stones or bags of sand or earth, at least to the thickness of the outer wall. Cover them on the outside with planks, boards or sheet steel and cover these with earth, sand or gravel. Leave room for ventilation when doing so.

### Against gas and dust (chemical cloud, radioactive cloud)

- Close all windows and doors and all openings where outside air comes in: broken windows, open fireplaces, the cooker hood in the kitchen. Make the bottom of doors draught-proof with damp cloths.
- Close the ventilation slots in window frames.
- Seal windows, doors and air vents with thick plastic sheeting and tape. Measure and cut the sheeting in advance, a few centimetres wider than the opening, and label each piece. Tape the corners first, then all the edges.
- Gaps around cooker and dryer vents can be sealed with wet towels, sheeting, tape, wax paper or aluminium foil.
- Other usable sealing material: self-adhesive foam strip, plaster, gypsum, putty, modelling clay or clay.

### Doors

Reinforce the entrance door of a cellar shelter with planks and crossbars, make it gas-tight with sealing material and an adapted threshold, and, once the shelter is occupied, secure it with beams and wire against being sucked open after a blast wave. In an air attack without gas, the door does not need to be airtight, but it must be strong enough to withstand a blast wave.

### Propping up ceilings

You can prop up cellar ceilings with wooden beams and posts. Have any reinforcement of ceilings assessed by a structural engineer. Do not prop up vaults that rest in the walls. Never knock out a load-bearing wall to obtain material.

## Ventilation

Here two aims conflict: keeping outside air out and keeping enough oxygen.

- **In a chemical or radioactive cloud:** switch off ventilation, air conditioning and heating that draw in outside air. If that is not possible, set the system to 100 per cent recirculation.
- **Do make sure the air is refreshed,** so that you do not feel unwell.
- **A completely sealed room without a filter installation** can only be used for a limited time, depending on the size of the room and the number of people. Exhaled carbon dioxide can then lead to poisoning. Sealing a room is a temporary measure.
- **No candles:** they use up oxygen unnecessarily. Use open flames only in exceptional cases and only at the ventilation outlet. Do not smoke.
- **In an air attack without gas:** make sure the air circulates freely, without strong smells, smoke or stuffiness. For more than 50 people, natural ventilation is often not enough.

## What to have ready

### Basics

- Drinking water: allow 3 litres per person per day, for drinking and cooking. More if you can, for hygiene. Keep it in sealable containers.
- Long-life food that you can prepare without water, gas or electricity, and a tin opener or pocket knife.
- Enough water and food to manage on your own for at least 72 hours.
- A battery-powered or wind-up radio, preferably with DAB+ and FM, rechargeable batteries and spare batteries. That way you stay informed even without electricity: in a power cut, the internet and mobile networks can also fail.
- A paper list of important telephone numbers.
- A torch, preferably wind-up. Warm clothing or blankets.
- Iodine tablets. Never take them on your own initiative.
- Baby food, nappies and toys if you have young children. Food and equipment for pets.
- A small first aid kit, medication for at least three days, a whistle and a charged power bank.

### For the shelter room itself

- An emergency toilet: a portable bucket with seat and lid, separately behind a curtain. After use, sprinkle with lime, sand or earth. Sealed bags or containers can also serve as a temporary toilet.
- A waste bin, a separate corner for waste and a bucket for used water.
- Hygiene products.
- Beds and seats, a sleeping bag and mat.
- Warm things: cellars are cold and damp.
- A torch and batteries, a radio, a first aid kit and a fire extinguisher.
- Earplugs and a sleep mask, books and games.
- Thick plastic sheeting, tape and scissors to seal windows and ventilation.
- A set of spare clothing per person and a bin bag for contaminated clothing.

### What not to take

Do not take alcohol, drugs, weapons, devices that produce heat (gas heaters, storm lanterns) or strongly scented products into the shelter room.

### Where to keep it

Keep everything together in a fixed, easily accessible place and make sure everyone knows where it is. Store water and food in a cool, dark place and check regularly that everything is still usable. When a conflict threatens, place the emergency supply in or near the shelter room early on.

## How long you stay in the shelter

In Belgium, sheltering lasts from a few hours up to a maximum of 24 hours, also in a nuclear accident. Afterwards the authorities may ask you to evacuate. Stay until the authorities say it is safe.

With a nuclear weapon, you stay in the best-protected place for the first 24 hours. If you shelter in a cellar against air attacks or in a war, prepare for a few days.

## Behaviour while sheltering

- Follow the instructions of the authorities on a battery-powered or wind-up radio. Also watch for police loudspeaker vans in the street. If the network works, the instructions also come via BE-Alert and official websites.
- Do not make unnecessary calls. An overloaded network can disrupt the emergency response.
- Do not smoke in the shelter room.
- Be considerate: do not make noise, avoid arguments and walk back and forth as little as possible. A shelter is demanding on people, physically and mentally.
- Expect that it may be uncomfortable and crowded.
- If you go to a shelter outside your home: shut off the gas, electricity and water, close windows and air vents, switch off electrical appliances, put out open fires, alert your neighbours and house pets as well as possible with water and food.
- If you cannot leave the room on your own, tell your neighbours.

## Children

- Create a calm atmosphere. Children quickly pick up on what adults feel.
- Listen to their questions and feelings. Say that it is normal to be afraid, sad, angry or uncertain.
- Explain it simply and honestly, in a way suited to their age. Say so if you do not know something.
- Practise simple safety rules in a playful way.
- Keep to the daily routine as much as possible.
- Let children help with the emergency plan and the emergency kit, and let them choose a small toy or cuddly toy for the kit.

Source: [National Crisis Center: brochure Prepared together](https://crisiscenter.be/sites/default/files/documents/files/WEB-Brochure%20Prepared%20Together-EN.pdf)

## People who need extra help

Think of older people, people with a disability, people who live alone, people who understand Dutch, French or German less well, people who need extra support for medical reasons and homeless people.

- Discuss in advance with family, friends, neighbours or care providers what help is needed.
- Include information about prescribed medication and aids in your emergency plan.
- Arrange transport to a protected place in advance.
- Consider whether someone depends on an assistant or electronic aids; that changes the preparation.
- Give people in poor health a place closer to the ventilation.
- During an emergency, check regularly whether vulnerable people need help, without putting yourself in danger.

## Pets

- Include your pets in your emergency plan, with the vet's number. Make sure every animal is identified with a microchip or ear tag.
- Have ready: a cage or basket, lead and collar or harness, food, water and bowls, pet passport and vaccination booklet, a photo of you and your animal, toys, medication and bags for droppings.
- If you can let your animals shelter safely without risk to yourself, do so.
- If your pet has to stay at home while you shelter elsewhere, leave extra food and water.

## Step-by-step plan for setting up your shelter

This is how to prepare an improvised shelter:

1. Choose the suitable room.
2. Determine how many people can shelter there, based on floor area and volume.
3. Determine which adaptations are needed, what material you need and how much time it will take.
4. Find out where the main taps for gas, water and heating are.
5. Clear the room.
6. Carry out the adaptations outside, including ventilation if needed.
7. Carry out the adaptations inside and seal the room.
8. Furnish the room.

Also agree who is responsible for the keys, the water, the first aid kit and the fire extinguisher. Make a simple checklist, agree basic rules and mark the entrance, exits and contact details. Check the wiring and the electrical cabinets, provide ventilation and dry the room if needed. Remove anything that can fall, break or burn.

| Is the room underground or in the middle of the building | Yes |
