## First: asking for help without a network

This guide is for those who cannot call: no coverage, no device, no battery or a network outage. Those who have already been found but still need to pass something on to a helicopter or aircraft will also find the signs here.

If there are people nearby, speak to them first. Ask for help in your neighbourhood and send someone to fetch help. What to do in a village or town if you cannot call is in the guide Emergency call, under If you cannot call at all.

If your phone does work, call 112 or use the 112 BE app.

| Situation | Best choice | Good choice | Last resort |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| No coverage, someone nearby | Have someone fetch help or send them to a place with coverage | Go to a place with coverage yourself | Sound signals: whistle, shouting |
| No coverage, nobody nearby, daytime | Whistle (three or six signals) | Mirror towards an aircraft or helicopter | Clothing or material on the ground in a large pattern |
| No coverage, at night | Torch: SOS or the alpine distress signal | Whistle | Fire, only if it is allowed and can be done safely |
| A helicopter or aircraft is in sight | Arm signals (Y or N) | Mirror, torch | Ground-to-air signal |
| You do have coverage after all | Call 112 or use the 112 BE app | Have someone else call | If 112 does not answer: see the guide Emergency call |

Save your energy. A whistle is better than shouting.

Source: [National Park Service: Outdoor Emergency Plan](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/healthandsafety/upload/NPS-Outdoor-Emergency-Plan-508c.pdf) · [Deutscher Alpenverein: Erste Hilfe](https://www.forststeig.sachsen.de/download/Erste_Hilfe_2012_DAV.pdf) · [US Army FM 21-76, chapter 19](https://archive.org/details/fm-21-76-survival-manual) · [FPS Economy: questions from households about electricity shortages](https://economie.fgov.be/nl/themas/energie/bevoorradingszekerheid/elektriciteit/elektriciteitsschaarste/veelgestelde-vragen/veelgestelde-vragen-door)

## The international distress signal: three

The number three is the generally recognised sign of distress.

- **Whistle:** blow three times, pause, and blow three times again. Repeat.
- **Fire:** make three fires in a triangle or in a straight line, about 25 metres apart. If you are alone, keeping one fire going is often hard enough.
- **Smoke:** three columns of smoke.
- **Shots:** three shots with clear intervals.
- **Light:** SOS with the torch (see below).
- **Colour:** red is the international distress colour.

A whistle is excellent at close range. There are known cases in which a whistle was heard up to 1.6 kilometres away. A manufactured whistle carries further than whistling with your mouth.

Source: [National Park Service: Outdoor Emergency Plan](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/healthandsafety/upload/NPS-Outdoor-Emergency-Plan-508c.pdf) · [US Army FM 21-76, chapter 19](https://archive.org/details/fm-21-76-survival-manual)

## The alpine distress signal: six per minute

In the mountains, use the alpine distress signal if you have no phone connection and are within sight or earshot of a valley, a hut or another inhabited place. Whistle signals, shouting and light signals are suitable.

| | How |
| --- | --- |
| **Distress signal** | Minute 1: one signal every 10 seconds, so 6 times. Minute 2: pause. Minute 3: again 6 times, and so on. |
| **Reply** | One signal every 20 seconds, so 3 times per minute. Then 1 minute pause, and so on. |

If you hear or see the reply, you know that someone has seen or heard you. Stay where you are until help arrives.

Source: [Deutscher Alpenverein: Erste Hilfe](https://www.forststeig.sachsen.de/download/Erste_Hilfe_2012_DAV.pdf) · [Deutscher Alpenverein: So geht das, Notfall alpin](https://www.alpenverein.de/artikel/so-geht-das-notfall-alpin_62bc1334-e9c7-4d8b-8a02-89dd681cbb83)

## SOS and Morse

**SOS** is the internationally recognised distress signal in Morse: three dots, three dashes, three dots.

`· · ·   − − −   · · ·`

- With a torch: three times short, three times long, three times short.
- With a flag: the flag to the left for a dash, to the right for a dot.
- Keep repeating the signal.
- A torch drains your battery. Use it wisely.

**Timing according to the international convention (ITU)**

| Element | Duration |
| --- | --- |
| Dot | 1 unit |
| Dash | 3 units |
| Pause within a letter | 1 unit |
| Pause between two letters | 3 units |
| Pause between two words | 7 units |

## Mirror and light

**Mirror (daytime, in sunshine)**

- On a sunny day a mirror is the best signalling device. If you have no mirror, use something else that shines: a metal cup, a buckle.
- Practise now. Do not wait until you need it.
- If you have a signal mirror, follow the instructions on the back.
- Wear the mirror on a cord around your neck, with the glass towards your body, so that it is ready for use.
- Do **not** flash **rapidly in succession**. Do **not** aim the beam at the cockpit **for more than a few seconds**: you can blind the pilot.
- Haze, ground fog and mirages make your flashes harder to see. If possible, go to the highest point.
- If you cannot see the aircraft, flash in the direction of the sound.
- Under ideal conditions, pilots have seen mirror flashes up to 160 kilometres away.
- A mirror does not need a battery.

**Torch, strobe light and rescue blanket**

- At night you can signal SOS to an aircraft with a torch or a strobe light.
- You can attract the attention of an approaching helicopter with a rescue blanket or a torch.
- At night, the arm signal "yes" goes with **green light**, "no" with **red light** (see below).

Source: [US Army FM 21-76, chapter 19](https://archive.org/details/fm-21-76-survival-manual) · [National Park Service: Outdoor Emergency Plan](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/healthandsafety/upload/NPS-Outdoor-Emergency-Plan-508c.pdf) · [Deutscher Alpenverein: Erste Hilfe](https://www.forststeig.sachsen.de/download/Erste_Hilfe_2012_DAV.pdf)

## Fire and smoke

Fire is the most visible signal at night, smoke during the day. But in Belgium making a fire in forests and nature areas is prohibited, except at a fixed campfire site or with a permit, and when there is a fire risk sometimes altogether. A signal fire can itself cause a wildfire. Only use fire if your life depends on it and there is no other means. See the guide on outdoor survival for the rules.

What the manuals say:

- **At night:** three fires in a triangle or in a line, about 25 metres apart. Prepare them and protect them until you need them.
- **During the day:** smoke. Make sure there is contrast: dark smoke against a light background, light smoke against a dark one. Almost smothering the fire with green leaves, moss or a little water produces white smoke.
- Smoke only works on fairly calm, clear days. Wind, rain and snow disperse the smoke.
- Always choose a place where you will not cause a forest fire or wildfire.
- The National Park Service advises against making a fire when the fire risk is high.

Source: [US Army FM 21-76, chapter 19](https://archive.org/details/fm-21-76-survival-manual) · [National Park Service: Outdoor Emergency Plan](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/healthandsafety/upload/NPS-Outdoor-Emergency-Plan-508c.pdf) · [National Crisis Center: Wildfire](https://crisiscenter.be/en/risks-belgium/natural-risks/wildfires)

## Ground-to-air signals (ICAO)

These signs are agreed internationally and are set out in Annex 12 to the Convention on International Civil Aviation (ICAO). Pilots of rescue aircraft know them.

**For those who need help**

| Sign | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **V** | Require assistance |
| **X** | Require medical assistance |
| **N** | No |
| **Y** | Yes |
| **↑** (arrow) | We are going in this direction |
| **SOS** | When in doubt: the international distress signal |

**How to make them**

- Use strips of fabric, branches, stones or whatever you have.
- Create as much colour contrast with the ground as possible.
- Make the signs at least 3 metres (10 feet) high or larger. The US Army gives as a minimum 1 metre wide and 6 metres long, with the same proportions if you make them larger.
- Lay them out exactly as above, in an open place that is clearly visible from the air.
- In snow: drag, shovel or stamp out the signs. Depressed areas look black from above. You can fill the trenches with branches.
- In sand: use boulders, plants or seaweed. Among bushes: cut patterns out of the vegetation.
- Build mounds that cast shadows.
- In addition, try to attract attention in every other way: radio, light, smoke.
- The pilot confirms that they have understood your message by rocking the wings from side to side.

**What rescue teams lay out on the ground** (so that you recognise it)

| Sign | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| LLL | Operation completed |
| LL (underlined) | We have found all personnel |
| ++ (two crosses) | We have found only some of the personnel |
| XX | We are not able to continue, returning to base |
| Two arrows pointing in opposite directions | We have split into two groups, each going in the direction indicated |
| Two arrows one behind the other | Information received that the aircraft is in this direction |
| NN | Nothing found, we will continue to search |

**An aircraft wreck with a yellow cross** means that the accident has already been reported and identified.

Source: [FAA Aeronautical Information Manual, chapter 6, section 2](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap6_section_2.html) · [US Army FM 21-76, chapter 19](https://archive.org/details/fm-21-76-survival-manual)

## Arm signals to a helicopter

If a helicopter or aircraft is close enough to see you clearly, use your body.

| Signal | Position | Meaning | At night |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Y (Yes)** | Both arms raised diagonally, so that your body forms a Y | Yes. We need help. Yes to a dropped question. Land here. | Green light |
| **N (No)** | One arm straight up, the other straight down along your body | No. We need nothing. No to a dropped question. Do not land. | Red light |

**How best to signal**

- Stand in open ground.
- Make sure the background, seen from above, is not confusing.
- Make the movements slowly and repeat each signal until you are sure that the pilot understands it.

**If the helicopter comes in to land**

1. Find a landing site of at least 25 by 25 metres, completely free of obstacles. **No cables or wires.**
2. Firm ground of at least 4 by 4 metres is needed for the skids. Not right next to the patient. Rega mentions a distance of about 100 metres from the accident site.
3. Clear away everything that is loose: clothing, rucksacks, ropes, ski poles, parasols. The downdraught from the rotor can suck them up and damage the helicopter or even make it crash. Put on goggles in snow.
4. Guide the helicopter in: stand with your **back to the wind** and make the Y sign, both arms raised with the palms facing inwards.
5. Stay where you are at the edge of the landing site, even if the helicopter comes straight at you. Crouch down and keep eye contact with the pilot.
6. Do not be alarmed if the helicopter first circles: the pilot is surveying the site.
7. Only approach the helicopter once the **rotor has come to a complete stop** or the crew calls you.
8. Always approach **from the front**, bent over, and in the mountains always **from the downhill side**.
9. Carry long objects such as skis or probes **horizontally**.

## Clothing and material on the ground

If you have no time or material for letters, attract attention from the air in another way.

- Spread clothing on the ground or in a treetop. Choose colours that stand out against the surroundings and lay them out in a large geometric pattern.
- Straight lines, circles, triangles and Xs stand out in uninhabited areas, as does a large bright object that moves slowly, or strong contrast in colour or shadow.
- Lay out your tarpaulin or emergency blanket, or make a shape with stones that can be seen from an aircraft or helicopter.
- Wear bright clothing yourself.
- Children who are lost: wave your arms, blow a whistle or flash a torch.

Source: [US Army FM 21-76, chapter 19](https://archive.org/details/fm-21-76-survival-manual) · [National Park Service: Outdoor Emergency Plan](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/healthandsafety/upload/NPS-Outdoor-Emergency-Plan-508c.pdf) · [National Park Service: Junior Ranger Park Explorer](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/healthandsafety/junior-ranger-park-explorer.htm)

## Sirens and BE-Alert in Belgium

**Sirens.** Belgium had a network of warning sirens. Until December 2018 it was tested audibly four times a year: on the first Thursday of January, April, July and October. After that, BE-Alert took over these test moments. The BE-Alert page on how you are warned does not list sirens among the channels.

**How you are warned now.** In serious emergencies the authorities try to reach you through as many channels as possible. Without power or a network, these are:

- the radio: a battery radio, a wind-up radio or the car radio;
- police loudspeakers and loudspeaker vans driving through the street;
- the police going door to door;
- information boards and leaflets.

If the network works, a warning also comes via BE-Alert (text message, voice message, email), television and social media.

**In advance.** Get a battery radio or a wind-up radio, preferably with DAB+ and FM. Register with BE-Alert; it is free.

**Recognising test messages.** A BE-Alert test always contains the words "BE-Alert", "This is a test message. You do not need to do anything", a link or information number, in the right language. Municipalities only test on the first Thursday of the month (except on public holidays), between 08:00 and 20:00, and inform their residents in advance.

**Do you hear a siren anyway?** We did not find a Belgian instruction for people who hear a siren. In the Netherlands, where the siren network still works, the advice is: go indoors as quickly as possible, close windows, doors and other openings, switch off mechanical ventilation, and follow the news. If you cannot go indoors, stay in the car, switch off the engine and close the air vents. The Dutch sirens are tested every first Monday of the month at 12:00. That is the Dutch arrangement, not the Belgian one.

## Rescue team markings on buildings

After an earthquake, explosion or collapse, international search and rescue teams (USAR) work with the marking system of INSARAG, the United Nations network. A country can also use its own system. The markings are applied with paint, construction crayon, stickers or waterproof cards, in a colour that contrasts with the wall.

**Worksite marking** (at the entrance or the front of a collapsed building)

- A box of about 1 to 1.2 metres.
- Inside the box: the worksite code (letters about 40 cm high), the team, the level of search carried out, the date.
- Above the box: a hazard, for example asbestos or a gas leak, in plain language.
- Below the box: the priority category.
- An arrow can point to the correct entrance.
- **A horizontal line through the entire marking:** all work has been done, there is nothing more to do.

**Victim marking** (as close as possible to the place where someone is lying under the rubble, about 50 cm in size)

| Sign | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Large **V** | Location of a potential victim, alive or deceased. An arrow can specify the location. |
| **L-2** below the V | Two confirmed live victims at this location |
| **D-3** below the V | Three confirmed deceased victims at this location |
| L or D crossed out and replaced (for example L-2 becomes L-1) | A victim has been removed |
| All L and D signs crossed out | All known victims have been removed |

**Rapid clearance marking** can have two meanings: "clear" (searched, no live or deceased victims) or "deceased only" (searched, only deceased victims remain).

**Building sides and floors.** The street side is side 1; the other sides are numbered 2, 3 and 4 clockwise. Inside, quadrants A, B, C, D are named clockwise, E is the centre (stairs, lifts). The level at street height is the "ground floor", above it Floor 1, below it Basement 1.

**What you do with them:** do not erase or change these markings. They prevent teams from duplicating work or missing a place.

Source: [INSARAG Guidelines Volume II, Manual B](https://insarag.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/INSARAG20Guidelines20Vol20II2C20Man20B.pdf)

## Sound signals of rescue teams

At a disaster site, international rescue teams use air horns with fixed signals. They are intended for the teams themselves, but it helps if you recognise them.

| Signal | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Three short blasts of one second, repeated until the site is empty | **Evacuate** |
| One long blast of three seconds | **Cease operations, quiet** |
| One long blast and one short | **Resume operations** |

Source: [INSARAG Guidelines Volume II, Manual B](https://insarag.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/INSARAG20Guidelines20Vol20II2C20Man20B.pdf)

## Leaving a note

**If you leave your home during an evacuation:** leave a note stating **when you left and where you are going**. Also help to warn your neighbours that they must evacuate. If the network works and you have time, contact your contact person outside the area and say where you are going, and let family and friends know when you are leaving and when you arrive.

**Agree in advance:** agree with your family where you will meet if you cannot reach each other.

**If you go out into nature:** make a trip plan and give it to someone who is staying at home. State where you are going and what you plan to do. Agree on when you will be back.

## On the radio: mayday and pan-pan (background)

These procedures are intended for those who are allowed to use a marine VHF radio or another radio. They are included here as background, so that you recognise them or can use them in extreme need.

**Who may use a marine VHF radio in Belgium?** You need an operator's certificate from the BIPT (after an exam), a ship station licence on board, and a correctly programmed device. The basic certificate (VHF) is sufficient for a marine VHF radio without DSC. For a marine VHF radio with DSC, at least the SRC certificate is needed; the BIPT recommends it for recreational boating at sea. In Belgium, report problems at sea to the Maritime Rescue and Coordination Centre via VHF channel 16. A marine VHF radio is mandatory for vessels of more than 7 metres.

| Call | When |
| --- | --- |
| **MAYDAY** | Grave and imminent danger to a vessel or person, help needed |
| **PAN-PAN** | Concern about the safety of a vessel or person, but no immediate danger to life |

**A mayday call on channel 16, in this order** (British coastguard)

1. If you have a device with DSC: press the red distress button and hold it down until the alert has been sent. Wait for the acknowledgement or about 15 seconds.
2. "Mayday, mayday, mayday", then the name of the vessel three times.
3. Call sign or other identification, and the MMSI number.
4. "Mayday" again, name, call sign and MMSI.
5. Your position, preferably in latitude and longitude.
6. What is wrong.
7. What help you need.
8. How many people are on board.
9. Other information.
10. "Over".

**A pan-pan call:** "Pan pan, pan pan, pan pan", three times "All stations", the name of the vessel (three times), call sign or identification and MMSI, the working channel you want to use, "Out". Then transmit your message on that working channel.

**If you hear a mayday yourself:** stop everything that could interfere with the distress traffic, keep listening on channel 16, and relay the message to a coast station if nobody answers.

**Contact with an aircraft via a radio** (US Army): "Mayday, mayday", call sign, name, location, number of survivors, possible landing sites, and what help is urgently needed.

## Emergency frequencies

**Radio amateurs.** The international amateur union IARU sets the frequencies on which radio amateurs gather in emergencies. These are "centres of activity": emergency traffic can be up to 20 kHz either side.

| Band | Europe and Africa (Region 1) | Worldwide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 80 m | 3760 kHz | |
| 40 m | 7110 kHz | |
| 20 m | 14300 kHz | 14300 kHz |
| 17 m | 18160 kHz | 18160 kHz |
| 15 m | 21360 kHz | 21360 kHz |

For Belgium the IARU also lists, among others, 51.510 MHz FM (6 m), 145.500 MHz FM (2 m) and 433.500 MHz FM (70 cm).

- To transmit on these frequencies, you need an operator's certificate for radio amateurs from the BIPT, for which you take an exam.
- In Belgium there is B-EARS, the emergency radio service of the radio amateur association UBA, with more than 300 members. The network only operates at the request of official bodies, when their ordinary communications fail. Its predecessor operated at the request of the Red Cross from 1962.

**Aviation.** Aircraft emergency beacons (ELT) transmit on 121.5 MHz, 243.0 MHz or 406 MHz. Since 2009 the Cospas-Sarsat satellite system no longer listens to 121.5 and 243.0 MHz, only to 406 MHz. On 121.5 MHz an emergency beacon is still picked up by a nearby air traffic control unit or an aircraft listening on it.

**Sea.** VHF channel 16 is the channel for distress calls at sea (see above).

## Per person

| Who | What fits |
| --- | --- |
| Children | Teach them: stay where you are, find a tree (or a bench, lamp post, large stone) and stay there. Listen for your name being called. Wave your arms, blow your whistle, flash your torch. Your family is not angry. A whistle belongs in the kit. |
| Older people and people with limited mobility | A whistle and a torch take little strength. Shouting wears you out. |
| Those in a group | Stay together. If someone has to leave the group, do it with a buddy. If someone is injured, send two or more people to raise the alarm; the rest stay with the injured person. |
