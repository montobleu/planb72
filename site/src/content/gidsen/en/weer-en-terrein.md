## The RMI warning codes

The RMI issues warnings several times a day for dangerous **wind, rain, thunderstorms, slippery conditions, fog, storm surge, cold and heat**. They apply per province and for a specific period. The colours have been agreed at European level.

| Code | Meaning | When issued |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Green** | Nothing in particular | |
| **Yellow** | **Be vigilant** | At the earliest 48 hours in advance |
| **Orange** | **Be prepared and follow the advice** | At the earliest 24 hours in advance |
| **Red** | **Take action and follow the advice strictly** | At the earliest 12 hours in advance |

In case of great uncertainty, a code can also be issued at shorter notice.

**How you receive the warnings**

1. **Radio.** The Crisis Center names radio and TV as the first way to follow the weather forecast. During a power cut, the internet and mobile networks may fail: keep a battery radio, a wind-up radio or the car radio to hand (Crisis Center, FPS Economy).
2. **If the internet works,** also check the RMI website or app.
3. **Reading the weather yourself.** Clouds and thunder also tell you something, especially outdoors (see Reading clouds and thunderstorms).

**How to read a warning**

- "Very locally" is less than a quarter of a province, "locally" a quarter to half, "widespread" at least half.
- A colour code for a province means that the threshold of that colour will be exceeded with at least a 65 per cent probability over at least a quarter of the province, or that the threshold of the next, higher colour may be exceeded over a smaller area or with a lower probability.
- A first warning often applies to a larger area. As the event draws closer, it becomes more precise.
- **The text from the forecasting office is the most important part**, more than the colour. Always read the accompanying text.
- The RMI forecasts the weather, not the damage. Whether there will be flooding also depends on local conditions.
- The RMI also issues a warning for **carbon monoxide poisoning** (without a colour) when windless weather with a strong temperature inversion hinders the discharge of flue gases.

**Thresholds per colour** (as published by the RMI)

| Phenomenon | Yellow | Orange | Red |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wind | Gusts of 80 to 100 km/h (70 to 90 km/h with leaves on the trees, 15 April to 15 November) | Widespread 101 to 130 km/h (91 to 120 km/h with leaves) | Widespread more than 130 km/h (more than 120 km/h with leaves) |
| Rain | 20 to 30 l/m² in 1 hour, or 20 to 40 in 6 hours, or 25 to 50 in 24 hours | 31 to 50 l/m² in 1 hour, or 41 to 60 in 6 hours, or 51 to 100 in 24 hours | Already major flooding and more heavy rain on the way, or more than 50 in 1 hour, 60 in 6 hours or 100 in 24 hours |
| Thunderstorm | Rain as for yellow, or locally severe gusts, or hail of 1 to 2 cm | Rain as for orange, or widespread severe gusts, or hail of 3 to 5 cm | Rain as for red, or widespread extreme gusts, or hail larger than 5 cm |
| Slippery conditions | 1 to 5 cm of fresh snow in 6 hours, or 5 to 10 cm in 24 hours, or locally freezing rain, patches of hoar frost or ice | 3 to 5 cm in 1 hour, or 6 to 10 cm in 6 hours, or 11 to 25 cm in 24 hours, or widespread freezing rain or patches of ice | Very slippery everywhere and fresh snow, or more than 5 cm in 1 hour, 10 cm in 6 hours or 25 cm in 24 hours, or intense general freezing rain |
| Fog | Widespread visibility below 200 m, or locally below 50 m | Widespread visibility below 50 m | No red code for fog |
| Storm surge | 5.60 to 6.20 m TAW at Ostend, or 6.60 to 7.20 m TAW at Antwerp | 6.21 to 6.50 m TAW at Ostend, or 7.21 to 7.50 m TAW at Antwerp | More than 6.50 m TAW at Ostend, or more than 7.50 m TAW at Antwerp |

Hourly amounts are forecast only one hour in advance.

**What the words mean**

- **Freezing rain**: rain or wet snow that freezes on contact.
- **Patches of hoar frost**: hoar frost or moisture freezing on a road surface that was not wet from a shower.
- **Patches of ice**: wet patches or remaining snow that freeze later.

**What to do with orange or red for storm or flooding:** if you need the fire service but nobody's life is in danger, use the online counter 1722.be if the network works, or call 1722 if that number is active. Call 112 only if a life is in danger or a rescue is needed. If you cannot get through: have neighbours or bystanders call or ask for help nearby (see Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call).

**Note:** the Agentschap Natuur en Bos has a **separate** colour code for the risk of wildfires (green, yellow, orange, red). It is independent of the RMI codes.

## Reading clouds and thunderstorms

Outdoors, or without radio and internet, you can watch for signs in the sky yourself. PlanB72 includes only weather signs that the RMI itself describes. We leave out popular weather lore.

- **Small tufts of cloud with turrets** (altocumulus castellanus, a type of high heaped cloud) indicate that thunderstorms may follow later in the day.
- **Clouds with strong, column-shaped vertical growth** (cumulonimbus, the actual thunderclouds) announce that a thunderstorm is possible very soon or nearby. Thunderstorms always go with a cumulonimbus.
- **Thunder** is rarely heard further away than 10 km.
- **A hissing sound** indicates immediate lightning danger: adopt the safety position at once.
- **10-second rule**: count the seconds between the flash and the thunder and divide by three for the distance in km. If you count 10 seconds or less, the storm is dangerously close: look for a safe shelter.

**When are there thunderstorms?** On average there are thunderstorms in Belgium on about 95 days a year, most from May to September inclusive. In summer there are more thunderstorms inland, in autumn and early winter more at the coast, because the seawater is then still relatively warm. Thunderstorms arise mainly when the atmosphere is unstable, such as on days with warm, moist air near the ground or very cold air higher up.

**What to do:** go inside and stay inside, windows and doors closed. Outside: shelter in a building (away from the windows) or a car with a metal body. Avoid metal structures, trees, summits and hills, fences, forest edges and open plains. Do not walk during a lightning strike: stand still and crouch. More in the guide on dangers in nature.

## Storm and wind

| Name | Average wind speed |
| --- | --- |
| Storm | From 75 km/h (9 Beaufort) |
| Severe storm | From 89 km/h (10 Beaufort) |
| Violent storm | From 103 km/h (11 Beaufort) |
| Hurricane (force) | From 117 km/h (12 Beaufort) |

**Preparing:** secure loose objects or bring them inside (garden furniture, trampoline). Keep an eye on the weather forecast, for example via the radio, TV, or the RMI's website or app. Keep pets inside.

**During:** go inside and stay inside. Close windows and doors. Listen to the weather forecast and follow the recommendations.

**If you have to travel anyway**

- **Never shelter under a tree** in strong winds.
- Do not drive on flooded roads.
- Do not park near trees.
- If there is lightning: stay away from metal structures, do not walk, stand still and crouch.

**Outside in a forest or in the field:** the US Army recommends not building your shelter under dead trees or loose branches that could fall. For the RMI wind codes, the threshold is lower between 15 April and 15 November, because the trees are then in leaf.

## Flood-prone places

**Three types of flooding in Belgium**

| Type | What | Where and why it is dangerous |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **River** (fluvial) | A river bursts its banks and floods lower-lying land | Along rivers and in valleys |
| **Rain** (pluvial) | More rain than the soil or sewers can absorb | Water in the street is usually a slow process. **Flash floods** after very heavy rain rise very quickly, carry debris along and have a strong current, especially where the valley floor lies much lower than the surroundings. |
| **Sea** (coastal) | Storm surge and strong wind together with high tide | Waves can overtop dykes, erode dunes and flood quays. Areas below or just above sea level, such as the **Flemish Polders**, are most at risk. |

**Knowing whether you live or are staying in a flood-prone area**

Look this up in advance, while the internet works:

- On the **Watertoets map** of Waterinfo Vlaanderen you can see the flood-prone areas (2019 version) and the maps that go with the Watertoets decree.
- The Crisis Center recommends finding out about the zones that are vulnerable to flooding.
- During high water, **Waterinfo** has current water levels and forecasts for flooding, precipitation, drought and tides, as long as the internet works. Otherwise: listen to the radio (see How you receive the warnings).

**Outside in the field**

- Do not choose your sleeping place or shelter where water can rise quickly, not in the bed of a stream, and not by water below the high-water mark.
- Limit your movements and avoid dangerous zones.
- Do **not** walk, swim or drive through floodwater: just 15 cm of moving water can knock you over and 30 cm can sweep a car away.
- Stay away from bridges over fast-flowing water.

**At home**: see the scenario on flooding. In short: keep gutters clear, sandbags and watertight barriers, put papers and valuables up high, know where to shut off your gas and electricity, do not leave the car in the garage.

Source: [National Crisis Center: Fluvial flooding](https://crisiscenter.be/en/risks-belgium/natural-risks/fluvial-riverine-flood) · [National Crisis Center: Pluvial flooding](https://crisiscenter.be/en/risks-belgium/natural-risks/surface-water-flooding) · [National Crisis Center: Coastal flooding](https://crisiscenter.be/en/risks-belgium/natural-risks/coastal-flood) · [Waterinfo Vlaanderen: Watertoets](https://waterinfo.vlaanderen.be/watertoets) · [Vlaamse Milieumaatschappij: Tips for floods](https://vmm.vlaanderen.be/tips/water/overstromingen) · [Ready.gov: Floods](https://www.ready.gov/floods) · [US Army FM 21-76](https://archive.org/details/fm-21-76-survival-manual)

## Coast and tides

**How tides work.** Ebb and flood are caused by the gravitational pull of the moon, and to a lesser extent of the sun. Along the Dutch coast there are two high tides and two low tides every 24 hours and 50 minutes; one tidal cycle lasts about 12 hours and 25 minutes.

| Tide | When | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Spring tide** | About two days after full and new moon | High tide extra high, low tide extra low |
| **Neap tide** | A few days after the first and last quarter | High tide less high, low tide less low |

The weather can make the water level differ from the calculated tide. In a storm the water can come higher: the RMI then issues a warning for **storm surge** (see the table of thresholds above).

**Where to find the data for the Belgian coast**

- **Getijtafels Kust** (coastal tide tables): the times of high and low tide, published each year by the Flemish government. Look them up in advance or print them: then you also have them without internet.
- **kustweerbericht.be**, if the internet works: sea and coastal weather forecast, waves, tide, wind and visibility at sea.

**Swimming in the sea**

- Swim only where **lifeguard posts are open**. In July and August there are 82 along the coast, every day from 10:30 to 18:30. In May, June and from 1 to 15 September there are fewer, sometimes only at weekends.
- Look at the **signs and coloured flags** on the beach: they show where and when you may swim.
- Water quality at the sea usually deteriorates after heavy showers.

**In a storm surge or coastal flood**: follow the instructions of the authorities. The Polders are the most vulnerable.

## Crossing rivers and streams: do not do it

PlanB72 does not give techniques for crossing a river. Military survival manuals do describe them, for soldiers who are trained and equipped. For civilians, the sources say:

- **Do not walk, swim or drive through moving water.** Just 15 cm of moving water can knock you over. Turn back.
- In Belgium, **swimming in navigable waterways** (rivers, canals, harbour docks) is prohibited where it is not permitted. Many bodies of water in nature areas are polluted, too deep, too cold or dangerous in some other way.
- Bridges over fast-flowing water can be washed away without warning.
- The military manuals also name places to avoid: a rock ledge across the river (often indicates dangerous rapids), a river mouth (wide, strong current, tides), and whirlpools (which can pull you under water).

**Someone has fallen into the water:** see to your own safety, try to rescue without going into the water yourself, call 112 or have someone call. If you cannot get through, send someone to fetch help (see the guide on dangers in nature, If you cannot get through). Only go into the water if you are trained for it: a drowning person who is conscious will cling to the rescuer in panic.

## Snow, freezing rain and slippery conditions in Belgium

- Stay inside as much as possible. Listen to the weather forecast and follow the warnings about road safety.
- If you have to go outside: wear several layers, cover your whole body, wear a hat.
- **By car:** watch your speed, be extra careful on bends. Put a blanket, an ice scraper and a small bottle of water in the car. Take hot drinks or water with you for when you are stuck in a traffic jam.
- **By bicycle:** watch your speed and keep your distance, so that you have to brake less.
- If the network works, report slippery roads via the RMI app, so that gritting services can respond faster.

Source: [National Crisis Center: Snow](https://crisiscenter.be/en/risks-belgium/natural-risks/snow) · [RMI: Explanation of our warnings](https://www.meteo.be/en/weather/warnings/info-warnings)

## Mountains and snow: for those travelling abroad

**Emergency numbers.** 112 works throughout the EU and in Switzerland. In Switzerland you can also call the air rescue service Rega directly on 1414, or from abroad on +41 333 333 333; Rega itself mainly recommends its emergency app. The German Alpenverein recommends saving the numbers of the local mountain rescue in your phone. Without signal: the alpine distress signal (six signals per minute, see the guide on signals). If you are in a group and someone is injured, send two or more people to raise the alarm; the others stay with the injured person (see the guide on the emergency call).

**The European avalanche danger scale** (EAWS, used in all Alpine countries)

| Level | Name | What it means | How often |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Low | Generally stable. Triggering an avalanche is usually only possible with a high additional load on a few very steep, extreme spots. Only small and medium-sized natural avalanches. | About 20 per cent of the season, about 5 per cent of avalanche deaths |
| 2 | Moderate | Increased danger in certain places. Triggering possible, mainly with a high additional load on the indicated steep slopes. Often no alarm signs, which does not mean it is safe. | About 50 per cent of the season, about 30 per cent of deaths |
| 3 | Considerable | Dangerous. Triggering possible, even with a low additional load. Recent avalanches, cracks and "whumpf" sounds. | About 30 per cent of the season, about 50 per cent of deaths |
| 4 | High | Very dangerous. Triggering likely, even with a low load, on many steep slopes. Many large natural avalanches possible. | A few days per winter, about 10 per cent of deaths |
| 5 | Very high | Exceptional. Numerous very large natural avalanches, even on moderately steep slopes. | Very rarely, about 1 per cent of deaths (SLF) |

In practice, the most dangerous level for those going off-piste is level 3: that is where most deaths occur.

**What the Swiss avalanche institute SLF recommends**

| Level | Advice |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Extremely steep slopes one at a time. Watch out for the danger of falling. |
| 2 | Plan your route carefully, especially on slopes with the aspect and altitude mentioned. Very steep slopes one at a time. |
| 3 | The most critical level for those going off-piste. Choose the safest possible route and limit your risk. Avoid very steep slopes with the aspect and altitude mentioned. **Those who are inexperienced stay on the open pistes and routes.** |
| 4 | Stay on moderately steep terrain, avoid the runout zones of large avalanches. **Those who are inexperienced stay on the open pistes and routes.** |
| 5 | Do not leave the open pistes and routes. Avalanches can reach roads and villages in the valley. |

A danger level applies to a whole region, not to a single slope, and is always a forecast that you must assess on the spot.

**Moving in mountains and snow** (US Army)

- Avoid places where avalanches of earth, rock or snow can fall, and deep crevasses in ice fields.
- Lee slopes (the side sheltered from the wind) collect snow blown off the windward side and form slabs.
- A loose layer of snow at the bottom is more dangerous than a compacted one.
- Do not travel during a snowstorm.
- Cross thin ice by spreading your weight: lie flat and crawl.
- Do not cross glaciers during the day.

**Health during winter sports.** Cold and alcohol increase the risk of hypothermia; alcohol widens the small blood vessels in the skin. You can also get sunstroke in winter. After a fall: if in doubt, treat it as a fracture.

## Per place

| Place | What to watch out for |
| --- | --- |
| At home | Follow RMI warnings via the radio, or via the RMI website and app if the network works. In a storm: stay inside, secure loose objects. In case of flooding: 1722 if nobody's life is in danger and the network works. |
| On foot in forest or fields | In a thunderstorm: away from isolated trees, forest edges, open plains. In a storm: never shelter under a tree. No sleeping place in the bed of a stream. |
| In the car | Do not drive through water. Do not park near trees in a storm. A metal car is a good shelter in a thunderstorm. Blanket, ice scraper and water in the car in winter. |
| At the coast | Swim only in supervised zones. Look up the tide tables in advance. Follow storm surge via the radio, and via kustweerbericht.be and the RMI if the network works. |
| In the mountains | Read the avalanche bulletin and danger level. Inexperienced: stay on the pistes from level 3. Save the emergency numbers. |
