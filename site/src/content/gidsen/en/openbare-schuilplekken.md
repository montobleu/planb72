## What exists in Belgium

Belgium has no network of public air-raid shelters. The sheltering page of the National Crisis Center contains no list or map of public shelters. The Belgian sheltering advice is: go inside the nearest accessible building, your own home, your workplace or another building, and stay there until the danger is over. If you are on the road, go into the nearest accessible building. If you cannot reach a safe building, for example on foot in a rural area or a nature area, call 112. If you cannot get through, have someone else call or ask for help nearby (see Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call). For people on the move with no building nearby, the Crisis Center also says: move further away from the emergency until you come across a safe and accessible building.

What does exist in Belgium:

- **Reception centres in an evacuation.** In larger emergencies, the municipality or province provides a place to receive evacuees, for example a sports hall or function hall. On site, a contact person provides information. That is accommodation after an evacuation, not protection against an explosion.
- **An information and reception point of the municipality.** For load shedding (a planned power cut), mayors and governors have prepared emergency measures, for example an information and reception point for the population (FPS Economy). Ask your municipal administration in advance which solutions your municipality has. The map in the app (Nearest button) shows the town halls.
- **Emergency plans of schools and communities.** Schools, companies, hospitals, sports centres and other places where many people gather are encouraged to have an internal emergency plan and to practise it. Within the nuclear emergency planning zones, they are also asked to provide iodine tablets for staff and visitors.
- **Let others shelter with you.** The Crisis Center explicitly asks you to let people who need a place shelter with you.

## What the possible shelters on the map are

Because there is no official list, PlanB72 shows places from OpenStreetMap, the open world map maintained by volunteers. These are the same kinds of places that foreign authorities mention as shelter during an air attack: underground car parks, metro stations, tunnels, underpasses and cellars. Each place gets the label: **"Possible shelter. Not officially designated. Access and condition unknown."**

The count for Belgium on 17 November 2026:

| Pedestrian tunnel, underpass | 2,551 | Footpath tagged as tunnel, at least 10 metres |
| Historic bunker | 2,517 | military=bunker or historic=bunker |
| Railway tunnel | 592 | Railway tagged as tunnel, at least 50 metres |
| Underground car park | 573 | Car park with parking=underground or location=underground |

Of those 6,907 places, 59 have opening hours filled in and 632 have access filled in (for example public, private or customers only). For the others, you do not know whether you can get in.

## Per type of place: what foreign authorities say

### Underground car park

- Germany mentions underground garages as good protection against explosions and radioactive substances. Thick walls and earth stop blast waves and radiation. Stay well away from open entrances.
- Sweden mentions garages as a protected place during an air-raid alert.
- The Netherlands (TNO) mentions an underground car park as the first choice for people who are outside during an air threat.
- Poland includes solid underground garages in its list of shelter points.
- Ukraine mentions underground car parks among the simplest shelters, usable during short periods of shelling.

### Metro station and underground station

- Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands (TNO) and Poland mention underground metro stations.
- Ukraine: in Kyiv, Kharkiv and Dnipro, metro stations serve as shelters day and night. The platform is the shelter area. Note: in an emergency, the metro serves first and foremost as transport to a safe place. If you go to a station, do not count on staying there: you may be taken to another place.
- In Belgium, no station has been officially designated as a shelter: there is no official list.

### Pedestrian tunnel and underpass

- Sweden: tunnels and walls offer some protection.
- Ukraine and Poland explicitly mention underpasses.
- Germany warns: avoid underpasses in high water and heavy rain.

### Road and railway tunnel

Sweden mentions tunnels as a place with some protection. In a fire in a tunnel, the opposite applies: drive out if you can, and if that is not possible, leave your vehicle and walk against the direction of the smoke to an emergency exit (Crisis Center). On the PlanB72 map, road and railway tunnels therefore get a warning: traffic, smoke, trains and live rails are a risk. Do not walk on the tracks.

### Building with underground floors

Germany mentions cellars under public buildings as possible places of refuge. Ukraine recommends contacting the owners of underground spaces (shops, gyms, warehouses) in advance and noting down their telephone number.

### Historic bunker

OpenStreetMap says that the tag military=bunker is only intended for bunkers that are fully operational or still in military use, but it is also used for civilian bunkers. In practice, many old bunkers and forts in Belgium are on the map. PlanB72 therefore puts them in a separate class with the warning: often closed, overgrown or unsafe.

## When an underground place is dangerous

A place below ground protects against one threat and is life-threatening in another.

| Threat | Underground | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Air attack, explosion | Good choice | Earth and concrete stop the blast wave and debris (BBK) |
| Radioactive substances | Good choice according to Germany; Belgian advice: a central room on the ground floor of the nearest building | Thick walls and earth shield against radiation (BBK) |
| Flood, cloudburst | Never | You become trapped; risk of electrocution (BBK) |
| Gases and vapours | No | Most gases are heavier than air and collect in cellars and low places (BBK) |
| Fire, smoke | No, if there is only one exit | Smoke fills the space; Belgian tunnel guideline: away from the smoke to an emergency exit |

Other risks the sources mention:

- **Locked.** In Ukraine, a shelter is sometimes closed when the siren sounds. A car park may be locked at night.
- **Full.** Ukraine recommends noting down two or three addresses in case the first place is full.
- **Poorly maintained.** Ukraine warns against leaking, damp, poorly ventilated cellars and against cellars with pipes or hazardous substances. Lithuania: critics say that underpasses and garages are usually not suitable to withstand bombing, and that staying for more than a few hours is difficult without chairs, beds, heating and electricity.
- **No second exit.** The safest shelters have several exits (Ukraine).

## How reliable the map is

- **Not official.** No place on the PlanB72 map has been designated as a shelter by the Belgian authorities.
- **Incomplete.** OpenStreetMap contains what volunteers have mapped. A car park or underpass that has not been mapped is missing.
- **Access unknown.** For most places it is not known whether they are public, when they are open and whether you are allowed in during an emergency. In OpenStreetMap, access=private means that the public has no access, access=customers that only customers may enter.
- **Condition unknown.** The map says nothing about the state of a bunker, the ventilation of a car park or the solidity of a tunnel.
- **No right of access.** The official Polish map also states explicitly that it gives no right to enter places where restrictions apply.

So use the map as a starting point to explore your neighbourhood in advance, not as a promise that a place is open and safe.

## Exploring your neighbourhood in advance

These steps come from the Ukrainian, Swedish, Finnish and Czech guidelines.

1. **Look for places close to home, school and work.** Sweden recommends looking for shelters or other protected places near your home, school and workplace.
2. **Keep the distance short.** The Czech Republic gives 500 to 800 metres as the recommended walking distance to a shelter. During an air-raid alert there is little time; Finland therefore says that the safest place to shelter is often where you are.
3. **Note down two or three addresses**, in case the first place is closed or full (Ukraine).
4. **Walk the route in advance**, so that you know how long it really takes (Ukraine).
5. **Check for yourself whether the place is usable.** Is it open, accessible, dry, ventilated, free of fuel or gas cylinders (Ukraine).
6. **Contact the owner or manager** of a cellar, car park or shop with underground space and note down their telephone number (Ukraine).
7. **Ask your employer** what to do in an emergency at work (Finland). In Finland, the emergency plan of a building should contain information about the shelter or the nearest public shelter.
8. **Put the addresses on paper** with your emergency plan. The Crisis Center recommends a paper list with important telephone numbers, because the internet and mobile networks can go down during a power cut.

### What to do if a place is closed

Ukraine gives this order:

1. Try to reach the owner and ask them to open the door.
2. Quickly look for another place nearby: metro, ground floors and cellars of buildings, underground car parks, underpasses.
3. If you are outside without shelter and hear explosions: lie down on the ground at once and cover your head with your hands. A kerb, ditch, concrete bin or staircase can give protection.

### Improving the map

If you know a car park or underpass, you can add the opening hours and access in OpenStreetMap. The next version of the PlanB72 map will include this. Other people's private cellars do not belong on the map.

## How neighbouring countries organise it

| Country | What there is | What citizens do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Netherlands | No network; technical advice from TNO (May 2026) to the ministry; emergency support points being set up | Follow Denk vooruit and the safety region |
| Germany | Construction and maintenance of public shelters stopped in 2007; dismantling halted in March 2022; inventory and national concept being developed | Cellar, underground garage, metro station; otherwise an interior room without windows |
| Sweden | About 64,000 shelters for about seven million people; ready within 48 hours | Go to the nearest shelter; you are not assigned one |
| Finland | Shelters in blocks of flats, shops, offices, schools and hospitals; public ones in large cities | Nearest shelter or the sturdiest interior room; often where you are |
| Switzerland | About 370,000 shelters with about nine million places; ready within five days | The municipality assigns your place |
| Poland | More than 70,000 shelter points in the "Gdzie się ukryć" application | Find the nearest point, also offline |
| Lithuania | Shelters (priedangos) for a few hours and buildings for collective protection for a few days, on lt72.lt | Find your nearest place on lt72.lt |

### The Netherlands

The Netherlands has no working network. In May 2026, commissioned by the Ministry of Justice and Security, TNO delivered technical advice on shelter options in the existing built environment during air attacks with explosives. In November 2026 the minister sent that report along with the second progress letter on the National Crisis Management Agenda, together with a timeline for emergency support points. The TNO advice works with a decision tree: outside, first an underground building such as a metro station or car park, then a building with a strong structure and robust façades, then a sturdy barrier or ditch. Inside, first an underground space such as a cellar, then an interior room with robust walls, then a robust object such as a kitchen unit.

### Germany

According to the Berlin district of Tempelhof-Schöneberg, there are no longer any public shelters in Germany that are ready for use. In 2007 the federal government decided to give up construction and dismantle existing facilities. The Federal Ministry of the Interior halted that dismantling on 17 March 2022. The federal government and the federal states are making an inventory and working on a national shelter concept. The BBK is planning a digital overview of all accessible places of refuge and is considering signposting.

### Sweden

There are about 64,000 shelters, with room for about seven million people, in residential and industrial buildings. They are marked with a blue triangle in an orange rectangle. In peacetime they may be used for something else, but they must be ready within 48 hours. You are not assigned a fixed shelter: go to the nearest one. During an air-raid alert you can also go to a cellar, garage or underground metro station. MCF publishes a map of all shelters.

### Finland

Shelters are located in blocks of flats and terraced houses, shops, offices, industrial buildings, schools and hospitals. Large cities have public shelters for people who are out and about. Anyone may use a shelter if there is room. They are marked with a blue equilateral triangle on an orange background. Without a proper shelter, buildings can be reinforced according to the instructions of the authorities.

### Switzerland

Since the 1960s, Switzerland has been building shelters with the aim of providing a place for every inhabitant. In about 370,000 private and public shelters there are about nine million places. In case of a threatening conflict, the municipalities assign the places; the shelters must be ready within five days. They protect well against nearby impacts and fragments, but hardly against a direct hit.

### Poland

The State Fire Service offers the application "Gdzie się ukryć" (Where do I take shelter) with more than 70,000 points, including underpasses, metro stations, tunnels and solid underground garages. The data are checked by the fire service and competent services. After downloading, the map works offline. Each point shows the access: open 24 hours, limited opening hours, or limited access where managers must let people in during danger as part of mutual assistance.

### Lithuania

According to the Lithuanian public broadcaster LRT, all shelters and buildings for collective protection are listed on lt72.lt, the website of the fire and rescue service. Shelters (priedangos) protect for a few hours. Buildings for collective protection, with a blue triangle on an orange background, can accommodate people for at least a few days. According to the Lithuanian Ministry of the Interior, there were 6,453 shelters for about 1.5 million people. The city of Vilnius says that a shelter must be ready within 12 hours of activation. The Lithuanian National Audit Office found that many shelters are not properly prepared.

### Ukraine as a practical example

Ukraine is not a neighbouring country, but has the most practical experience. Anyone may go to a shelter in any city or any building that is on the shelter map. Shelters must be open during every air-raid alert. The authorities recommend noting down two or three addresses in advance and walking the route. You can recognise a real shelter by a sign "Shelter" or "Civil protection facility", with the telephone number of the person who opens the door.

About metro stations as shelters, Ukraine says:

- During an air-raid alert, access is through a controlled entry point. Follow the instructions at the entrance.
- The platform is the shelter area. The staff will show you a place.
- After the alert ends at night, you can leave or stay until the morning.
- Identity papers may be checked: carry them with you.
- Do not leave the shelter before the end of the alert has been officially announced.

## What to take to a public shelter

- **Sweden:** water in bottles or cans, food you can eat at room temperature, valuables (identity card or driving licence, cash, bank card), mobile and charger or power bank. If you have time: warm clothing and toiletries. A shelter has water and basic sanitation, but no food or hygiene products.
- **Finland:** only take what is quickly to hand. Always keep identification, essential medication, your mobile, some cash and something to drink with you. Have ready at home what you need to leave quickly.
- **Ukraine:** during an air-raid alert, only take your emergency bag. In the metro, identity papers may be checked; always carry them with you.
