Water is more important than food. Without water from the tap you cannot drink, cannot cook, cannot wash your hands and cannot flush the toilet. This guide follows the order in which you need it: first know how much, then store it, then find water when your supply runs out, and only as a last step make water safe yourself.

Source: [National Crisis Center: An emergency kit at home](https://crisiscenter.be/en/what-can-you-do/build-emergency-kit/emergency-kit-home) · [BBK: Vorsorgen für Krisen und Katastrophen](https://www.bbk.bund.de/SharedDocs/Downloads/DE/Mediathek/Publikationen/Buergerinformationen/Ratgeber/BBK-Vorsorgen-fuer-Krisen-und-Katastrophen.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=44)

## How much water per person per day

The sources give different amounts. That is because they count different things: only drinking, drinking and cooking, or also washing and the toilet. In Belgium, the advice of the National Crisis Center applies.

| Source | Amount | For what | How long |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| National Crisis Center (Belgium) | 3 to 10 litres per person per day | 3 litres is just enough to drink and cook; the rest for washing and flushing the toilet | at least 72 hours |
| National Crisis Center, drinking water page | 4 litres per person per day | during an interruption of the water supply | a few days |
| Denk vooruit (Netherlands) | at least 3 litres per person per day | drinking and eating | 72 hours |
| BBK (Germany) | 2 litres per person per day, of which 0.5 litres for cooking | drinking and cooking | preferably 10 days, at least 3 |
| MCF (Sweden) | at least 3 litres per day | mainly drinking and cooking | at least a week |
| Ready.gov (United States) | 1 gallon (about 3.8 litres) per person per day | drinking and hygiene | several days |
| WHO/Sphere, survival | 2.5 to 3 litres (drinking and food), 2 to 6 litres (basic hygiene), 3 to 6 litres (cooking), together 7.5 to 15 litres | all basic needs | per day |

What the Crisis Center adds: an adult drinks 1.5 to 2 litres of water every day. The BBK gives an example for a family of three: 18 litres for 3 days, 30 litres for 5 days, 60 litres for 10 days.

According to Ready.gov, these need more water:

- Children, breastfeeding women and sick people.
- Someone with a medical condition.
- Everyone in hot weather. In very high temperatures, the need can double.
- Your pets. The BBK says explicitly: think of your pets too. See the guide on animals.

Ready.gov advises not to ration drinking water unless the authorities ask you to. Drink what you need today and look for more for tomorrow. Reduce what your body needs by moving little and staying cool. Do not drink carbonated or caffeinated drinks instead of water; caffeine and alcohol make the body lose fluid.

## Storing water

### Best choice: bought bottled water

The Crisis Center calls bottled water from the shop the safest to store: less chance of contamination with bacteria and a longer shelf life. Ready.gov says the same: keep it in its original, sealed packaging.

- Store it in a cool, dark place.
- Check the best-before date. The Crisis Center points out that it is often only one year.
- The German Bundeszentrum für Ernährung explains the difference. In a sealed bottle, mineral water keeps almost indefinitely. Manufacturers usually give glass bottles a best-before date of two years and PET bottles one of a year, because PET is less airtight.
- Do not keep PET bottles in full sun or in the car. A substance from the bottle can then get into the water and affect its taste and smell. According to the same source, this substance is harmless to health.

### Good choice: tap water in your own containers

Ready.gov describes how to store water yourself:

1. Buy containers that are suitable for food.
2. Wash them thoroughly with washing-up liquid.
3. Disinfect them before you fill them. For this, Ready.gov gives a solution of one teaspoon of unscented liquid household bleach to a quart of water (about a litre).
4. Fill them with water from the public mains.
5. Replace water you stored yourself every six months.

The MCF recommends containers or buckets with a lid, in a dark and cool place. Check once or twice a year whether the water tastes or smells strange, and change it if necessary. The WHO says about storing treated water: use clean, covered containers in a cool, dark place. A bucket with a wide neck and a tight-fitting lid is best, because it is easy to clean. A tap on the container prevents hands and cups from getting into the water.

### Extra: bottles in the freezer

The MCF recommends putting plastic bottles of water in the freezer. During a power cut they act as ice packs. When the ice has melted, you can drink the water. Do not fill the bottles to the brim, or they may burst.

### Where in the house

- The Crisis Center recommends a dry place and warns that cellars and garages are more prone to flooding.
- Denk vooruit mentions the cellar, shed or hall cupboard as a cool and dark place.
- In a flat: the Crisis Center suggests putting together an emergency kit with the residents of the building, for example in a cellar or another shared space.

### In advance or at the last moment

The Crisis Center recommends that, when an interruption is expected, you also have some empty, clean bottles or a jerrycan and set aside extra non-drinking water to wash yourself and clean. Collect rainwater for the garden, so that you do not waste drinking water.

## Order of use

Ready.gov gives this order:

1. First drink water that you know is not contaminated.
2. When that runs out, use questionable water (cloudy water from the tap, water from streams or ponds) only after you have treated it.
3. If you cannot treat it, put off drinking questionable water for as long as possible, but do not become dehydrated.

Treat water of uncertain quality for everything that goes in your mouth: drinking, washing or preparing food, washing up, brushing teeth and making ice cubes. The CDC adds: also do not make baby formula with unsafe water and do not wash your hands with it.

The WHO points out that only water for drinking and preparing food needs to be treated. That is still about five litres per person per day.

Source: [Ready.gov: Water](https://www.ready.gov/water) · [CDC: How to Make Water Safe in an Emergency](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/index.html) · [WHO/WEDC: Technical notes](https://wedc-knowledge.lboro.ac.uk/resources/who_notes/WHO_TNE_ALL.pdf)

## Finding water in and around the house

### In the house

| Source | Usable for | What you need to know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water heater with storage tank (boiler) | drinking, cooking, hygiene | FEMA calls the tank of the water heater an emergency source, provided it is part of your drinking water system and not of the central heating. A tankless (instantaneous) water heater does not provide this. |
| Water in the pipes | drinking, cooking | After closing the main stopcock you can let the water run out of the pipes (see below). First make sure the water source is not contaminated. |
| Melted ice cubes | drinking | Only if the ice was made from uncontaminated water. Otherwise treat it first. |
| Bottles in the freezer | drinking | Drinkable after melting (MCF). |
| Drinks and tinned food in the house | drinking | Sealed packaged drinks and the liquid from tinned vegetables or fruit, preferably packed in water. Caffeine and alcohol in large amounts make you lose fluid. Use nothing from packaging that has been under floodwater. |

The Regional Water Providers Consortium (a partnership of American public water utilities) describes the steps:

**Water from the pipes**

1. Check that the water source is not contaminated.
2. Close the main stopcock of the home.
3. Let air into the pipes by opening the tap at the highest point of the home.
4. Draw off water at the tap at the lowest point.

In a flat, the main stopcock is often not inside your home. How much water you get then depends on your floor and on how many others are drawing water from the pipes at the same time.

**Water from the water heater**

1. Switch off the energy supply to the appliance. For an electric appliance, use the correct fuse in the fuse box. For a gas appliance, the source says to turn the knob to the pilot setting.
2. Close the water supply to the appliance.
3. Let air in, through the pressure relief valve or by opening a hot tap in the house.
4. Put a container under the drain valve at the bottom of the tank and draw off what you need.

The water can be very hot. Wear gloves and safety glasses.

### Not for drinking

| Source | Why not | Usable for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Radiators and boiler of the central heating | FEMA and the CDC: never use for drinking, cooking or hygiene | nothing |
| Toilet bowl and cistern | unsafe to drink, even after treatment (Regional Water Providers Consortium) | flushing the toilet, cleaning |
| Swimming pool, water feature, hot tub | FEMA and the Consortium: not for drinking | sanitation (swimming pool, water feature) |
| Waterbed | unsafe to drink | sanitation |
| Floodwater | unsafe, even after treatment | nothing |
| Water with floating debris, a strange smell or colour | unsafe, even after treatment | nothing |
| Water that may be contaminated with fuel, heavy metals or toxic substances | boiling or disinfecting does not make it safe | nothing |

### Around the house

- **Rainwater.** The sources differ on this. FEMA says: do not use collected rainwater for drinking. The Dutch Waterschap Zuiderzeeland says that pets that go outside can drink rainwater, but that you should not drink it yourself because it is not clean enough. The Consortium lists rainwater among the sources you only use after treating or filtering. The Crisis Center recommends collecting rainwater for the garden, so that you save drinking water. So use rainwater first and foremost for washing, cleaning and flushing the toilet.
- **Streams, rivers, ponds, lakes, springs.** Only after treating or filtering (Consortium). The Waterschap Zuiderzeeland advises avoiding contact with water from ditches and canals during a prolonged power cut, because waste water is then treated less well.
- **Water from the authorities.** The Crisis Center writes that in case of a shortage a mayor, governor or minister can have bottled water distributed. The MCF says that municipalities can set up water tanks. You need your own clean containers for this.

Source: [FEMA: emergency water sources](https://fema.gov/node/what-are-emergency-water-sources-if-im-running-out-clean-water) · [Regional Water Providers Consortium: Emergency Water Sources](https://regionalh2o.org/node/39) · [CDC: How to Make Water Safe in an Emergency](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/index.html) · [Waterschap Zuiderzeeland: leaflet](https://bijlagen.nos.nl/artikel-24778431/260226_digitaal_toegankelijke_versie_folder_noodsituatie_Waterschap_Zuiderzeeland.pdf) · [National Crisis Center: Disruption of drinking water](https://crisiscenter.be/en/risks-belgium/societal-risks/failure-drinking-water-supply) · [MCF: In case of crisis or war](https://rib.msb.se/filer/pdf/31523.pdf)

## Purifying water: overview of the methods

No single method does everything. Ready.gov writes that a combination is often the best solution. The WHO says that household methods remove physical and microbiological contamination, but not chemical contamination.

| Method | Kills bacteria and viruses | Parasites such as Cryptosporidium | Removes chemicals, salts, heavy metals | Needed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Boiling | yes | yes | no | fuel, pot |
| Chlorine (bleach) | yes, most | Cryptosporidium is resistant | no | unscented bleach, dropper |
| Chlorine dioxide tablets | yes | yes, if you use them exactly according to the instructions | no | tablets |
| Chlorine or iodine tablets | yes | no, not against Cryptosporidium | no | tablets |
| Portable filter | bacteria (pores 0.3 micron or smaller), not viruses according to the CDC | yes (pores 1 micron or smaller) | no | filter |
| Solar disinfection (SODIS) | yes | not mentioned | no | clear plastic bottles, sun |
| UV device | yes, for small amounts of clear water | not mentioned | no | device, batteries |
| Distilling | yes | yes | yes, most | fuel, pot, cup |

Source: [CDC: How to Make Water Safe in an Emergency](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/index.html) · [Ready.gov: Water](https://www.ready.gov/water) · [WHO/WEDC: Technical notes](https://wedc-knowledge.lboro.ac.uk/resources/who_notes/WHO_TNE_ALL.pdf)

## Step 0: first make cloudy water clear

Almost every method works better, or only, on clear water.

- **Let it settle.** The WHO: let cloudy water stand so that larger particles sink to the bottom. Even after settling, you still have to treat it. Storing it for two days reduces the contamination further.
- **Strain it.** Ready.gov and the CDC: pour it through a coffee filter, kitchen paper or several layers of clean cloth, or let it settle and skim off the clear water.
- **Aerate it.** The WHO describes how to make iron and manganese settle out and improve smell and taste: shake a half-full container vigorously for about five minutes and then let it stand for 30 minutes.

Source: [WHO/WEDC: Technical notes](https://wedc-knowledge.lboro.ac.uk/resources/who_notes/WHO_TNE_ALL.pdf) · [CDC: How to Make Water Safe in an Emergency](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/index.html) · [Ready.gov: Water](https://www.ready.gov/water)

## Method 1: boiling (best choice)

Ready.gov calls boiling the safest method. The WHO calls it very effective, but it uses a lot of energy.

**How long.** The sources differ here. In Belgium, always follow the boil water advice of your water supply company.

| Source | Boiling time |
| --- | --- |
| De Watergroep (Belgium), boil water advice Roeselare, March 2026 | at least 5 minutes |
| RIVM (Netherlands) | 3 minutes |
| CDC and Ready.gov (United States) | 1 minute at a rolling boil |

**Method**

1. First make cloudy water clear (see step 0).
2. Bring the water to the boil in a large pot or kettle until it bubbles vigorously.
3. Let it boil for as long as the boil water advice says. Some water will evaporate.
4. Let it cool.
5. Store it in clean, disinfected containers with a tight-fitting lid.

**Improving the taste.** Boiled water tastes flat. Pour it back and forth a few times between two clean containers and let it stand for a few hours, or add a pinch of salt per litre (CDC). The WHO recommends shaking the cooled water vigorously in a closed container.

**With an official boil water advice.** In Roeselare, De Watergroep asked people first to let all taps run for at least 2 minutes, and then to boil water for drinking, preparing food, caring for wounds and brushing teeth. Washing, washing up, flushing the toilet and cleaning could be done with unboiled tap water. It remained better to sterilise baby bottles. The RIVM says that unboiled water is not dangerous for showering, brushing teeth or washing up. Here too the Belgian advice takes precedence: in Roeselare you had to brush your teeth with boiled water.

**If it is not possible.** If you have no fuel or no way to cook, go to chlorine (method 2).

Source: [VRT NWS: De Watergroep boil water advice](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/03/20/roeselare-vraagt-om-drinkwater-in-enkele-centrumsteden-tijdelijk/) · [RIVM: Explanation of boil water advice](https://www.rivm.nl/drinkwater/toelichting-kookadvies-drinkwater) · [CDC: How to Make Water Safe in an Emergency](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/index.html) · [Ready.gov: Water](https://www.ready.gov/water) · [WHO/WEDC: Technical notes](https://wedc-knowledge.lboro.ac.uk/resources/who_notes/WHO_TNE_ALL.pdf)

## Method 2: chlorine from household bleach

The CDC and Ready.gov describe disinfecting with ordinary liquid household bleach (sodium hypochlorite).

**Which bleach**

- Unscented. No scented bleach, no colour-safe bleach, no bleach with added cleaners (Ready.gov).
- First follow the instructions for drinking water on the label, if there are any (CDC).
- If there are none, look for the percentage of sodium hypochlorite on the label and use the table below.

**How much (CDC)**

| Strength of the bleach | Per litre of clear water | Per 5 litres of clear water (derived: five times the amount per litre) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 to 9 per cent sodium hypochlorite | 2 drops (0.1 ml) | 10 drops |
| 1 per cent sodium hypochlorite | 10 drops (0.5 ml) | 50 drops |

If the water is cloudy, coloured or very cold: use double the amount (CDC).

**Method**

1. Make the water as clear as possible (step 0).
2. Add the correct amount.
3. Stir well.
4. Let it stand for at least 30 minutes.
5. Smell it. Ready.gov: the water should smell slightly of chlorine. If it does not, add the same amount again and wait 15 minutes. If it still does not smell of chlorine, throw it away and find another source.
6. Store it in clean, disinfected containers with a tight-fitting lid.

**Limits**

- Chlorine kills most viruses and bacteria. Some parasites, especially Cryptosporidium, are resistant to it (WHO).
- Chlorine does not help against chemical contamination.
- The WHO warns that chlorine products differ in strength. Always use them according to the manufacturer's instructions and keep them in their original packaging. You cannot tell how much you need by looking at the product.

Source: [CDC: How to Make Water Safe in an Emergency](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/index.html) · [Ready.gov: Water](https://www.ready.gov/water) · [WHO/WEDC: Technical notes](https://wedc-knowledge.lboro.ac.uk/resources/who_notes/WHO_TNE_ALL.pdf)

## Method 3: tablets and drops

The CDC: follow the instructions on the packaging. Each product is made for a specific amount of water.

| Product | Kills | Does not kill | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chlorine dioxide | viruses, bacteria, Cryptosporidium (if you use it exactly according to the instructions) | | |
| Chlorine tablets | viruses, bacteria | Cryptosporidium | |
| Iodine and iodine tablets | viruses, bacteria | Cryptosporidium | Do not use if you are pregnant, have thyroid problems or are hypersensitive to iodine. No one should drink water treated with iodine for more than a few weeks in a row. |

Ready.gov generally advises against using chemical agents other than bleach with 5.25 to 6 per cent sodium hypochlorite. The CDC does describe the tablets, with the limitations above. The WHO mentions products that make particles settle out and disinfect with chlorine at the same time.

Source: [CDC: How to Make Water Safe in an Emergency](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/index.html) · [Ready.gov: Water](https://www.ready.gov/water) · [WHO/WEDC: Technical notes](https://wedc-knowledge.lboro.ac.uk/resources/who_notes/WHO_TNE_ALL.pdf)

## Method 4: filtering

**Portable filters (CDC)**

- Choose a filter with an absolute pore size of 1 micron or smaller to stop parasites such as Giardia and Cryptosporidium.
- Choose 0.3 micron or smaller to stop bacteria.
- Portable filters do not stop viruses. So disinfect the filtered water afterwards with chlorine, iodine or chlorine dioxide.

**Filters according to the WHO**

- **Ceramic filters (candle filters).** The water runs slowly through a ceramic candle. Some are treated with silver, which kills bacteria; in that case the water does not need to be boiled after filtering. Regularly brush the dirt off the candle under running water. Ceramic filters keep for a long time, so you can get them in advance.
- **Membrane filters.** Can be very effective, also against smaller organisms such as viruses. Follow the manufacturer's instructions; they often need to be cleaned.
- **Sand filters.** A container with layers of sand and gravel that the water flows through.

So the CDC and the WHO differ on viruses. Assume that a filter does not stop viruses unless the manufacturer explicitly says otherwise, and disinfect after filtering.

**No filter in the house.** A cloth, coffee filter or kitchen paper makes water clearer, but does not make it safe (step 0). Boil or disinfect afterwards.

Source: [CDC: How to Make Water Safe in an Emergency](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/index.html) · [WHO/WEDC: Technical notes](https://wedc-knowledge.lboro.ac.uk/resources/who_notes/WHO_TNE_ALL.pdf)

## Method 5: solar disinfection (SODIS)

The UV radiation of the sun kills pathogens in water.

**Method (CDC and WHO)**

1. Make the water clear: filter it, or let it settle and skim off the clear water.
2. Fill clean, transparent plastic bottles of one or two litres with the clear water.
3. Lay the bottles on their side in full sun. A dark surface helps (CDC).
4. Leave them for as long as needed (see below).
5. Let the water cool and shake it vigorously before use (WHO).

**How long.** The CDC gives 6 hours in sunshine and 2 days when it is cloudy. The WHO writes that the time depends on the transparency of the bottle, the strength of the sun and the clarity of the water, and gives 24 hours in sunshine and 48 hours when cloudy for areas around the equator. In Belgium the sun is weaker than around the equator. So take the longest time you can.

**When not.** With cloudy water that you cannot make clear. With chemical contamination.

Source: [CDC: How to Make Water Safe in an Emergency](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/index.html) · [WHO/WEDC: Technical notes](https://wedc-knowledge.lboro.ac.uk/resources/who_notes/WHO_TNE_ALL.pdf)

## Method 6: UV device

The CDC: UV devices only work on small amounts of clear water. Filter cloudy water first. Follow the instructions of the device. A UV device needs electricity or batteries; see the guide on light and energy.

Source: [CDC: How to Make Water Safe in an Emergency](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/index.html)

## Method 7: distilling

Ready.gov: boiling and chlorine kill most microbes, but distilling also removes microbes that are resistant to them, as well as heavy metals, salts and most other chemicals. You boil the water and collect only the vapour that turns back into liquid.

**Method (Ready.gov)**

1. Fill a pot halfway with water.
2. Tie a cup to the handle of the lid, so that the cup hangs upright when you put the lid upside down on the pot. The cup must not hang in the water.
3. Put the lid upside down on the pot.
4. Boil the water for 20 minutes.
5. The water that drips from the lid into the cup is distilled.

This gives you little water for a lot of fuel. It is the only household method the sources mention against most chemicals.

Source: [Ready.gov: Water](https://www.ready.gov/water)

## What does not help against chemical contamination

- The CDC: water with fuel, toxic chemicals or radioactive substances does not become safe by boiling or disinfecting. Use bottled water or another source and contact the health authorities.
- The WHO: household methods remove physical and microbiological contamination, not chemical contamination.
- The Regional Water Providers Consortium: water that you know or suspect contains fuel, heavy metals or toxic substances is unsafe to drink, even after treating or filtering.
- Ready.gov: only distilling removes most chemicals (see method 7).

In an accident involving hazardous substances or a nuclear incident, follow the instructions of the authorities on the use of tap water. See the scenario cards and the guides on sheltering and nuclear emergencies.

## After the interruption

The Crisis Center:

- If brown or cloudy water comes out of the tap, let it run for a few minutes.
- If little water comes out of the tap, the pressure still has to recover. It increases slowly.
- Keep using water sparingly. Do not waste it.
- After a flood: listen to the recommendations of the authorities on the use of tap water.

The RIVM: a boil water advice remains valid until checks show that the water is no longer contaminated.

Source: [National Crisis Center: Disruption of drinking water](https://crisiscenter.be/en/risks-belgium/societal-risks/failure-drinking-water-supply) · [RIVM: Explanation of boil water advice](https://www.rivm.nl/drinkwater/toelichting-kookadvies-drinkwater)

## Per person and per situation

- **Babies.** Do not make baby formula with unsafe water (CDC). Ready.gov recommends ready-to-feed formula; if you do have to make up powder, use bottled water, or boiled water as a last resort. With a boil water advice from De Watergroep, sterilising baby bottles remained recommended. See also the guide on food without electricity.
- **Pregnant women and people with thyroid problems.** No water treated with iodine (CDC).
- **Older people.** The German Bundeszentrum für Ernährung counts on 2 litres of drinks per day for people over 65 instead of 1.5 litres (see the guide on emergency supplies).
- **Sick people and people with young children.** Need more water (Ready.gov). The RIVM points out that contaminated drinking water can have serious consequences for young children, older people, pregnant women and people with a weakened immune system.
- **High up in a block of flats.** Denk vooruit describes that during a power cut no more water comes out of the tap on the third floor, because pumps stop working. The BBK says that the water supply may be limited depending on the floor. So build up a supply here in advance.
- **On the road or in the car.** The Crisis Center recommends bottles of water in the glove compartment or the boot, extra in summer.
