## What comes first

There is no fixed order that always applies. Put your needs in order of importance, depending on where you are, and adjust that order when circumstances change.

| Situation | First priority | Then |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Someone is injured | **First aid**, whatever the climate | Then the rest |
| Cold, wet or windy | **Warmth**: dry clothing, shelter, possibly a fire | Water, signals, food |
| Hot and sunny | **Shade** and **water**: look for shade, get out of the sun | Shelter, signals |

- In the cold, shelter can be more important than food, and possibly even than water. Prolonged exposure to cold makes you exhausted and weak, and an exhausted person can lose the will to survive.
- Even in the cold you need at least 2 litres of water a day to keep functioning properly.
- You can go several weeks without food, but without food you quickly become weaker, physically and mentally.
- Always leave a trip plan with someone who stays at home.

**Getting help.** Outdoors, help can take hours or days to arrive. Do not count on having signal.

1. If you have signal: call 112. See the guide on the emergency call.
2. No signal, but someone nearby: speak to people you meet and ask them to go to a place with signal and call 112 from there.
3. In a group with an injured person: send two or more people to raise the alarm. The others stay with the injured person.
4. Alone with the injured person: first help them as well as you can, then go and get help.
5. Nobody nearby: stay where you are and give signals (see Getting found).

## Recognising hypothermia

Hypothermia occurs when your body loses more heat than it produces. Below a body temperature of 35 °C, many body functions are at risk.

**What you see**

- The person is in a cold environment, or has just come out of one.
- Uncontrollable shivering and chattering teeth. If the hypothermia continues, the shivering gradually stops.
- Cold, pale, dry skin; later blue lips, ears, fingers and toes.
- At first faster, later slow and shallow breathing.
- Difficulty moving and speaking.
- Confusion, drowsiness, memory loss, strange behaviour, tiredness.

**Call 112 if** the person no longer shivers, the muscles stiffen, or there is impaired consciousness. If you cannot get through, see What comes first, Getting help.

**What you do**

1. Move the person carefully, without abrupt movements. Move them horizontally.
2. Check consciousness and breathing, and take enough time for it: breathing can be so slow that it seems as if someone is not breathing.
3. Take the person to a warmer place, or protect them from further cooling.
4. Wet clothing: carefully remove it completely, dry them, cover them warmly.
5. **Do not rub them warm.**
6. If the person is still shivering and fully conscious: let them warm up in a sleeping bag or under a blanket (with a survival blanket if you have one), cover the head too, give dry clothing and a warm, sugary drink or food.
7. If they no longer shiver or are not fully conscious: rewarm actively, preferably with an electric blanket, otherwise with heat packs, cherry stone cushions or a hot water bottle. Watch out for burns. In that case give **nothing to eat or drink**.

**Prevention: the mnemonic COLD**

- **C**lean: keep clothing clean. Dirty clothing insulates less well.
- **O**verheating: avoid it. Sweat makes your clothing wet and cools you down. Take off a layer or open your coat before you start sweating.
- **L**oose and layered: wear clothing loose and in layers. Several thin layers hold more air than one thick one.
- **D**ry: keep clothing dry. Brush off snow before you go into a warm shelter. Dry socks and mittens against your body.

Always cover your head: you lose a lot of heat there. Alcohol widens the small blood vessels in the skin, which makes you lose more heat.

## Choosing a site for your shelter

Look for a site as soon as you realise you need shelter. Stop early enough to make a shelter before dark.

**What the site must have**

- Material to make the shelter.
- Enough flat space to lie down comfortably.
- Protection from wind and cold in winter; in summer a place with few insects.
- A place where you can give signals.

**What you avoid**

- Places where water can rise quickly, and spots near water below the high-water line.
- Avalanche or rockfall areas in the mountains.
- Dead trees, loose branches and rocks that could fall on you.
- Low-lying ground such as ravines, narrow valleys and stream beds. At night the cold air sinks there, and there are more insects.
- Plants that are poisonous or prickly, insect nests, ticks.

**The most common mistake** is a shelter that is too big. It must be big enough to protect you, but small enough to retain your body heat. In the cold, that is vital.

Source: [US Army FM 21-76](https://archive.org/details/fm-21-76-survival-manual) · [US Army ATP 3-50.21](https://archive.org/details/survival-atp-3-50-21)

## Types of shelter

| Option | What you need |
| --- | --- |
| Existing shelter: a place that protects you from sun, rain, wind and snow | Nothing. In a thunderstorm, a building or an all-metal car is the safest shelter. |
| Lean-to with a tarpaulin or poncho | A tarpaulin, 2 to 3 metres of rope, a few sticks, two trees or poles |
| Hut of branches and leaves | Branches and a lot of dry material, time |
| Snow cave | Deep snow, time and energy |

**Lean-to with a tarpaulin or poncho**

1. Choose two trees or poles 2 to 3 metres apart.
2. First check where the wind is coming from: the back of the lean-to must face **into the wind**.
3. Stretch a rope at hip height between the trees, or tie the corners of the tarpaulin to the trees.
4. Slope the tarpaulin towards the ground and fix it with pointed sticks through the eyelets.
5. Tie a small stick of about 10 cm to each rope, close to the tarpaulin. This stops rainwater running along the rope to the inside.
6. If you expect rain or are staying longer, make a support in the middle.
7. Put branches, your rucksack or other material against the sides against wind and rain.
8. Put insulating material on the ground (see below).

**Hut of branches and leaves.** One of the best emergency shelters for warmth and simplicity.

1. Lay a long ridgepole with one end on a sturdy support (a tree stump, a fork of two short sticks) or tie it to a tree at hip height.
2. Lean large branches at an angle against both sides of the ridgepole, like the ribs of a wedge. Wide enough for your body, steep enough to shed water.
3. Lay finer branches crosswise over the ribs, like a lattice.
4. Cover everything with dry, light and soft material (leaves, grass, pine needles), **at least 1 metre thick**. The thicker, the better.
5. Inside, put down a layer of **30 cm** of insulating material.
6. At the entrance, have a pile of material ready that you can pull towards you to close the opening.
7. Finally, lay branches on top, so that the wind does not blow the material away.

**In snow.** Snow insulates well. A snow cave requires a snowbank about 3 metres deep, a lot of time and energy, and you get wet doing it. Keep the roof domed. Make your sleeping place higher than the entrance. Close the entrance with a rucksack or a block of snow. Always provide ventilation.

**Carbon monoxide.** Any open flame can produce carbon monoxide, even in a ventilated shelter. Carbon monoxide is colourless and odourless and can cause unconsciousness and death without warning. Always put out your stove or lamp before you go to sleep. Go outside at once in case of headache, pressure on the temples, burning eyes, a pounding pulse, drowsiness or nausea.

**What is allowed in Belgium?** In Flemish woods and nature reserves it is prohibited to put up tents or other structures, even temporarily, unless a management plan, the access regulations or a permit allows it. Access regulations may designate a bivouac area. In recognised nature reserves, tents and other structures are prohibited.

## Insulating yourself from the ground

- **Never sleep directly on the ground.** At rest you lose up to 80 per cent of your body heat to the ground.
- Put pine branches, grass, leaves, pine needles or other insulating material under you.
- In a hut of branches and leaves: a layer of 30 cm.
- In a snow cave or pit: put green conifer branches on the floor.
- If you have no sleeping bag, make one: dry material such as leaves, pine needles or moss between two layers of fabric.
- A down sleeping bag loses much of its insulation when it gets wet. Keep it dry.

Source: [US Army FM 21-76](https://archive.org/details/fm-21-76-survival-manual)

## Fire: safely and according to the Belgian rules

**The rules in Flanders**

| Where | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| In a wood or nature area | **Making a fire is prohibited**, except at a fixed campfire site included in the nature management plan or the access regulations. If there is no campfire site, you can ask the Agentschap Natuur en Bos for a permit. You always also need the owner's permission. |
| Outside woods and nature areas | Allowed at least **25 metres from the edge of a wood** and **100 metres from heathland**, with the permission of the landowner, who notifies the municipality in writing in advance. The municipality can impose extra conditions. |
| Smoking in nature | **Always prohibited** |

Even at a permitted site, the **wildfire risk code** of the Agentschap Natuur en Bos (per province) decides what is allowed:

| Code | What is allowed |
| --- | --- |
| Green and yellow | General ban on fires, except cooking fires (in all forms) and campfires |
| Orange | General ban on fires, except cooking fires as a gas or pit fire |
| Red | Ban on fire in any form |

Wallonia and Brussels have their own rules. Find out in advance about the fire risk and the colour code on the website of the Agentschap Natuur en Bos, Wallonie.be, the municipality or the nature site. On site: follow the rules and guidelines posted at the edge of the nature area.

**Making a fire safely**

- Choose a dry spot, out of the wind.
- In a wood or among shrubs, clear a circle of at least 1 metre and scrape away the top layer of soil.
- Do not use wet or porous stones around the fire: they can explode when they get hot.
- Keep flammable material away from the fire.
- Do not use accelerants, only a lighter or matches.
- Do not make the fire bigger than necessary.
- Never leave it unattended. Keep something to put it out with ready.
- Put it out immediately after use.
- Never make a fire in a closed shelter without ventilation (carbon monoxide).

**How a fire works.** A fire needs three things: air, heat and fuel. Take one away and it goes out. You need three kinds of material: **tinder** that catches fire from a spark (completely dry), **kindling** that burns quickly, and **fuel wood** that burns slowly and evenly. Build them up in that order, for example as a tepee.

**If you see a wildfire:** get yourself to safety. Follow existing paths away from the fire and the smoke, if possible at right angles to the wind direction. Flee to a public road, a large paved area or a built-up area. Help others to a safe place. Call 112 as soon as possible and give recognisable landmarks, such as paths, pools, lookout towers or car parks. If you have no signal, evacuate as quickly as possible and speak to people you meet.

## Finding water

- Almost every environment has water somewhere.
- Dew: tie cloths or tufts of fine grass around your ankles and walk through dewy grass before sunrise. Wring the water out into a container.
- Water sometimes collects in tree forks or rock crevices.
- Grazing animals (including deer) are a better guide to water than predators. Bees rarely go further than about six kilometres (four miles) from their nest and usually have water within that distance.
- Rainwater that you collect in clean containers is usually safe. Water from lakes, ponds, marshes, springs and streams must be purified, especially near human settlements.
- In Flanders, many bodies of water are polluted, too deep, too cold or dangerous in some other way. Pay attention to signs about blue-green algae.

**Never drink**: seawater, urine or alcohol.

Source: [US Army FM 21-76](https://archive.org/details/fm-21-76-survival-manual) · [US Army ATP 3-50.21](https://archive.org/details/survival-atp-3-50-21) · [Vlaanderen.be: Swimming or water sports safely in open water](https://www.vlaanderen.be/natuur-milieu-en-klimaat/wat-mag-u-als-bezoeker-doen-in-de-vlaamse-natuur/veilig-zwemmen-of-watersporten-in-open-water)

## Purifying water outdoors

| Method | What it does | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Boiling** (best choice) | Let water boil for a full minute. That kills pathogens, even at the altitudes travellers usually visit. | You need a fire or a stove. Chemicals remain. |
| **Filter** with pores smaller than 1 µm | Removes bacteria and parasites (protozoa) | Does not reliably remove viruses (for example norovirus) |
| **Chlorine or iodine** | Works against bacteria and viruses | Works poorly against Cryptosporidium at practical concentrations. Always follow the product's instructions. |
| **Chlorine dioxide** | Works against all pathogens in water, including Cryptosporidium | Follow the product's instructions |
| **UV light** | Kills bacteria, viruses, Giardia and Cryptosporidium | Works poorly in cloudy water |
| **Sunlight (SODIS)** | Water in transparent bottles, on their side in the sun | First make cloudy water clear; leave them longer when it is cloudy |
| **Filter and disinfection** | A microfilter together with chlorine or iodine, in either order, is an effective combination | |

**First make cloudy water clear.** Let it settle, or pour it through a filter of sand, crushed rock, charcoal or cloth. That makes the water clearer and tastier, but **not safe**: purify it afterwards. Cloudiness can make disinfectants ineffective.

**Chemical contamination.** Boiling and filtering do not remove chemicals. Activated carbon removes many chemicals and most heavy metals, but not microbes.

See also the guide on drinking water for water at home.

Source: [CDC Yellow Book: Water disinfection for travelers](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/preparing-international-travelers/water-disinfection-for-travelers.html) · [US Army FM 21-76](https://archive.org/details/fm-21-76-survival-manual)

## Food: be extremely careful

**You need it less urgently than you think.** You can go several weeks without food. Water, warmth and being found come first. It is better not to eat if you have no water.

**Mushrooms: do not eat them.**

- Do not eat mushrooms in a survival situation. Only a certain identification is good enough, and there is no room for experimenting. Some mushrooms kill quickly, and for some there is no antidote.
- There are **no tricks** to tell an edible mushroom from a poisonous one. Young mushrooms are not always edible. White mushrooms can be poisonous. A poisonous mushroom can taste good. Slugs also eat poisonous species. Cooking, drying or freezing does not make the poison of the death cap harmless. A single specimen can already be very serious.
- Identifying with a book or a photo is very dangerous.
- **In Flanders and Brussels, picking mushrooms in public areas is prohibited.** In Wallonia it is restricted: with the owner's permission, between sunrise and sunset, at most 10 litres per person, and not during the hunting season in state forests.

**Plants and berries**

- There is **no single rule** for recognising poisonous plants. The fact that animals eat a plant does not mean it is safe for humans. Cooking does not remove all toxins. Not all red plants are poisonous, and not all non-red ones are safe.
- Many poisonous plants look like edible ones. Hemlock, for example, looks like wild carrot. Some plants are only edible in certain seasons or stages of growth.
- In Flemish woods and nature reserves it is **prohibited** to pick flowers, fruits, mushrooms and other vegetation, and to disturb animals or their nests, unless a management plan, access regulations or a permit allows it.

**Why PlanB72 does not give an edibility test.** Military manuals describe a "universal edibility test" for unknown plants. The Poison Centre says that the only way to avoid poisoning is to learn to recognise the edible and the deadly species in your region. That is why we do not include such a test.

**Has someone eaten something anyway?**

1. Remove the plant remains from the mouth and give a glass of water.
2. Contact the Poison Centre (070 245 245, if the network works) or the GP.
3. Keep the plant: take a whole branch with leaves and flowers or fruits, or take photos.
4. For mushrooms: keep the remains (raw, cooked, peeled). The Poison Centre asks which mushrooms, where they were picked, how they were stored and prepared, how much time passed between eating and symptoms, and whether everyone who ate them is ill.
5. In case of serious symptoms: call 112. If you cannot get through, see What comes first, Getting help.

## Getting found

1. **Before you set off:** leave a trip plan with someone who stays at home. If you have to leave your home in an emergency, leave a note saying when you left and where you are going.
2. **Stay where you are** if you are lost. Only move if there is immediate danger to life.
3. **Make yourself visible:** wear bright clothing, lay out your tarpaulin or stones in a shape that can be seen from above.
4. **Give signals:** blow a whistle three times, pause, three times again. Flash SOS with a torch. Flash a mirror at aircraft. A mirror needs no battery.
5. **Save your energy.** Whistle instead of shouting.
6. Do not make a fire when the fire risk is high.

See the guides on signals and emergency codes and on navigation without GPS.

Source: [National Park Service: Outdoor Emergency Plan](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/healthandsafety/upload/NPS-Outdoor-Emergency-Plan-508c.pdf) · [Ready.gov: Evacuation](https://www.ready.gov/evacuation)

## Heat outdoors

Even in Belgium it can become dangerously hot outdoors. Heatstroke is life-threatening.

**Recognising it:** headache, seeing black spots, dizziness or fainting, nausea and vomiting, muscle or abdominal cramps, red skin, first heavy sweating and then no more sweating, convulsions, confusion or drowsiness.

**Call 112 in case of** impaired consciousness, abnormal behaviour, convulsions, if the person no longer sweats or cannot drink without vomiting, or with a body temperature above 39 °C. If you cannot get through: have someone fetch help and start cooling in the meantime (see What comes first, Getting help).

**What you do:** let the person rest in a cool place and cool them down immediately: a fan, wet cold cloths or cold packs in the groin, armpits and neck, spraying with cold water, hands and feet in cold water. If the heatstroke is the result of exertion and the person is fully conscious, give them something cool to drink. Do not give fever-reducing medicine.

**Prevention:** avoid heavy exertion in the heat, drink enough, look for shade, wear light clothing, do not drink alcohol. Never leave people or animals behind in a car in the sun.

Source: [Rode Kruis-Vlaanderen: First aid for heatstroke](https://www.gezondheidenwetenschap.be/eerste-hulp/eerste-hulp-bij-hitteslag-of-hitteberoerte) · [National Park Service: Outdoor Emergency Plan](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/healthandsafety/upload/NPS-Outdoor-Emergency-Plan-508c.pdf)

## Per person

| Who | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Children | Teach them: stay where you are if you are lost, find a tree (or bench, lamppost, large stone), listen for your name, wave, blow a whistle, flash a torch. Small children often put berries, leaves or mushrooms in their mouths; keep an eye on them. |
| Groups | Stay together. If someone has to leave the group, then with a buddy. Watch each other for cold and heat injuries. If someone is injured: two or more people go for help, the others stay. |
| People who are injured | First aid always has priority. Only move an injured person if there is immediate danger. |
| People who are alone | A trip plan with someone at home. A whistle and torch to give signals. |
| Pets | Take them with you in an evacuation and plan their care in advance. Never leave them in a hot car. |
