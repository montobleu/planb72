## Why this is needed

The National Park Service counts navigation (map, compass and GPS) among the ten essentials for anyone going into nature, and recommends practising with a compass or GPS beforehand and taking spare batteries. If your mobile phone or GPS fails, you have to fall back on a map, a compass or the aids that nature gives you.

Navigating without GPS combines three things:

1. **Direction**: with a compass, or without a compass with the sun, a shadow stick, a watch or the stars.
2. **Distance**: with your paces or by estimating.
3. **The terrain**: finding recognisable points from the map in the landscape.

Source: [National Park Service: Outdoor Emergency Plan](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/healthandsafety/upload/NPS-Outdoor-Emergency-Plan-508c.pdf) · [US Army FM 3-25.26](https://archive.org/details/milmanual-fm-3-25.26-map-reading-and-land-navigation)

## Before you set off

- **Take a map.** Print it, download it, or get it in advance. For walking, take a detailed topographic map.
- **Look at your route on the map.** Note landmarks along the way.
- **Practise with your compass** or your GPS before you leave. Take spare batteries for your torch.
- **Leave a trip plan** with someone who is not coming: where you are going, which route, when you will be back.
- **Take a whistle and a mirror.** See the guide on signals and emergency codes.
- **Wear bright clothing**, so that search teams can see you more easily.

**Maps of Belgium (NGI).** The National Geographic Institute makes four series of topographic maps:

| Series | Scale | 1 cm on the map is | Suitable for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Topo25 | 1:25 000 | 250 m | Walking and cycling. Map area 16 by 10 km. The NGI's base map; the maps at 1:10 000 and 1:20 000 are no longer made. |
| Topo50 | 1:50 000 | 500 m | Exploring a region. Map area 32 by 20 km. The fifth edition is expected for the whole of Belgium by 2028. |
| Topo100 | 1:100 000 | 1 km | Overview per province (nine map sheets) |
| Topo250 | 1:250 000 | 2.5 km | Overview of the country |

- Topo25 and Topo50 are made in the Belgian Lambert 2008 projection, with in addition a UTM grid (zone 31, partly 32) and geographic coordinates.
- The **legend** and the **magnetic declination** are in the map margin.
- Classic topographic maps show **no street names**.
- With the NGI's free **Topomapviewer** (web and app) you can view the maps, measure distances and save part of a map as a PDF to print. The viewer needs an internet connection; you can print the PDF in advance.

**Compass and metal.** Metal and electricity disturb a compass. The US Army recommends these distances:

| What | Distance |
| --- | --- |
| High-voltage power lines | 55 m |
| A vehicle | 18 m |
| Telephone wires and barbed wire | 10 m |
| A steel helmet or rifle | 0.5 m |

Check your compass regularly against a known direction. Also check that the needle turns freely and does not stick.

## Map and compass: the basics

**Three north directions.** On a map there are three "norths": true north, grid north and magnetic north (where your compass needle points). The difference between grid north and magnetic north is given in the map margin, in degrees. Use that value, not the drawing: the lines in the diagram are not to scale. If you do not correct for it, every direction you transfer from map to terrain will be off by that declination.

**Orienting the map with a compass**

1. Lay the map flat and horizontal.
2. Place the straight edge of the compass along a north-south grid line, with the top of the compass towards the top of the map.
3. Turn the map and compass together until the magnetic needle is under the fixed index line.
4. Then turn further by the value of the magnetic declination from the map margin. Now the map is set correctly: north on the map points to north in the terrain.

**Orienting the map with the terrain.** Find two or three points that you recognise both on the map and in the landscape (a church tower, a crossroads, a river) and turn the map until they lie in the same direction.

**Following a direction**

1. Hold the compass flat and still, between chin and belt, with your elbows against your sides.
2. Turn your whole body until the needle is on the set mark.
3. Choose a **steering mark** on that line: a conspicuous tree, rock, hilltop, pole, tower or building. Take the furthest and highest point that you can keep recognising clearly.
4. Put the compass away and walk to the steering mark. Do not walk with the open compass held out in front of you: that way it does not stay still and level and the reading is wrong.
5. Choose the next steering mark there. Repeat.
6. At night you only see silhouettes. Then choose steering marks with an even clearer shape.

**Getting round an obstacle.** Turn 90 degrees, walk a known distance, turn back to your original direction and keep walking, turn 90 degrees the other way and walk the same distance back. You are then back on your line. Example from the US manual: you are walking east (90 degrees); go 100 m south (180), 150 m east (90), 100 m north (360), and then on to the east.

**Deliberately aiming off your target.** If your target is on a road or stream, deliberately aim a few degrees to the left or right of it. When you reach the road, you then know immediately which way to go. Each degree of deviation shifts you about 18 metres per 1,000 metres. According to the manual, ten degrees is enough for most purposes.

**Navigating by the terrain (without much compass work)**

- **Handrail**: a line in the landscape that runs along with you, such as a road, railway, power line, stream or ridge. Follow it as long as it goes in your direction.
- **Catching feature**: a conspicuous point that tells you to turn, or that you have gone too far.
- **Attack point**: the last easily recognisable point before your target. From there, continue precisely with compass and paces. Preferably no more than 500 metres from your target.

Source: [US Army FM 3-25.26](https://archive.org/details/milmanual-fm-3-25.26-map-reading-and-land-navigation)

## Finding direction without a compass

| Method | When | Accuracy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shadow stick | During the day, with sun, 10 to 15 minutes' time | Simple and accurate |
| Watch with hands | During the day, with sun | Can be wrong, especially closer to the equator |
| Pole Star | At night, clear sky | Less than 1 degree from true north |
| Sunrise and sunset | Rough direction | Before midday the sun is in the east, after midday in the west |

**The shadow stick**

1. Push a stick or branch upright into the ground on a level spot where it casts a clear shadow.
2. Mark the tip of the shadow with a pebble or twig. **This first mark is always west**, anywhere on earth.
3. Wait 10 to 15 minutes until the shadow tip has moved a few centimetres. Mark the new tip.
4. Draw a straight line through both marks: that is roughly the east-west line.
5. Stand with the first mark (west) on your left. Then north is in front of you, east to your right and south behind you.

A line perpendicular to the east-west line is roughly the north-south line.

**Shadow clock.** Put the stick upright at the intersection of both lines. The western end of the east-west line is 6 o'clock, the eastern end 18 o'clock, the north-south line is 12 o'clock. The shadow is the hour hand. Each day thus counts 12 unequal hours, from sunrise (6 o'clock) to sunset (18 o'clock). Around midday the time comes closest to the clock.

**The watch (northern hemisphere, such as Belgium)**

1. Hold the watch flat and point the **hour hand at the sun**.
2. **South** lies halfway between the hour hand and **12 o'clock**. If summer time applies, take the midpoint between the hour hand and **13 o'clock**.
3. If you are unsure which side is north: before midday the sun is in the east, after midday in the west.

This method can give errors and make you walk in circles. The manual recommends setting your watch using a shadow clock and checking it again after an hour of walking.

**The Pole Star**

1. Find the Plough (the "saucepan").
2. The two stars at the front of the pan are called the **pointers**.
3. Draw an imaginary line through the pointers and extend it about **five times** the distance between them.
4. There is the **Pole Star**, the last star in the handle of the Little Bear. It is not the brightest star, but it stays in almost the same place all night.
5. Directly below the Pole Star, on the horizon, is north.

You can use a star low on the northern horizon as a steering mark for about half an hour. If you are heading south, check your direction every quarter of an hour.

Source: [US Army FM 3-25.26](https://archive.org/details/milmanual-fm-3-25.26-map-reading-and-land-navigation) · [US Army ATP 3-50.21, chapter 7](https://archive.org/details/survival-atp-3-50-21)

## Measuring and estimating distance

According to the US manual, distance is the biggest source of errors in navigation.

**Counting your paces**

1. Find an accurately measured stretch of 100 metres. It may also be longer, up to 600 metres: then divide your number of paces by 6.
2. Count how many paces you need for 100 metres. A pace is one ordinary step. Practise on terrain similar to the terrain you will be walking on.
3. On the way, keep track of how many times 100 metres you have walked. Do not keep it in your head: put a pebble in your pocket every 100 metres, tie a knot in a cord, or make a mark in a notebook.

Your pace changes. Count more paces per 100 metres:

- uphill (downhill your pace becomes longer);
- into the wind (with the wind it becomes longer);
- on sand, gravel, mud or snow;
- in rain, snow or freezing rain;
- with a lot of clothing or shoes with little grip;
- in poor visibility: fog, rain, darkness.

Example from the manual: someone who normally needs 120 paces for 100 metres may need 130 or more uphill.

**Estimating by eye**

- Learn to picture 100 metres in the terrain. Up to 500 metres, count how many times 100 metres lies between you and the point.
- Beyond 500 metres: choose a point halfway, estimate the distance to it and double it.

**Distance to a thunderstorm.** Count the seconds between lightning and thunder and divide by three: that is roughly the distance in kilometres. If you count 10 seconds or less, the storm is dangerously close.

Source: [US Army FM 3-25.26](https://archive.org/details/milmanual-fm-3-25.26-map-reading-and-land-navigation) · [RMI: How do I protect myself against lightning?](https://www.meteo.be/nl/switch-language/634?nis=21004)

## Reading and passing on coordinates

**Geographic coordinates** give a location in degrees (°), minutes (') and seconds ("). Each degree has 60 minutes, each minute 60 seconds.

- **Latitude**: how far north or south of the equator, from 0° to 90°. Always say **N** or **S**.
- **Longitude**: how far east or west of the Greenwich meridian, from 0° to 180°. Always say **E** or **W**.
- Belgium lies in the northern hemisphere and east of Greenwich.

**Grid coordinates** (Lambert 2008 or UTM on the NGI maps): always read **to the right** first, then **up**. First the east-west value, then the north-south value.

**Which system?** Lambert 2008 is the official Belgian coordinate system. It is aligned with the European ETRS89 system and uses the same reference model as satellite navigation. Worldwide systems such as WGS 84 also exist. The NGI offers an official conversion tool, cConvert. When passing on coordinates, always say which system you are using.

**Passing on your location to the emergency services**

| What you have | What you pass on |
| --- | --- |
| The 112 BE app | Call via the app: the emergency centre sees your location. Confirm it verbally. |
| A mobile phone with GPS | The GPS coordinates of your device |
| A map | The name of the path or area where you set off, your last known location, coordinates from the map if you can read them |
| Nothing | Recognisable points: paths, ponds, lookout towers, car parks, prominent features, the altitude in the mountains |

Also say what clothing you are wearing (colour), what you have with you, and whether anyone has a medical problem.

## Finding your way back and leaving traces

- **Stay on the paths.** In Flemish forests and nature areas that is also the rule: pedestrians may only use the designated roads and paths, except in marked-out play areas. The National Park Service recommends it so that you do not get lost.
- **Pay attention along the way** to path junctions, information boards and landmarks. Check regularly whether you are still on your planned route.
- **Know your route backwards** as well as forwards. Look back regularly: the way back looks different.
- **Leave a trail of information**: a trip plan with someone at home. If you have to leave in an emergency, leave a note saying when you left and where you are going.

## Lost: stay or go

**Step 1: stop and breathe.** Take a moment to think calmly. The US Army recommends not making a decision immediately after the shock: wait until the first fright has passed, then assess the situation and decide.

**Step 2: find your location.** Use your map, compass, GPS or visible landmarks.

**Step 3: retrace your steps**, if it is safe to do so, until you are back on your planned route.

**Step 4: call for help** if that does not work. See the guide on emergency calls.

**Step 5: stay where you are.** If you move, it is harder to find you. Only move if your life is in immediate danger.

**While you wait**

- Find shelter from sun, rain, wind or snow.
- Look at what you have with you and use it sparingly.
- Stay warm: put on extra layers if you get cold.
- Give signals: whistle three times, pause, three times again. Flash SOS with your torch. Flash your mirror at aircraft and helicopters. Lay out your tarpaulin or stones in a shape that can be seen from above.
- Save your energy and wait patiently.
- Do not make a fire when the fire risk is high.

**When to go after all?** The US Army gives as reasons to move: the place does not provide enough water, food or shelter, or you cannot communicate from there. Only go when you are sure that you can reach water, shelter, food and help, or when, after several days of waiting, you are convinced that no help is coming and you are equipped to travel. Do not leave a safe shelter to set off in bad weather. If you are injured or have a head injury, that limits your ability to travel and to make good decisions.

**If you do have to go**

- Use handrails in the terrain (a stream, road, path or power line) to keep your direction.
- Keep assessing your strength along the way: rest, drinking, eating, injuries.
- Watch whether the weather is changing.
- Stop early enough to still make a shelter before dark.
- Keep an eye on each other in a group, including for cold and heat injuries.

## Someone is injured

- Send two or more people to alert the emergency services. The others stay with the injured person.
- If you are alone with the injured person: care for them as well as you can and then go and look for help.
- Give the people who leave a precise description of the location.
- Only move the injured person if their life is in immediate danger.
- Only leave an injured person alone if there is no other option.
- Make yourself visible: lay out your tarpaulin or stones in a shape that can be seen from above.

## Per person

| Who | What fits |
| --- | --- |
| Children | Teach them: if you are lost, **stay where you are**. Find a tree (or a bench, lamp post, large stone) and stay by it. Listen for your name being called. Wave your arms, blow your whistle, flash your torch. Your family is not angry. |
| Parents | Make a trip plan and give it to someone who is not coming. Take the ten essentials (map and compass, light, first aid, water, food, extra clothing, shelter, sun protection, fire, tools). Keep the group together; if someone has to leave the group, do it with a buddy. |
| Groups | Stay together. Agree on a meeting place, as in a family emergency plan. |
| People going alone | Always leave a trip plan with someone who stays at home. |
| People who are injured or have limited mobility | Stay where you are and give signals. |

## By location

| Location | What the sources say |
| --- | --- |
| Forest or nature area in Flanders | Stay on the paths; that is also the rule. Call 112 with recognisable points (paths, ponds, lookout towers, car parks). In a fire, flee along existing paths away from the fire, preferably across the wind, towards a public road or built-up area. |
| Mountains | Give the alpine distress signal if you have no phone but are within sight or earshot of a valley or hut. When calling, give prominent terrain features, the altitude and if possible coordinates. |
| At night | Use the Pole Star. Your pace becomes shorter in the dark. Choose steering marks with a clear shape. Stop early enough to still make a shelter. |
| Fog, rain, snow | Your pace becomes shorter. Count more paces per 100 metres. Use the compass and handrails in the terrain. |
| In a thunderstorm | Count the seconds between lightning and thunder. At 10 seconds or less: find a safe shelter. |
