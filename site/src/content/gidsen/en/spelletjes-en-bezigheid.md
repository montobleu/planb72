For children, play is not a way to pass the time but the way they learn and process things. In stressful times, making time for play is one of the most important things you can do. Something similar applies to teenagers and adults: an activity that demands attention brings calm and the feeling of having some control.

This guide gives ideas without electricity, by age and by place. The rules of card, board and pencil-and-paper games are in a separate guide.

Source: [UNICEF and LEGO Foundation: Learning through play](https://www.unicef.org/southsudan/media/5371/file/UNICEF-Lego-Foundation-Learning-through-Play-2018.pdf.pdf) · [UNICEF: Activity guide for the ECD Kit](https://www.unicef.org/supply/documents/activity-guide-early-childhood-development-ecd-kit) · [Kind en Gezin: play is always valuable](https://www.kindengezin.be/nl/thema/spelen-en-bewegen/speel-en-beweegtips/spelen-altijd-waardevol)

## How much movement

| Who | Guideline |
| --- | --- |
| Toddlers and pre-schoolers (1 to 5 years) | at least 3 hours of movement every day, spread over the day; for pre-schoolers at least 60 minutes of that vigorous; no more than an hour of sitting still at a time |
| Children and young people (6 to 17 years) | light movement for most of the day, and on top of that an average of 60 minutes a day of moderate to vigorous activity; vigorous activity on at least 3 days a week |
| Adults | 150 to 300 minutes a week of moderate intensity, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous intensity; muscle-strengthening exercises twice a week; break up sitting every 20 to 30 minutes |

Helping in the house, fetching water and walking also count as movement. In an emergency, more is not always better: make sure you eat and drink enough, and do not exert yourself heavily in the heat or when you are eating little.

## What to set aside in advance

Put a small stock that needs no electricity in the emergency kit or in a dedicated box:

- A pack of cards, two or three dice, a few counters or buttons.
- Paper, an exercise book for each child, pencils, colouring pencils, a rubber and a sharpener.
- A ball of string, sticky tape, scissors with rounded tips, rubber bands.
- A soft ball or a ball of rolled-up socks, and chalk for outdoors.
- A few books to read aloud, and songs you have written down yourself.
- For each child, a familiar toy or cuddly toy that they choose themselves.

## Babies and toddlers

- **Peekaboo** with your hands or a cloth.
- **Naming and pointing:** objects, body parts, animals and their sounds.
- **A shaker bottle** with buttons or dried beans. Screw the cap on tightly.
- **The kitchen cupboard:** plastic pots, a wooden spoon and a saucepan to drum on. Putting in and taking out, emptying, hiding.
- **Building towers and knocking them over** with boxes or cups.
- **A row of chairs** to walk along, a tunnel of boxes to crawl through.
- **Make-believe:** the sofa becomes a boat, a box becomes a car.
- **Helping** to lay the table or do the washing-up: that is also play and movement.

Keep button batteries, balloons, glass and small objects away from children under 3 years.

## Indoor games

| Game | Age | How it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hot and cold | 2 to 6 | hide a cuddly toy; say "hot" or "cold" until the child finds it |
| I spy | 3 and older | guess an object from a colour or letter |
| Follow the leader | 2 to 7 | one child makes movements, the others copy |
| Musical statues | 3 and older | sing and dance; when it goes quiet, everyone freezes |
| Guess the animal sound | 3 to 6 | one child makes a sound, the others guess the animal |
| Obstacle course | 4 to 8 | over cushions, under the table, around a chair |
| Pass the whisper | 5 and older | whisper a sentence along the line; the last person says it out loud |
| Kim's game | 4 and older | lay out ten objects, look, cover them, take one away: what is missing? |
| Who am I? | 6 and older | guess a person or animal with questions that are answered yes or no |
| Charades | 7 and older | act out a word without speaking |
| Story in turns | 5 and older | everyone adds a sentence |
| Building dens | 3 to 10 | blankets over the table and chairs |

## Outdoor games

If possible, go outside every day, if it is safe. Always follow the instructions of the authorities: stay indoors if they ask you to, for example in the event of a gas cloud, a nuclear accident or a severe storm.

| Game | Age | How it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tag | 4 and older | whoever is tagged becomes the new tagger |
| Hide and seek | 4 and older | one person counts to 30 with their eyes closed; agree on boundaries |
| Grandmother's footsteps | 4 and older | whoever moves when the caller turns round goes back to the start |
| Captain, may I cross? | 4 and older | the captain names a colour; whoever is wearing it may cross safely |
| Hopscotch | 5 and older | squares drawn with chalk or a stick; hop over the square with your pebble |
| Skipping | 5 and older | alone, or with two people turning the rope |
| Relay race | 5 and older | in teams, with an object as the baton |
| Scavenger hunt | 5 and older | find what is on a list: a round leaf, a feather, something rough |
| Ball games | 4 and older | throwing and catching, dodgeball, football with two coats as goalposts |
| Cooperation games | 6 and older | untangling a knot of arms together, keeping a ball in the air without letting it drop |

**Safe outdoors**

- First check the ground for glass, rubbish, sharp stones, rubble, loose cables and fallen trees.
- Agree how far a child may go: within sight or earshot. Keep children away from water, damaged buildings and traffic.
- Let children drink regularly. In warm weather, play in the morning or evening and in the shade.
- Keep a first aid kit to hand.
- Make your own equipment: a ball of rags or old newspapers, bottles filled with sand as markers or goalposts, a kite from a plastic bag and string.

## Crafts with what is in the house

| Material | What you make with it |
| --- | --- |
| Empty boxes, toilet rolls | houses, cars, a puppet theatre, a marble run, binoculars |
| Newspapers, waste paper | origami, hats, boats, papier mâché, a ball |
| Socks, rags, buttons | hand puppets, a ball, dressing-up clothes |
| Corks, caps, bottle tops | counters, a morris board game, counting material |
| Dried beans or rice in a closed bottle | a rattle or musical instrument |
| String, yarn | plaiting, friendship bracelets, cat's cradle figures |
| Twigs, leaves, pebbles | collages, figures on the ground, a sundial |
| Flour, water and salt | modelling dough, not for eating |

Keep and display what children have made. Do not use glass, sharp tins or batteries.

## Storytelling and reading aloud

- **Reading aloud.** Read aloud every day, also to children who read by themselves: then choose a book that is a little more difficult than what they can manage alone. Reading aloud helps language, strengthens the bond and is a calm ritual before sleep. Teenagers and older people also enjoy a reading-aloud session.
- **For babies and toddlers**, singing, reciting rhymes and looking at pictures also count as reading aloud.
- **Telling without a book.** A fairy tale you know, or a story about the old days in the family.
- **Story in turns.** Begin with "Once upon a time" and let everyone add a sentence.
- **Story cards.** On cards, draw a character, a place, an object, a problem, a helper and an ending. Draw three and make a story with them.
- **Puppet theatre.** A box with an opening and hand puppets made from socks.
- **Diary and letters.** Older children and teenagers keep a diary, or write a letter to someone they miss.

Do not force a child to tell or draw what they went through. Let them choose for themselves.

## Music, dance and singing

- Sing songs that children know, ring songs and lullabies. Dance together.
- Make instruments: a drum from a pot or a box, a rattle, clapping and stamping.
- Teach each other a song from another language or region. In a group with different languages, that brings people together.
- Games with movement instead of words, such as musical statues or mirroring, also work when children do not speak each other's language.

## In a small space

- **Fixed places.** Agree where people sleep, eat, play and learn, even if that is the same table. Tidy up after every activity.
- **Alternate quiet and lively moments.** After a calm activity, a short movement break: stretching, running on the spot, dancing.
- **Taking turns.** Let children take turns to choose what is played.
- **One toy, two children.** Learn to play together with the same object instead of fighting over toys.
- **Quiet activities:** drawing, puzzles, reading, patience, plaiting, folding.
- **Exercises on a small floor:** seated stretching, arm circles, standing on tiptoe, calm breathing. That way older people and people with a disability can join in too.
- **Time away.** Give each family member a moment alone, even if it is in a corner with a blanket as a screen.

## In a reception centre or with many children together

A safe, supervised space for children gives them safety, structure and a bit of ordinary life. Parents can set one up together with volunteers, in consultation with the person in charge of the centre.

**Organisation**

1. **A safe place.** Remove glass, cables and other hazards. Provide drinking water, toilets nearby and a first aid kit.
2. **Fixed hours.** At the same time every day, matched to the meals and the daily schedule of the families. Put up the schedule.
3. **A fixed opening and closing.** For example a circle and a group song. New children learn this quickly.
4. **Age groups.** For example 0 to 3, 4 to 7, 8 to 12 and 13 to 18 years, with a corner for babies and their parents. Let older children help younger ones.
5. **Alternate free and guided play.** Set out different activities in different places and let children choose.
6. **A manageable number.** Two smaller groups rather than one large one.
7. **Regular supervisors.** The same adults, who know the children. Work in pairs: an adult is never alone with a child. Only touch children when it is in their interest, with an explanation.
8. **Positive discipline.** Never punish with violence or humiliation.
9. **For everyone.** Girls and boys, children with a disability, children who do not speak the language.

**Example of a morning**

| Time | Activity |
| --- | --- |
| 10:00 | welcome and circle |
| 10:15 | group conversation or song |
| 10:30 | activities in small groups by age; older children help younger ones |
| 11:30 | story in the circle |
| 12:00 | closing song and goodbye |

**Activities** that work in a group: singing, drama, dance, drawing, stories and reading aloud, sport, cooperation games, and a moment for reading, writing and arithmetic. Choose cooperation games rather than competitions with winners and losers. Keep a quiet back-up activity ready and stop an activity that upsets children.

**What not to do:** force children to talk about or draw difficult experiences. Difficult behaviour is often a way of saying that something is hard. Some children need more help than play: refer them to the care workers at the centre.

## Activities for teenagers

Teenagers want to be taken seriously. Give them real tasks and time of their own.

- **Responsibility.** Supervising a small group of younger children during a game, reading aloud, keeping track of water or supplies, writing the family logbook.
- **Sport and exercise.** Running, football, skipping, strength exercises with their own body weight. Agree on a fixed time.
- **Strategy games.** Draughts, chess, Hearts, Thirty-one, Battleships.
- **Learning for themselves.** Practising a skill: cooking on another heat source, first aid, map reading, an instrument, a language, drawing.
- **Writing and making.** A diary, stories, song lyrics, a comic strip, a small newspaper for the reception centre.
- **Together with people their own age.** Teenagers often prefer talking to each other rather than to adults. Allow that and stay nearby.

## Activities for adults

- **A fixed routine for yourself.** Getting up, tasks, movement, rest and sleep at fixed times.
- **Movement.** Walking, exercises on the spot, calm breathing or stretching and relaxation exercises.
- **Reading, writing and playing.** Books, a diary, card games, puzzles, crosswords on paper.
- **Passing on what you can do.** Teaching a skill to children or neighbours: cooking, sewing, repairing, gardening, a language, an instrument.
- **Handicrafts.** Knitting, sewing, mending clothes and tools, woodwork.
- **Helping others.** Running a games session for children, reading aloud to older people, helping out in the reception centre. Helping each other gives the feeling of doing something meaningful.
