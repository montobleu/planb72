If the power goes off, the heating often goes off too: boilers and circulation pumps need electricity. In winter, rooms then cool down quickly. In summer, fans and air conditioning do not work. This guide covers both situations.

Source: [National Crisis Center: OFF ON](https://crisiscentrum.be/sites/default/files/documents/files/OffOn-triptyque-N.pdf) · [BBK: Vorsorgen für Krisen und Katastrophen](https://www.bbk.bund.de/SharedDocs/Downloads/DE/Mediathek/Publikationen/Buergerinformationen/Ratgeber/BBK-Vorsorgen-fuer-Krisen-und-Katastrophen.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=44)

## Cold: what to get in beforehand

- Blankets, sleeping bags and sleeping mats.
- Warm clothing: woollen clothes, thick socks, hats, gloves and scarves.
- Candles, tea lights, matches and firelighters.
- A fire extinguisher.
- Smoke alarms and a CO alarm.
- With a stove or open fireplace: a supply of coal, briquettes or wood.
- A heat source that works without electricity and is approved for indoor use, with fuel.

Have a professional check whether an alternative heat source can be installed in your home. Insulate the house with draught strips and sealing, and learn how to prevent pipes from freezing.

## Keeping the house warm: the options

**1. Keeping the heat in**

- Close windows and doors.
- Close off the rooms you do not use or rarely use.
- Hang blankets over the windows and put rugs on the floor.

**2. Living in one room**

- Stay in one room as much as possible and keep the doors closed, so the heat does not escape.
- Gather with several people to share light and warmth. Agree with your neighbours beforehand whose home you can go to and which things you will share.

Choose a room you can ventilate: even in one room, regular ventilation remains necessary (see below).

**3. Keeping the body warm instead of the room**

- With several layers of warm clothing, blankets and sleeping bags you can bridge the outage for a while.

**4. Another heat source**

- Avoid portable combustion appliances as much as possible. If you do use a gas heater, ethanol fireplace or paraffin heater that works without electricity, use only an appliance approved for indoor use and follow the rules for safe heating below.
- With gas appliances: make sure they have an oxygen depletion safety device and a flame failure device.
- A wood-burning stove or open fireplace, with a supply of fuel.

**5. Going to a warm place**

- If the cold is extreme, go to a place in your municipality that has electricity and find out which warm places are open.
- Mayors and provincial governors have prepared emergency measures for a planned power cut, such as an information and reception point. Ask your municipality beforehand what it has planned. During the outage: listen to a battery radio or the car radio and ask for help in your neighbourhood.

**What not to do**

- Do not heat your home with a gas cooker or oven. This increases the risk of CO poisoning, fire and burns.
- Do not bring a barbecue indoors for heating.
- Use a generator outdoors only. See the guide on light and energy.

## Ventilating, even when it is cold

It seems contradictory, but it is necessary.

- Ventilate your home for 10 minutes every day. Open the windows wide and then close them again, so the house does not cool down.
- If you burn candles, ventilate more, to refresh the oxygen.
- If you use another heat source, open a window for fresh air.

## Clothing in layers

- Wear several layers and cover your whole body: woollen clothes, thick socks, gloves, a scarf and a hat.
- If you have to go outside: limit your time outdoors and wear layers of warm clothing. Watch for signs of frostbite and hypothermia.
- Avoid overexertion when shovelling snow and walking in snow, to reduce the risk of a heart attack.

Source: [National Crisis Center: Cold wave](https://crisiscenter.be/en/risks-belgium/natural-risks/cold-wave) · [MCF: In case of crisis or war](https://rib.msb.se/filer/pdf/31523.pdf) · [Ready.gov: Winter Weather](https://www.ready.gov/winter-weather)

## Sleeping warm

- Sleeping bags, blankets and sleeping mats.
- Put out heat sources and candles before you go to sleep.
- Never use portable combustion appliances in a bedroom.
- Install CO alarms on every floor, in central locations, with a backup battery.

## Heating safely and carbon monoxide (CO)

In Belgium, carbon monoxide causes most fatal poisoning accidents. Every year a few dozen people die and around 1,000 are admitted to hospital. Most accidents happen between October and April. All heating appliances can produce CO, except electric ones.

**What CO is.** A gas without smell, colour or taste. You cannot detect it yourself. It is produced by incomplete combustion of wood, coal, natural gas, butane, propane and paraffin, when there is too little oxygen. 0.1 per cent CO in the air is fatal within 1 to 3 hours, 1 per cent within 15 minutes. Children are particularly sensitive. In pregnant women, prolonged exposure can harm the unborn child.

**By appliance**

| Appliance | Risk | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Portable paraffin heater, portable butane appliance, bio-ethanol fireplace | not connected to a chimney; uses up the oxygen in the room and the combustion gases stay in the room | do not use continuously; refresh the air regularly; leave a door or window ajar; not in small spaces such as a caravan; never in a bedroom |
| Barbecue indoors | smouldering charcoal releases a lot of CO, even with the garage door open | do not do it |
| Car or machine with a petrol engine in a garage | exhaust gases contain CO | do not let it run in a small or closed space |
| Wood-burning stove | damp wood disrupts the draught; ash up to the grate leads to incomplete combustion | only dry, clean wood; remove ash regularly; have the chimney swept once a year |
| Kitchen water heater without a flue | | use for no more than about ten minutes per half hour; never for a shower or bath; no cooker hood or extractor at the same time |
| Gas appliance | a yellow-orange flame indicates a lack of oxygen; a good flame is blue | with an orange flame, contact a professional |

Condensation on windows or walls can indicate that combustion gases are not being removed properly.

**General rules**

- Indoors, use only appliances approved for that purpose.
- Install tested, approved CO alarms in all useful places in the home and test them regularly.
- If you heat your home with gas, heating oil, an open fireplace or a coal, paraffin or pellet stove, take the necessary precautions against CO.

## Recognising CO poisoning and what to do

**Recognising**

- **Acute**, with a lot of CO: first headache and dizziness, great tiredness, nausea, vomiting. Anyone who does not realise they must leave the room loses consciousness and can then no longer save themselves.
- **Chronic**, with small amounts over a long time: vague complaints such as headache, a heavy stomach, muscle weakness, concentration problems, changing moods.
- **Suspicion.** It grows stronger if several people have the same complaints at the same time, or if the complaints appear in the same room and disappear when you leave it.

**What to do**

1. Never enter a room where an unconscious casualty is lying. Protect yourself.
2. Call the emergency services immediately, or have someone call.
3. Open windows and doors, if you can do so without risk to yourself.
4. If the casualty is unconscious: call 112 and say it is probably CO. Switch off the appliance if possible. Get the casualty out of the room. If the casualty is no longer breathing, give mouth-to-mouth rescue breaths; this is safe here. If they are still breathing, lay them on their side without a pillow.
5. If the casualty is conscious: call the GP, if the network works.

If calling does not work: try another phone or have neighbours or bystanders call, and say it is probably CO. If calling does not work at all, send someone for help to the nearest police station, fire station or hospital (the map in the app shows them, with the Nearest button). Leave the casualty alone only if there is no other way.

The Poison Centre can be reached free of charge day and night on 070 245 245, if the network works. Write the number down on your paper list beforehand. See also the scenario card on CO.

## Fire from heating and candles

- During a power cut, the fire risk rises due to incorrect or unattended use of gas appliances, candles and the like. Smoke alarms in every living space are an extra safety measure.
- Immediately after the power cut begins, switch off all electric heating appliances: cooker, oven, iron, coffee maker, kettle, electric blanket, fan heater. This prevents them from switching on uncontrolled when the power comes back.
- Keep extinguishing equipment ready: a fire extinguisher, a garden hose, a fire blanket.
- Never put out burning fat, wax or liquid fuel with water; the water evaporates instantly and causes a fat explosion.

More on candles in the guide on light and energy.

## Hypothermia and frostbite

| | Signs | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Frostbite | numbness; white or greyish-yellow skin; firm or waxy skin, especially on the face, fingers and toes | go to a warm room; soak in warm water; warm with body heat; do not massage and do not use a heating pad |
| Hypothermia | shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, drowsiness; a body temperature below 35 °C is an emergency | go to a warm room; warm the centre of the body first (chest, neck, head, groin); keep the person dry and wrapped in warm blankets, including head and neck |

See also the first aid pages.

Source: [Ready.gov: Winter Weather](https://www.ready.gov/winter-weather)

## Pipes and after the cold

- In severe frost, water pipes can freeze and burst.
- Take care when it thaws: frozen pipes may be damaged and leak.
- After a gas shortage or energy shortage, the authorities sometimes ask you to turn the thermostat down and use less hot water.

## Cold by person and by place

- **Older people, children, sick people and pets** are at greater risk in winter weather.
- **Pets.** Keep them indoors or make sure they have enough food and drinking water.
- **Family members who need care.** Prepare to look after them yourself for a while.
- **In the car.** Take a blanket, hot drinks or water with you, in case you get stuck in a traffic jam. If you are stuck in the car, stay in it. See the guide on the emergency kit for the winter kit in the car.
- **Outdoors.** Without a hat you lose 30 per cent of your body heat.
- **Flat.** Use the rules for one room and ventilation. Portable heaters without a chimney need extra attention to ventilation.

## Heat: staying cool without power

A heatwave is a period of at least five consecutive summer days of at least 25 degrees, of which three days are at least 30 degrees. A heatwave can also cause power cuts.

**Before the heat**

- Follow the weather forecast from the RMI, for example on radio or TV.
- If you are at risk, for example a heart patient or an older person, ask your GP about the risks.
- Ask your doctor or pharmacist whether heat can affect your medicines.
- Visit people who need help, to help them prepare.

**Keeping the house cool**

- Cover windows with curtains or shutters.
- Seal doors and windows with draught strips.
- Use reflective window film that bounces heat back outside.
- Use the oven less.

**Keeping the body cool**

- Drink enough, more than usual. Do not wait until you are thirsty. Water is better than soft drinks.
- Take a bottle of water with you if you will be on a bus, train or in the car for a long time.
- Wet your forehead and neck regularly, or take a cool shower or bath.
- Wear light, loose, light-coloured clothing.
- Wear a cap or hat; outdoors, a hat that protects your face.
- Avoid the sun and stay indoors until it is cooler. If that is not possible, look for shade.
- Avoid heavy exertion and sport, especially in the hottest part of the day.
- Use sunscreen with at least factor 30.
- If it is too hot at home, find a cool place such as a service centre, library or shopping centre.

**Fan.** Do not rely on a fan as your main means of cooling. It gives a false sense of comfort, but does not lower your body temperature and does not prevent heat illness.

**Never.** Do not leave people or animals in a parked vehicle.

Source: [National Crisis Center: Heatwave](https://crisiscenter.be/en/risks-belgium/natural-risks/heatwave) · [Ready.gov: Extreme Heat](https://www.ready.gov/heat)

## Recognising heat illness

| | Signs | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heat cramps | muscle pain or cramps in the abdomen, arms or legs | go to a cooler place, remove excess clothing, take small sips of water or a sports drink; call your GP if it gets worse or lasts longer than an hour, if the network works |
| Heat exhaustion | heavy sweating, paleness, muscle cramps, tiredness, weakness, fast or weak pulse, dizziness, headache, fainting, nausea, vomiting | same as for heat cramps |
| Heat stroke | | call 112 or take the person to a hospital; move them to a cool, shady place; remove outer clothing; put cold wet cloths or ice on the head, neck, armpits and groin, or soak the clothing with cool water; raise the feet |

If you have cramps, a high fever or a headache, or feel faint, call your GP. Call 112 only in a life-threatening situation. If calling does not work, see Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call.

Source: [Ready.gov: Extreme Heat](https://www.ready.gov/heat) · [National Crisis Center: Heatwave](https://crisiscenter.be/en/risks-belgium/natural-risks/heatwave)

## Heat by person

- **Older people, children and people with certain illnesses** are at greater risk.
- **Spinal cord injury.** The body sometimes cannot cool down by sweating.
- **Serious mental illness.** Medicines can interfere with the body's ability to cool down.
- **Dementia and intellectual disability.** A person may find it hard to understand or explain how the heat is affecting them. Ask carers, family and friends to visit regularly.
- **Electric medical devices.** Ask your energy supplier about priority during power cuts. If your medical needs mean you cannot stay at home and cannot go to an ordinary cool place, make a plan beforehand with a nearby hospital or care home. See the guide on medication and care.
- **Pets.** Provide plenty of cool water and shade. Tarmac can be too hot for their paws.
- **Neighbours.** Check on family, older people and neighbours.

After the heat: see your GP if you continue to feel unwell, keep drinking and get enough rest.
