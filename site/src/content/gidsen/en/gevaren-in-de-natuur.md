Poison Centre: **070 245 245**, free, 24 hours out of 24, if the network works. Write the number down in advance on a paper list of important numbers. If life is in danger: **112**.

**If you cannot get through**

- Try another phone or let bystanders call. Move a few metres and try again.
- In nature you do not always have signal. Speak to people you meet and ask them to go to a place with signal and call 112 from there.
- Only leave the casualty alone if there is no other option. If you are in a group, send two or more people to raise the alarm; the others stay with the casualty. Tell them where it is, what happened, how many casualties there are and what state they are in.
- If you are alone with the casualty, first help them as well as you can, then go and get help.
- See also Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call, and the guide on signals and emergency codes.

## Ticks and Lyme disease

**Where and when.** Ticks are present all year round in woods, heathland, meadows, parks, gardens and even dunes, mainly on the ground and in low vegetation. They are most active from April to September inclusive; most bites are reported in May, June and July. A tick does not jump and does not drop from a tree: it climbs onto tall grass, ferns and shrubs of 20 to 70 cm and then crawls over your body.

**Prevention**

- Stay on the paths, avoid dense vegetation.
- Wear clothing that covers as much as possible. Tuck your trouser legs into your socks.
- Wear light colours: then you can see ticks better.
- Use an insect repellent on uncovered skin.
- Take a bath or shower as soon as possible after a walk.
- **Check your whole body** after every visit to nature: head, hairline, behind the ears, armpits, navel, groin, between the buttocks, backs of the knees, between the toes. Ask someone to help or use a mirror. Check others too.
- Put clothing in the tumble dryer at a high temperature.

**Removing a tick**

1. Do it **as soon as possible**. The chance of infection is small if the tick is removed within 24 hours.
2. Grasp the tick by the head with a tick remover or sharp tweezers, as close to the skin as possible.
3. Pull it out slowly and in one movement, without twisting.
4. If a small piece of the mouthparts stays behind, that is harmless. Ask your doctor for help to get it out.
5. Then clean the small wound and your hands with alcohol or with soap and water.

**Monitoring.** Watch the site of the bite **for a month**. For example, set a weekly reminder for four weeks. Ordinary small red spots or bumps disappear within a week.

**See your GP** if you get a red patch or ring that grows larger (usually a few days to weeks after the bite, to at least 5 cm), or flu-like symptoms such as fever, headache, muscle or joint pain. Without treatment, the bacterium can cause problems with the nerves, heart or joints months to years later.

**Tick-borne encephalitis (TBE)** is a viral disease that ticks can transmit. In Belgium, infected ticks are rarely found. A vaccine exists, but is currently not recommended for staying in Belgium. If you go on holiday abroad, vaccination may be advisable.

You can report a tick bite to TekenNet, a research project of Sciensano.

Source: [Poison Centre: Tick bites](https://www.antigifcentrum.be/Toxinfos/tekenbeten/) · [Flemish Government: tekenbeten.be](https://www.tekenbeten.be)

## The adder

**The only native venomous snake in Belgium** is the common adder (Vipera berus). It occurs only in the Upper Meuse basin and in the Antwerp Kempen. It usually has a dark zigzag pattern on its back and vertical pupils. The adder is shy and bites mainly when it is picked up, surprised or cornered. **Always leave a snake alone.**

**Bitten: what to do**

1. An adder bite is rarely fatal, but must always be seen by a doctor quickly. **Call 112** or have someone call. If you cannot get through, see Overview, If you cannot get through.
2. Stay calm and move as little as possible.
3. Reassure the casualty.
4. Keep the bitten part of the body as still as possible.
5. Remove rings, bracelets, shoes and anything that could become tight before swelling starts.
6. Let the casualty sit or lie down comfortably.
7. Wash the wound carefully with soap and water if that is easy to do.
8. Only take a photo of the snake if you can do so without risk, from a safe distance.

**Do not**

- Do not suck out the bite, not even with a suction pump.
- Do not cut into or cauterise the wound.
- Do not tie off the limb. No pressure or constricting bandage.
- Do not try to catch or kill the snake.

**What can happen.** Not every bite injects venom. If it does, pain and swelling, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and a rapid heartbeat can develop within half an hour to a few hours. In serious poisoning, the swelling spreads and a drop in blood pressure, bleeding or a severe allergic reaction may follow. **Children are at greater risk**: the same amount of venom is a larger dose per kilo of body weight for them. In hospital, the symptoms are monitored; in serious poisoning an antivenom may be needed.

Source: [Poison Centre: Adder bites](https://www.antigifcentrum.be/Toxinfos/adderbeten/)

## Wasps, bees, bumblebees and hornets

One sting on the arms, legs or trunk is usually not dangerous. Pain, redness, itching and swelling are normal and can last a few days. If there is only a local reaction, you do not need to contact the Poison Centre.

**What to do**

1. Bee sting: remove the sting left behind as soon as possible. How quickly matters more than how.
2. Clean the area with water.
3. Cool for about 15 minutes.
4. Sting in the hand or fingers: remove rings before the swelling increases.
5. With a large swelling: keep the arm or leg raised.

**Call 112 immediately** or have someone call in case of a severe allergic reaction, which can occur after just one sting:

- swelling of the tongue or throat;
- difficulty swallowing, speaking or breathing;
- wheezing or difficult breathing;
- widespread hives together with other symptoms;
- severe dizziness, weakness or fainting;
- loss of consciousness.

If you cannot get through, see Overview, If you cannot get through.

If someone has an adrenaline pen because of a known allergy, use it according to their personal emergency plan and call 112 or have someone fetch help.

**Sting in the mouth or throat.** The swelling can narrow the airway. Always have this seen by a doctor urgently. In case of shortness of breath or rapidly increasing swelling: call 112.

**Many stings at once** can cause poisoning even without an allergy, sometimes with a delay. Observation in hospital is advisable with more than 5 stings in a small child, more than 12 hornet stings, more than 20 wasp stings or more than 50 bee stings in an adult. Also seek help with fewer stings if there are general symptoms such as weakness, headache, dizziness, vomiting, diarrhoea or fever.

**Sting in the eye:** have the sting removed, rinse with water and have an eye specialist look at it.

**See a doctor** if a large swelling causes a lot of discomfort, if pain or redness increases after a few days, or in case of fever or signs of infection.

Source: [Poison Centre: Stings from wasps, bees and hornets](https://www.antigifcentrum.be/Toxinfos/steken-van-wespen-bijen-en-hoornaars/)

## Oak processionary caterpillar

From May to July, in certain areas, hairy caterpillars live on oak trees. They have **stinging hairs** with tiny barbs that easily penetrate the skin, eyes and airways. Everyone reacts differently, and people who come into contact more often can react more and more severely. In the worst case, eye contact can cause blindness.

**What you see**

- Skin: within 8 hours a painful red rash and intense itching, lasting up to 2 weeks. Sweat or scratching spreads the hairs.
- Eyes: within 1 to 4 hours itchy, red, painful eyes.
- Inhaled: a lot of sneezing, sore throat, swallowing problems, sometimes difficulty breathing.
- Swallowed: a lot of saliva, inflamed mouth, vomiting, abdominal pain.
- Serious: generally unwell, fever, sweating, difficulty breathing due to swelling in the mouth and throat, fainting.

**What to do**

1. Do not scratch or rub.
2. Take off all clothing. Wash it as long and as hot as possible and dry it in the tumble dryer.
3. Brush the hair out thoroughly.
4. Rinse the skin and eyes thoroughly with water.
5. Shower and wash the hair with soap and water.
6. Hairs that are stuck: press sticky tape onto the skin and pull it off quickly.
7. Swallowed: drink a large glass of water.
8. Irritated eyes: go to an **eye specialist**. Serious symptoms: **call 112** or have someone fetch help.
9. Notify the municipality so that the caterpillars can be removed.

**Prevention.** From May to September, avoid the vicinity of affected trees (oak, beech, birch): you can recognise them by branches that have been stripped bare and nests with hairs and moulted skins. Keep playing children away. If there is no other option, wear gloves, long sleeves, long trousers, a cap and glasses.

Source: [Rode Kruis-Vlaanderen: First aid for irritation caused by oak processionary caterpillars](https://www.gezondheidenwetenschap.be/eerste-hulp/eerste-hulp-bij-irritatie-door-eikenprocessierupsen)

## Poisonous plants and berries

Young children often put berries or leaves in their mouths. Usually this ends well: only one in seven children develops symptoms, mainly nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain and diarrhoea. Most berries in Belgium cause no or few symptoms; one berry is usually of little danger.

**How plants cause harm**

| Way | Examples from the source |
| --- | --- |
| Swallowing | Many plants are poisonous when ingested, in whole or in part (leaves, berries, fruits, bulbs) |
| Skin irritation from plant sap | Spurge, greater celandine |
| Burns from sap and sunlight | Giant hogweed, common rue. The risk remains for up to a few days after contact. |
| Eye injuries from sap | Spurge, greater celandine |
| Wounds from thorns and spines | Firethorn, crown of thorns |

**What to do after swallowing**

1. Remove the plant remains from the mouth and give a glass of water.
2. Contact the Poison Centre (070 245 245, if the network works) or the GP. Have the name of the plant to hand.
3. If you do not know the name: take a whole branch with leaves and flowers or fruits to a garden centre, florist, pharmacist or plant expert, or take several photos.
4. If a child has eaten several berries, contact the GP or the Poison Centre.

There is no simple rule for recognising poisonous plants. Do not eat wild plants that you do not know for certain. Picking is also prohibited in Flemish woods and nature reserves.

Source: [Poison Centre: Poisoning by plants](https://www.antigifcentrum.be/Toxinfos/vergiftiging-door-planten/) · [Poison Centre: Berries](https://www.antigifcentrum.be/Toxinfos/bessen/) · [US Army FM 21-76](https://archive.org/details/fm-21-76-survival-manual) · [Vlaanderen.be: What are you allowed to do as a visitor in Flemish nature?](https://www.vlaanderen.be/natuur-milieu-en-klimaat/wat-mag-u-als-bezoeker-doen-in-de-vlaamse-natuur)

## Poisonous mushrooms

- There are **no tricks** to tell an edible mushroom from a poisonous one. Colour, smell, taste, age or the fact that slugs eat it tell you nothing.
- The death cap can be white, is said to taste good and remains deadly after cooking, drying or freezing. One specimen can be very serious.
- Identifying with a book or photo is very dangerous.
- In Flanders and Brussels, picking mushrooms in public areas is prohibited.

**About 60 per cent** of the calls about mushrooms to the Poison Centre concern children who accidentally ate a piece. That is usually not very serious, but sometimes identification is needed. The Poison Centre works with a network of mycologists who can help quickly. A meal with mushrooms you picked yourself is less often a problem, but potentially much more serious.

**Contact the Poison Centre** if the network works, and have this information ready: which mushrooms, where they were picked, how long between picking and eating, how they were stored and prepared, whether there are any remains (raw, cooked, peeled), which symptoms, how long after eating the first symptoms appeared, which medication and medical conditions, whether everyone who ate them is ill, what else was eaten and drunk.

Source: [Poison Centre: Tips for mushroom pickers](https://www.antigifcentrum.be/Toxinfos/tips-voor-paddenstoelenplukkers/) · [Poison Centre: Calls about mushrooms](https://www.antigifcentrum.be/Toxinfos/oproepen-rond-paddenstoelen/)

## Jellyfish on the coast

There are no deadly jellyfish on the Belgian coast, but their stinging cells can sting very unpleasantly. Even dead jellyfish washed up on the beach can still sting.

**What to do**

1. Do not touch the area. Wash your hands and put on disposable gloves if you have them.
2. Rinse as soon as possible with **seawater**.
3. Go to a beach first aid post. There the area can be placed in water as warm as possible (not too hot) for 20 to 30 minutes. If there is no warm water, cooling can help.
4. Visible tentacles: remove them with tweezers. Not visible: apply shaving foam and scrape it off with the edge of a bank card, from the fingers or toes upwards.
5. **Do not rub**, not even with sand. **No vinegar** on the North Sea. **No urine.** No pressure bandage.

**Call 112** or have someone call in case of general reactions: difficulty breathing or swallowing, heart rhythm disturbances, loss of consciousness, feeling unwell, abdominal cramps, vomiting, itching and redness all over the body.

Source: [Rode Kruis-Vlaanderen: First aid for a jellyfish sting](https://www.gezondheidenwetenschap.be/eerste-hulp/eerste-hulp-bij-een-kwallenbeet)

## Thunderstorms outdoors

**Recognising them.** Small tufts of cloud with turrets (altocumulus castellanus) can indicate that a thunderstorm will follow later in the day. Tall, column-shaped heaped clouds (cumulonimbus) announce a thunderstorm nearby. You rarely hear thunder further away than 10 km. A hissing sound indicates immediate danger of lightning.

**The 10-second rule.** Count the seconds between the flash and the thunder and divide by three: that is roughly the distance in km. **If you count 10 seconds or less, look for a safe shelter at once.**

**Where to shelter**

| Choice | Place |
| --- | --- |
| Safe | A sturdy building, away from the windows, windows and doors closed |
| Safe | A car with a metal body and roof (not a convertible, not plastic), windows and doors closed. A metal caravan, with the power cable disconnected. |
| No protection | Tent, bus shelter, carport, open shelter, golf buggy |

**If you are in open country and cannot shelter anywhere**

- Take up the safety position: crouch on your toes, arms around your knees, head as low as possible, hands over your ears, feet together. **Never lie flat.** If possible, crouch in a ditch.
- Do not walk during a lightning strike: stand still and crouch.
- In a wood: look for a group of low trees in the lowest part of the terrain. **Never under a solitary tree.**
- Stay away from tall trees, lampposts, towers, high-voltage lines, hilltops, the edges of woods, metal structures, open water. Stay at least 3 metres from gates and fences.
- Put metal objects down: tent poles, bicycles, fishing rods, golf clubs. Do **not use an umbrella**.
- Get out of the water, including indoor swimming pools.
- Get off your bicycle, moped, motorbike or horse and look for shelter.
- In a group: spread out, at least 2 metres apart.

**Someone has been struck.** Call 112 or have someone call (if that does not work, see Overview, If you cannot get through). A person who has been struck is **not electrically charged**: you can touch them straight away. If they are not breathing, start resuscitation: only one in three of the people struck dies, and those who are resuscitated quickly and for a long time often survive. Cool burns with cold or lukewarm water.

**After the thunderstorm:** do not touch cables lying on the ground.

Source: [RMI: How do I protect myself against lightning?](https://www.meteo.be/nl/switch-language/634?nis=21004) · [National Crisis Center: Lightning](https://crisiscenter.be/en/risks-belgium/natural-risks/lightning)

## Heat and cold outdoors

**Heat**

- Drink more than usual, do not wait until you are thirsty. Water is better than soft drinks.
- Put on a cap or hat, wear light clothing in light colours, use sun cream with a high protection factor.
- Avoid heavy exertion, especially in the hottest part of the day. Wet your forehead and neck regularly.
- Never leave people or animals behind in a parked car.
- **Heatstroke** is life-threatening. Signs: headache, dizziness or fainting, vomiting, cramps, red skin, first heavy sweating and then no more sweating, convulsions, confusion. Call 112 or have someone fetch help in case of impaired consciousness, convulsions, no longer sweating, being unable to drink without vomiting, or above 39 °C. Cool down immediately in the shade, with wet cold cloths in the groin, armpits and neck, cold water, a fan.

**Cold**

- Put on several layers and cover your whole body, including your head.
- Keep your clothing dry and avoid sweating.
- **Hypothermia**: uncontrollable shivering, later the shivering stops, pale cold skin, blue lips, confusion, drowsiness. Call 112 or have someone fetch help if someone stops shivering, becomes stiff or drowsy. Move them horizontally, remove wet clothing, cover them so they are dry and warm, **do not rub them warm**.
- **Frostbite**: with mild frostbite the skin becomes dull and whitish, with deep frostbite the tissue becomes hard and immobile. Feet, hands and the face are the most vulnerable. Check each other's faces in a group. Do **not thaw** frozen parts **by an open fire**.

See also the scenarios on heat and cold.

Source: [National Crisis Center: Heatwave](https://crisiscenter.be/en/risks-belgium/natural-risks/heatwave) · [Rode Kruis-Vlaanderen: First aid for heatstroke](https://www.gezondheidenwetenschap.be/eerste-hulp/eerste-hulp-bij-hitteslag-of-hitteberoerte) · [National Crisis Center: Cold wave](https://crisiscenter.be/en/risks-belgium/natural-risks/cold-wave) · [Rode Kruis-Vlaanderen: What to do in case of hypothermia](https://www.gezondheidenwetenschap.be/eerste-hulp/wat-doen-bij-onderkoeling) · [US Army FM 21-76](https://archive.org/details/fm-21-76-survival-manual)

## Water and currents

**Where swimming is allowed**

- **Sea:** only safe where qualified lifeguards are on duty. In July and August, 82 lifeguard posts are open along the coast, every day from 10:30 to 18:30. In May, June and from 1 to 15 September there are fewer. Signs and coloured flags on the beach show where and when you may swim.
- **Rivers, canals, harbour docks and other navigable waterways:** swimming is prohibited where it is not explicitly permitted.
- **Recreational ponds:** swimming is prohibited.
- **Nature reserves and woods:** usually not. Many bodies of water are polluted, too deep, too cold or dangerous in some other way. Only where there is a sign with a green swimmer. Swimming illegally can result in a fine.
- Pay attention to signs about **blue-green algae**.

**Floodwater**

- Do **not** walk, swim or drive through floodwater. Just 15 cm of flowing water can knock you over, 30 cm can sweep a car away.
- Stay away from bridges over fast-flowing water.
- If your car is stuck in fast-flowing water, stay in it. If the water rises inside the car, climb onto the roof.
- Floodwater can be polluted and contain dangerous debris.

**Someone is in difficulty in the water**

1. First make sure you are safe yourself.
2. Try to rescue **without going into the water yourself**.
3. Only go into the water if you are trained for it. A drowning person who is conscious will cling to the rescuer in panic.
4. Call 112 or have someone call. If you cannot get through, see Overview, If you cannot get through. Every drowning casualty must be examined, even if it does not seem serious.
5. Take the casualty out of the water **horizontally**.
6. If they are not breathing: start resuscitation. After a dive into shallow water: consider a neck injury.
7. If conscious: to a warm place, remove wet clothing, dry them, cover them warmly, do not rub them warm.

## Wild boar

Since the early 2000s there have been more and more wild boar in Flanders, including in residential areas. They are naturally shy of people, but intelligent. If people feed them, they learn to associate people with food; then they lose their shyness. They remain **wild animals that can react unpredictably**. They often cross the road without warning. They can carry pathogens: **avoid close contact**.

**What the Agentschap Natuur en Bos recommends**

- Do not feed them. Do not leave waste or food scraps behind.
- Screen off rubbish bags and compost heaps.
- Keep your dog on the lead when walking.
- A sturdy fence keeps them out of the garden: dug in at least 20 cm, mesh size at most 10 cm.
- Report damage or nuisance to the municipality.

In Flemish woods and nature reserves, dogs must always be kept on a lead, except in dog exercise areas.

Source: [Agentschap Natuur en Bos: Wild boar nearby?](https://natuurenbos.vlaanderen.be/sites/default/files/documenten/Hoe%20omgaan%20met%20everzwijnen.pdf) · [Vlaanderen.be: What are you allowed to do as a visitor in Flemish nature?](https://www.vlaanderen.be/natuur-milieu-en-klimaat/wat-mag-u-als-bezoeker-doen-in-de-vlaamse-natuur)

## The wolf in Flanders

The chance of meeting a wolf by accident is very small. Wolves are mainly active at night, shy, and well camouflaged. According to the Agentschap Natuur en Bos and the INBO, the number of incidents in our Western environment is extremely small. The chance of being injured by a dog is much greater than by a wolf. The dangerous ones are mainly wolves that have been fed by people and associate people with food.

**If you come face to face with a wolf**

1. Stay calm. The animal will probably run away quickly.
2. If the wolf does not leave by itself, chase it away: make yourself big and threatening, shout loudly, clap your hands, make large movements with your arms.
3. Keep your dog on the lead. A wolf may see a dog running loose as an intruder or prey and attack it.
4. **Never feed a wolf.**
5. Report the sighting later via wolf@inbo.be, if the internet works.

A wolf that is no longer shy of people or behaves boldly is considered a problem wolf. The Agentschap Natuur en Bos and the INBO closely monitor the behaviour of the wolves in Flanders.

Source: [Agentschap Natuur en Bos: Q&A on the current wolf situation in Limburg](https://natuurenbos.be/sites/default/files/2025-09/Q%26A_Actualiteit_wolf_Limburg_20250909_V2.0.pdf) · [Agentschap Natuur en Bos and INBO: Frequently asked questions about the wolf](https://natuurenbos.be/sites/default/files/2024-04/Veelgestelde_vragen_over_wolf_versie_04-2024.pdf)
