## What happens in a nuclear accident

A nuclear power plant uses nuclear fuel to make steam and generate electricity. In an incident, heat and pressure build up. The steam can then be released outside the plant together with radioactive substances.

In the Belgian national risk analysis, the National Crisis Center describes a serious scenario as follows: damage to the nuclear fuel leads to a partial core meltdown, the containment systems fail and large amounts of unfiltered radioactive substances are released. In that scenario the substances are only released 24 hours or several days after the incident, over a long period. According to the frequently asked questions on nucleairrisico.be, in most accident scenarios several hours pass between the first signs of a problem and the first release. The authorities use that time to issue warnings and distribute iodine tablets.

In an accident, radioactive substances can be released into the air, into water or onto the ground. You cannot see, smell, taste or feel radioactivity. Only measuring instruments detect it.

### Contamination and irradiation

| Term | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| External contamination | Radioactive substances get onto skin, hair or clothes |
| Internal contamination | Radioactive substances enter the body through inhaled air or contaminated food |
| Irradiation | A radioactive source emits radiation onto your body from a distance, without direct contact |

According to the Crisis Center, the risks for the population are mainly long-term risks: cancer and genetic abnormalities. The probability of complications, not their severity, increases with the dose. Specific symptoms such as vomiting, burns and massive hair loss only occur after massive exposure. In principle this is only expected among workers at the installation and possibly among emergency responders. The WHO says the same: the general public is unlikely to receive a dose high enough for acute effects.

## The Belgian sites and zones

In Belgium there are nuclear power plants at Doel and Tihange, the Belgian Nuclear Research Centre (SCK CEN) and Belgoprocess at Mol-Dessel, the Joint Research Centre (JRC) at Geel and the National Institute for Radioelements (IRE) at Fleurus. Just across the border are the nuclear power plants of Borssele (the Netherlands) and Chooz (France). Chooz is less than 5 km from the province of Namur, Borssele about 15 km from East Flanders.

### Emergency planning zones

In an emergency planning zone, the authorities and emergency services prepare the protective measures in detail in advance.

| Site | Sheltering | Iodine | Evacuation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Doel (nuclear power plant) | 20 km | 20 km | 10 km |
| Tihange (nuclear power plant) | 20 km | 20 km | 10 km |
| Mol-Dessel (SCK CEN, Belgoprocess) | 20 km | 20 km | 4 km |
| Fleurus (IRE) | 10 km | 10 km | not prepared |
| Borssele (the Netherlands) | 20 km | 20 km | 10 km |
| Chooz (France) | 20 km | 20 km | 10 km |

Around Fleurus, evacuation is not specifically prepared, given the size of the site and the possible accident scenarios.

### Extension zones

The nuclear emergency plan also provides for extension zones, in which the measures can be extended on the basis of established principles: 100 km for sheltering and iodine, 20 km for evacuation around the nuclear power plants. The 100 km zone also applies around the French nuclear power plants of Gravelines and Cattenom. Gravelines is about 30 km from West Flanders, Cattenom about 35 km from the province of Luxembourg. A 100 km zone covers almost the entire Belgian territory.

### The zone at the time itself

In an emergency, the assessment of the situation determines the zone for protective measures. It can be smaller or larger than the emergency planning zone. Check in advance whether your municipality lies in an emergency planning zone: nucleairrisico.be has a map for this. The zones do not prevent anyone outside them from preparing as well.

## How you are warned

When you shelter, you are inside with windows and doors closed. The information from the authorities then comes mainly via the radio. The authorities alert local residents via:

- **Radio and TV.** Make sure in advance that you have a battery-powered or wind-up radio, preferably with DAB+ and FM. That way you can listen to the media even during a power cut.
- **Police loudspeaker vans** driving through the streets.
- **BE-Alert**, if the network works: a text message from the number 1789, an email from no-reply@be-alert.be or a voice call from 0477 77 77 55. Register in advance on be-alert.be. You can register several addresses, including that of your children's school.
- **The website of the National Crisis Center or the municipality and social media**, if the internet works.

If you have no landline or mobile phone, you still receive the necessary information: via loudspeaker vans, radio and TV. Do not make unnecessary phone calls, unless you are in a medical emergency, for example: an overloaded network can disrupt the emergency response.

Source: [Nuclear risk: Warning and protecting the population](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/what-are-authorities-doing/warning-and-protecting-population) · [Nuclear risk: frequently asked questions](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/faq) · [National Crisis Center: brochure Prepared together](https://crisiscenter.be/sites/default/files/documents/files/WEB-Brochure%20Prepared%20Together-EN.pdf) · [National Crisis Center: An emergency kit at home](https://crisiscenter.be/en/what-can-you-do/build-emergency-kit/emergency-kit-home)

## The four protective measures

| Measure | When | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sheltering | The first and most effective measure | Stay indoors, windows, doors and ventilation closed |
| Iodine tablets | Only in a release of radioactive iodine and only when instructed | Take them when the authorities say so (see the guide on iodine) |
| Evacuation | Exceptionally, if the exposure is unacceptable | Leave according to the instructions, never on your own initiative |
| Protection of the food chain | After the release | Follow the restrictions on vegetables, milk, water and livestock |

Evacuation does not take place at the moment the cloud passes, nor when there is too little time to clear the area before the cloud arrives. In that case the recommendation is to shelter. An evacuation may still follow afterwards.

Source: [Nuclear risk: Warning and protecting the population](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/what-are-authorities-doing/warning-and-protecting-population) · [Nuclear risk: frequently asked questions](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/faq)

## Sheltering: what it means exactly

In a nuclear emergency, sheltering is the best way to protect yourself, against irradiation and against contamination.

### At home

1. Stay indoors or go into the nearest building. Stay indoors until you are told that it is safe again.
2. Close all windows and doors.
3. Switch off all ventilation, heating and air conditioning. Shut off the supply of outside air from the chimney.
4. Also seal other openings where outside air comes in: broken windows, open fireplaces, the cooker hood in the kitchen.
5. Make the bottom of doors draught-proof with damp cloths.
6. Do make sure the air is refreshed, so that you do not feel unwell.
7. Settle in a central room on the ground floor. Walls and ceilings protect better than windows.
8. Bring your pets indoors.
9. Listen to the recommendations on the radio. If the internet works, also check the official websites and social media.

### If you were outside when the cloud passed

Go in through a room you do not use. Undress, take a shower and put on other clothes before you join the other people sheltering there.

### By location

| Where you are | What you do |
| --- | --- |
| At home | Follow the steps above |
| At work | Shelter where you are; follow the internal emergency plan |
| At school | Leave your children at school; they follow the same recommendations |
| In a shop, stadium, cinema | Stay indoors; institutions in the zones are asked to provide iodine tablets for staff and visitors |
| On foot | Go into the nearest building as quickly as possible and close windows and doors; that protects better than a car |
| In the car, close to home, work or a public building | Go indoors immediately |
| In the car, no building within reach | Park in a safe place or drive away, perpendicular to the wind direction. Engine off when you are stationary, windows closed, air conditioning and ventilation off, radio on. Drive carefully and follow the highway code |
| On foot in an area without buildings | Call 112 (general sheltering guideline of the Crisis Center). If you cannot get through: move further away from the emergency until you find a safe and accessible building (Crisis Center, sheltering on the move) |

### What foreign sources say differently about the location

Germany recommends going as deep as possible in the case of radioactive substances, preferably into a cellar. The United States (CDC) mentions the basement or the middle of the building, as far as possible from outside walls and the roof; a multi-storey brick or concrete building is best, but any building is safer than being outside. The WHO recommends choosing a room without windows and outside doors if possible. The Belgian advice remains: a central room on the ground floor. In Belgium, follow the Belgian advice and the instructions given at the time.

## How long you shelter

In principle, sheltering is limited to a maximum of 24 hours. In a prolonged release, the concentration of radioactive substances indoors eventually becomes as high as outdoors, even with windows and doors closed, because dust particles get in through gaps and ventilation openings. If experts estimate that sheltering must last longer or that the release is very large, a preventive evacuation may follow.

Stay indoors until the authorities say that it is safe or that you must evacuate. Do not go outside because you have taken iodine tablets: they only protect the thyroid.

Source: [Nuclear risk: frequently asked questions](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/faq) · [Nuclear risk: Sheltering, the best protection](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/what-can-you-do/sheltering)

## Time, distance and shielding

The US government (Ready.gov) sums up protection against radiation in three principles:

- **Time:** limit the time you are exposed.
- **Distance:** stay as far away as possible from a radioactive source.
- **Shielding:** put material between you and the radiation by going deep inside a sturdy building. Put as many walls as possible between you and the outside.

Germany says that ionising radiation is weakened as it passes through matter, and that this weakening is particularly great in cellars because of the surrounding earth and the floors above. Thick walls and earth increase the protection. The Swiss canton of Aargau also says that simply staying in a building, for example a cellar or shelter, can effectively reduce exposure to ionising radiation.

The Belgian Crisis Center says that the impact of exposure depends on the duration, the type of material and the distance from the source.

PlanB72 gives no instructions for calculating or measuring radiation doses yourself. Measurements are interpreted by specialists.

## Decontaminating yourself

If you were outside when a radioactive cloud passed, you can reduce the external contamination. The Belgian advice:

1. Take off your clothes and put them in a plastic bag. Put the bag outside.
2. Take a shower and wash with soap.
3. Put on clean clothing.
4. Drink enough.

Sometimes the authorities set up a reception centre where you can report for decontamination and a medical check.

### What the US guidelines (CDC) add

- **Clothing:** removing your outer layer of clothing can remove up to 90 per cent of the radioactive material. Do it carefully, so that no dust is shaken loose that you breathe in. Preferably do not touch your eyes, nose and mouth (Ready.gov). Put the bag far away from people and pets.
- **Shower:** take a warm shower and wash gently with plenty of soap. Do not scrub, scratch or scald your skin. Wash your hair with shampoo or soap. Do not use conditioner: it makes radioactive material stick to your hair. Cover small wounds and grazes while washing.
- **No shower:** wash your hands, face and the parts that were not covered at a tap with soap and plenty of water. No tap: use a wet wipe, a clean wet cloth or a damp paper towel, especially for hands and face. Put used wipes in a sealable bag.
- **Nose, eyes and ears:** blow your nose gently and wipe your eyelids, eyelashes and ears with a wet wipe.
- **Tap water:** even if the authorities recommend bottled water for drinking, you may use tap water to wash.
- **No clean clothes:** take off your outer layer, shake or brush it off and put it back on. Then wash your hands, face and bare skin again.
- **Helping others:** wear waterproof gloves and a dust mask if possible, and afterwards wash your own hands, face and bare skin.
- **Do not use:** household cleaning wipes on the skin. Hand gel does not protect against radioactive material (Ready.gov).

## Food and water

Radioactive material can lead to internal contamination through food or drinking water. Contamination of the food chain comes mainly from radioactivity that is deposited on the ground as the cloud passes over. The authorities can therefore take measures:

- a restriction on or ban of the use of fresh crops;
- a ban on the use of drinking water or surface water; using rainwater to water crops is discouraged;
- a ban on milk from non-commercial channels;
- a grazing ban: (dairy) livestock must be kept indoors, so that no contamination arises through consumption.

### While sheltering

- Food that was indoors is safe to eat (Ready.gov).
- Food in closed packaging that was outside may be eaten if you first wipe the packaging with a damp cloth. Put that cloth in a plastic bag, away from people and animals (Ready.gov).
- Do not eat vegetables from the garden or anything that was outside uncovered until the authorities say it is safe (Ready.gov).
- Food that was packed and sealed before the accident, such as in tins or plastic, is safe (WHO).
- Follow the instructions of the authorities about drinking water and food. The Netherlands mentions a harvesting or slaughter ban as possible measures.

More about food after the accident is in the guide "After the cloud".

## Animals

- **Pets:** bring them indoors when sheltering (Nuclear risk). Wash a pet that was outside carefully with shampoo or soap and water and rinse well (CDC). Ready.gov recommends gently brushing out the coat first.
- **Livestock:** the authorities can impose a grazing ban and ask for livestock to be kept indoors. The WHO recommends, if it can be done safely, moving livestock from the pasture into a shed, covering feed and hay outside with plastic or tarpaulin and closing greenhouses.
- **During evacuation:** people take priority over animals. Those who evacuate with their own transport may take their pets with them as far as possible; with public transport this is in principle not possible. Evacuation of livestock is only considered if the livestock cannot be adequately housed, the area is small enough and there is enough time without a risk of contamination.

## Children at school and in childcare

- Do not go and collect your children. The authorities strongly advise against it. You cause extra traffic jams, hinder the emergency services and risk you and your children ending up in the radioactive cloud.
- Children are in safe hands at school. They follow the same recommendations. Schools must draw up an internal emergency plan and are asked to provide iodine tablets.
- If evacuation is needed, the local authorities, unless otherwise announced, take care of evacuating the children to an assembly point.
- The school announces through its own channels how and when you can collect your child.
- Children are more sensitive to radiation than adults, says the WHO. It is therefore especially important that they follow the protective measures and receive medical follow-up after the emergency as soon as the authorities say it is safe.

## Vulnerable people and medical emergencies

- People who cannot leave the zone under their own steam during an evacuation receive help from the emergency services. During an evacuation, checks are made that everyone has left their home.
- If you need medical help while sheltering, for example for a birth, call 112 as always. If you cannot get through, have someone else call or ask for help nearby (see Emergency numbers in the app, If you cannot call). The police control access to the zone. Under certain conditions and with the necessary protection, emergency services can help in the zone.
- Discuss with your GP in advance what to do with your medication and whether you may take iodine tablets (see the guide on iodine).

## Evacuation

Evacuation is the most exceptional measure. It can be preventive, before the release reaches the zone, or deferred, after the release and sheltering. Never evacuate on your own initiative.

### What you do

1. Stay calm.
2. Shut off water, gas and electricity.
3. Lock the outside doors.
4. Take a minimum with you: identity documents, medication for daily use, some food and drink, mobile phone and charger, baby or children's things.
5. Hang a red cloth on the front door that is visible from the street. That way the emergency services know that nobody is left inside.
6. Go to the assembly point indicated by the authorities. Use the indicated routes and follow the directions of the police and security services.

### If the evacuation comes after the cloud and your car was outside

- Do not touch the outside of the car with your clothes or skin. Open the doors with a cloth.
- Keep the windows closed.
- Switch off the ventilation.
- Switch on the radio.

The police set up a safety zone. Once it has been cleared, you may no longer enter that zone.

Source: [Nuclear risk: Evacuation](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/what-can-you-do/evacuation) · [Nuclear risk: frequently asked questions](https://nuclearrisk.be/en/faq)

## Do not

- Do not evacuate on your own initiative.
- Do not collect your children from school or the crèche.
- Do not take iodine tablets on your own initiative, not even in an accident in a neighbouring country.
- Do not go outside because you have taken iodine tablets.
- Do not make unnecessary phone calls.
- Do not use conditioner when you wash your hair after contamination (CDC).
- Do not eat vegetables from the garden and do not drink milk from non-commercial channels as long as the authorities do not allow it.

## Preparing

- Check whether you live in an emergency planning zone.
- Keep a battery-powered or wind-up radio ready, with spare batteries.
- Register with BE-Alert, for your home address and those of school and work.
- Agree with your family where you will meet if you cannot reach each other.
- Get iodine tablets in the house if you belong to a target group (see the guide on iodine).
- Learn how to switch off the ventilation at home and go through the sheltering steps with your family (Finland).
- Keep tape ready to seal windows, doors and openings (Finland).
- Prepare a set of spare clothing per family member and a bin bag for contaminated clothing, together with packaged food, water and medication (Ready.gov).
