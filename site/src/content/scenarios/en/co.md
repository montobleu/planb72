## Recognise

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a gas without colour, smell or taste. You cannot detect it yourself. It is produced by poor, incomplete combustion of wood, coal, natural gas, butane, propane and paraffin. All heating appliances can produce CO, except electric ones.

In Belgium CO is the main cause of fatal poisoning. Every year a few dozen people die and about 1,000 people end up in hospital. Most accidents happen during the heating season, from October to April. Almost half of the victims are younger than 30. It does not take much: 0.1 per cent CO in the air is fatal within 1 to 3 hours, 1 per cent within as little as 15 minutes.

**Rapid poisoning (acute).** First you get a headache and become dizzy. You feel very tired and you may feel sick and vomit. Anyone who does not realise that they have to leave the room loses consciousness and can then no longer save themselves. At high concentrations, deep coma and death follow quickly. In children, abdominal pain is a common symptom.

**Slow poisoning (chronic).** Anyone who breathes in small amounts over a long period gets vague symptoms: headache, a heavy feeling in the stomach, muscle weakness, difficulty concentrating and changing moods. A doctor does not easily recognise this.

**Difference from flu or a cold.** The symptoms resemble flu. So in winter, do not be too quick to assume it is flu. Think of CO if:

- several people or animals in the house have the same symptoms at the same time;
- the symptoms always occur in the same room and disappear when you leave that room or go outside;
- the symptoms come at fixed times, for example while bathing, showering or washing up;
- the symptoms occur in a bathroom with a gas water heater.

**Signs in the home.** A yellow or orange flame in a gas appliance points to a lack of oxygen. A good flame is blue. Other signs are soot marks on the wall near a gas boiler or water heater, unusually heavy condensation on windows and walls, and corrosion, rust or soot on an appliance.

**Weather.** In still, foggy weather a layer of warm air can lie above the ground. The flue gases then do not rise properly and can blow back into the home. In severe cold we heat more and keep windows and doors shut, so that appliances get too little fresh air.

## Prepare

CO does not get a chance if enough fresh air comes in, the smoke is properly removed and the appliances work properly. An alarm is an extra warning, not a replacement for maintenance.

**1. Have appliances and the chimney checked.** This is the most important measure.

- Have a heating appliance installed by a qualified professional.
- The law regulates the maintenance of central heating. An installation is inspected before it is first used. Installations running on heating oil and solid fuel are serviced every year. Gas installations every 2 years in Flanders and every 3 years in Brussels and Wallonia. Installations older than 15 years are inspected once.
- Have the chimney swept every year by a qualified technician. A gas installation leaves no soot, but you should still have the tightness of the pipes checked regularly.
- A good chimney generally rises one metre above the ridge of the roof. If you are in doubt about the draught, ask a professional for advice.

**2. Make sure there is fresh air.**

- Every open-flued appliance needs openings for fresh air that cannot be closed. The best is an opening directly to the outside.
- Leave ventilation grilles or air vents open day and night.
- Do not seal gaps around windows during severe cold.
- After a renovation with new windows you must provide extra ventilation. Otherwise too little air may come in.
- A cooker hood, a window fan or a tumble dryer venting to the outside draws air out. This can cause flue gases to come back into the room. Do not use them at the same time as a kitchen water heater.

**3. Know your appliance.**

- **Kitchen water heater (type A, not connected to a chimney).** Use it only for the kitchen sink or washbasin, never for a bath or shower. Use it for no more than about ten minutes per half hour. The room needs two openings of at least 150 cm²: a low one near the floor and a high one near the ceiling that leads directly outside. An appliance from before 1989 has no oxygen safety device and is best replaced.
- **Bathroom water heater (type B, connected to a flue).** It does not belong in a bedroom, shower cubicle or toilet. The bathroom door has an unobstructed grille of at least 150 cm². Appliances from before 1996 have no backdraught safety device and are best replaced.
- **Room-sealed appliance (type C).** It takes air from outside and discharges the flue gases outside. The risk of CO poisoning is then non-existent.
- **Coal or wood stove.** Have the correct output calculated. A stove that is too powerful is turned down all the time, which makes it produce more CO. Use only the fuel the manufacturer recommends and only dry, clean wood. Remove the ash regularly. Clean the stove thoroughly at the end of the winter.
- **Portable paraffin or butane heater, decorative bioethanol fireplace.** These appliances have no chimney. Do not use them continuously and never as main heating. Air the room regularly, for example with a door or window left ajar. Do not use them in small spaces such as a caravan and never in a bedroom.

**4. Fit CO alarms.**

- Fit an alarm in every room with a combustion appliance or a flue. Fit it centrally on the ceiling, at a horizontal distance of 1 to 3 metres from the appliance and at least 30 cm from the walls.
- Also fit an alarm in rooms where you spend a long time, such as the bedroom and the living room. In a room without an appliance, fit it at least at shoulder height.
- Do not fit an alarm in the bathroom: the water vapour interferes with it. Fit it in the nearest room, such as the hallway or the stairwell.
- Choose an alarm complying with standard NBN EN 50291 and with a quality mark such as BOSEC, NF, TÜV, Q-label or VdS, with CE marking. Preferably choose a lifespan of 5 to 10 years.
- Test the alarms regularly. Replace them as the manufacturer specifies.
- Make an evacuation plan and practise it with everyone you live with.
- An alarm gives a false sense of security if it is badly placed, if not all risk rooms have one, if it is not suitable or if it is replaced too late.

**5. Renting?** The owner is responsible for repairing the chimney, replacing appliances and wear due to age. The tenant is responsible for regular servicing of the appliances and sweeping the chimney. Report necessary repairs to the owner immediately.

In Brussels the fire service offers a free home visit with tips about CO. A CO alarm is also offered during the visit.

## During

### At home

**The alarm goes off or you suspect CO.**

1. Think of your own safety first.
2. Open windows and doors, if you can do so without risk to yourself.
3. Leave the room as quickly as possible. Make sure everyone leaves the room. Go outside if you can.
4. Switch off the appliance if you can do so quickly and safely.
5. Call 112. Say that it is probably CO poisoning and whether anyone is unconscious.

If you cannot open the windows without risk to yourself, go outside immediately.

**Someone is unconscious.**

- Never enter a room where an unconscious person is lying. You will be overcome yourself. Call 112 immediately.
- If the emergency services cannot be there yet and you can ventilate the room safely: switch off the appliance and bring the casualty out of the room.
- If the casualty is not breathing, start rescue breaths. Mouth-to-mouth resuscitation is safe: it does not put you at risk of CO poisoning.
- If the casualty is breathing, lay them on their side, without a pillow. This way they will not choke on vomit.

**Someone is conscious and answers your questions.** Bring them into the fresh air and call the GP. Call the Poison Centre on 070 245 245 if you have questions. A doctor answers, 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. The call is free.

### Outdoors or on the move

- A caravan, motorhome or tent is a small, enclosed space. Do not use a portable paraffin or gas heater there.
- Never bring a smouldering barbecue inside for warmth, not even into a tent or garage. Smouldering coals give off a lot of CO.

### In the car

- Never leave an engine running in a garage attached to the house, not even with the door open.
- If a vehicle has to run in a detached garage, open the door for fresh air.
- If you drive with the tailgate open, also open the ventilation or windows, so that air flows through the vehicle.
- Have the exhaust checked every year by a mechanic.
- Use a power generator only outdoors, more than about 6 metres from windows, doors and air vents. Never in the house or garage, not even with doors and windows open.

### At work or at school

- CO also occurs at work. People who work with petrol or diesel machines in poorly ventilated spaces are at risk, for example forklift trucks or floor cleaning machines.
- Other places with a risk are underground car parks after large events, poorly ventilated tunnels and indoor karting with petrol karts.
- If several colleagues or pupils get symptoms at the same time, follow the same steps as at home: air, outside, 112.

### Children

- Children are particularly sensitive to CO.
- In children, abdominal pain is a common symptom, as well as headache, dizziness and nausea.
- Children younger than 12 are referred for treatment sooner.
- In a pregnant woman, prolonged exposure to CO can cause the death or disability of the unborn child. The unborn child can have more CO in its blood than the mother.

### Vulnerable people

- Older people and people with a chronic illness, with heart or breathing problems or with anaemia are at greater risk.
- Smokers, and certainly pipe, cigar and water pipe smokers, already have more CO in their blood.
- Anyone who is asleep, has been drinking or is under the influence can die of CO before noticing any symptoms. The CDC recommends an alarm near every sleeping area.

### Pets and animals

- Animals also become ill from CO. If animals and people in the house have symptoms at the same time, that is a warning sign.
- Take pets outside with you if you can do so without risk to yourself. Your own safety comes first.

## Afterwards

- Do not use the appliance again until a qualified professional has checked it. Also call a professional if you see an orange flame, soot, rust or corrosion.
- Also have the chimney and the connection checked. The whole of appliance and chimney must be airtight.
- Have yourself checked by a doctor. Anyone who is conscious calls the GP. After severe poisoning, brain damage sometimes occurs later. A normal blood value does not rule out poisoning, because CO disappears from the blood after a while.
- If you live in a rented home, report the necessary repairs to the owner immediately.
- If no alarm was fitted yet, fit one according to the rules above.

## Do not

- Do not go back inside to fetch belongings.
- Never enter a room where someone is lying unconscious.
- Do not heat your home with a gas oven or a hob.
- Do not burn charcoal indoors, not even in a barbecue or fire bowl.
- Do not use a camping gas stove indoors.
- Do not use a paraffin or gas heater as main heating and never in a bedroom.
- Do not run an engine or generator in the house or garage.
- Do not use a kitchen water heater for a bath or shower.
- Do not use a cooker hood or extractor fan at the same time as a kitchen water heater.
- Do not seal ventilation grilles and gaps.
