## Recognise

**When is it a heatwave**

- A heatwave is a period of at least five consecutive summer days of at least 25 °C, of which at least three are tropical days of at least 30 °C.

**RMI heat warnings**

| Code | Threshold | What to do according to the RMI |
|---|---|---|
| Yellow | maximum temperature of 32 °C or more, or the warning phase of the ozone and heat plan is active | Protect older and frailer people, drink more, keep vulnerable people out of the sun. |
| Orange | maximum temperature of 35 °C or more | Drink regularly, wear lighter clothing, spend the day in cooler rooms, eat light meals, keep doors and windows closed. |
| Red | maximum temperature of 40 °C or more | Drink a lot, keep up your salt levels, rest a lot, stay in a cooled room, avoid direct sunlight. Strictly follow the advice of the authorities. |

At the coast, code yellow also has a threshold of 25 °C in Middelkerke.

**Ozone and heat plan**

- The vigilance phase runs every year from 15 May to 30 September inclusive.
- The warning phase starts when the temperature threshold is exceeded.
- In the vigilance and warning phases the regions take measures. In the alert phase, among other things when ozone concentrations are too high, the federal government takes over coordination.
- An RMI warning and the heat plan are separate from each other: the RMI looks at the weather per province, the heat plan at public health.

**Ozone**

- In prolonged hot weather ozone levels often rise too. Symptoms are shortness of breath, irritation of the eyes and throat, and headache.

**Heat-related symptoms**

| Condition | Signs | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Dehydration | Great thirst, dry mouth, headache, dark urine or passing little urine, fewer tears | Cool down, drink enough, light clothing, rest. See a doctor if someone stops passing urine. |
| Heat exhaustion | Dizziness, fainting, tiredness, headache, heavy sweating, pale clammy skin, nausea, cramps | Go to a cool place, loosen clothing, drink water in small sips, cool down with wet cloths or a cool shower. See a doctor in case of vomiting, worsening or symptoms lasting longer than 1 hour. |
| Sunstroke | Severe headache, nausea, dizziness from sun on the uncovered head. Sometimes fever, drowsiness or loss of consciousness | Go to a cool place, drink water, cool down, rest. Call 112 in case of loss of consciousness. |
| Heatstroke | Body temperature above 39 °C, pulse above 100, restlessness, hot red dry skin, headache, vomiting, seizures, loss of consciousness | This is an emergency. See below. |

## Prepare

**Keeping your home cool**

- Sun shading on the outside keeps the heat out best: roller shutters, awnings or external blinds.
- Extra shade from plants or canopies, a green roof or a green facade also helps to cool.
- Extra roof insulation helps. You can also paint a flat roof white or keep it wet with a thin layer of water.
- If possible, sleep on the side of the home that gets the least sun.
- Have cooling and ventilation systems checked in spring.

**Your health**

- If you belong to a risk group, such as heart patients and older people, ask your GP about the risks of heat.
- Ask your doctor or pharmacist whether your medicines can have a different effect in hot weather, whether you may keep taking them as usual and how to store them.
- Follow the weather forecast via the radio, TV, or the RMI website or app.

**For others**

- Make a list of contact people for an older relative: neighbours, family, GP, home nursing.
- Ask your municipality about neighbourhood help initiatives.
- Help people who need help to prepare.

**Water and power**

- Water and power cuts are particularly difficult in hot weather. Keep bottles of water, other drinks and a charged power bank in the house.
- Find out now where you can cool off if it gets too hot at home.
- Make arrangements with neighbours, family or friends to keep in touch in hot weather.

## During

### At home

**Drinking and eating**

- Drink at least 1.5 litres a day at rest, more than usual, and do not wait until you are thirsty.
- Look at your urine: light yellow means you are probably drinking enough. Dark yellow or passing urine less often means you need to drink more.
- Water is better than soft drinks. Avoid alcohol, which makes you lose fluid faster, and sugary drinks, which are absorbed more slowly.
- Eat light, easily digestible meals and plenty of vegetables and fruit.

**Staying cool**

- Take a lukewarm shower, a footbath or a short dip in the swimming pool.
- Wet your forehead and neck regularly.
- Wear light, loose clothing in a light colour.
- Keep bed linen light and fresh and change it regularly.

**Keeping the home cool**

- Open the windows as soon as it is cooler outside than inside, usually in the evening and at night. Be aware of the risk of burglary.
- During the day, keep sun shading, windows and doors closed when it is warmer outside.
- If you cannot get your home cool enough, go to a cooler place. Possible cool places are libraries, community centres, covered shopping centres, places of worship, parks and woods. Some municipalities set up cool spaces.
- Do not rely on a fan alone. A cool shower or a cool place helps more.

### Outdoors or on the move

- Stay indoors during the hottest hours. If that is not possible, find shade or a cool place.
- Wear a cap or hat, light clothing in a light colour and sunglasses.
- Apply sunscreen with a protection factor of at least 30.
- Avoid heavy exertion or sport, especially in the heat of the day. Exercise or move about in the morning or evening.
- Take water and sun protection with you if you will be out for a long time.
- Expect delays or cancellations of public transport.
- Stay indoors on days with high ozone levels if you are sensitive.

### In the car

- Never leave people or animals in a parked car. The temperature rises very quickly inside, even on mild days and in the shade.
- Do not leave medicines in a hot car. Use a cool bag if necessary.
- Take water with you for long journeys.

### At work or at school

**Employees' rights**

The maximum temperature at work is measured with a WBGT meter, which also measures humidity and radiant heat. The limit depends on how heavy the work is:

| Work | Maximum WBGT |
|---|---|
| Light or very light (office work, manual work at a table) | 29 |
| Moderate (standing work) | 26 |
| Heavy | 22 |
| Very heavy (groundwork) | 18 |

If the limit is exceeded, the employer must, among other things:

- provide sunshades or headgear for those working in the sun;
- provide suitable refreshing drinks free of charge;
- install a ventilation system in work areas within 48 hours;
- allow rest periods if the discomfort continues.

When ozone levels are high, recommendations apply such as doing heavy work in the morning or early afternoon, working indoors or in the shade and taking breaks indoors. You can ask questions of your employer's prevention adviser.

**School and childcare**

- Children often only feel thirsty when they are already dehydrated. Offer them a drink before they ask for one.

### Children

- Babies have difficulty regulating their body temperature and become dehydrated quickly. Children under 3 cannot always make clear what they feel.
- Give children something to drink before they ask for it. Give small amounts often instead of a lot at once. Avoid ice-cold drinks, which can cause stomach cramps.
- Breastfeed more often. The mother drinks extra herself.
- Seek medical advice if a baby has fewer than 4 wet nappies in 24 hours, or in case of sunken eyes, extreme drowsiness or irritability, fever, vomiting, diarrhoea or difficulty breathing.
- Provide water toys or a paddling pool to cool off.
- Never leave a child in a parked car.

### Vulnerable people

**Older people**

- Visit or phone older people regularly on hot days. Bring vegetables and fruit for light meals.
- Agree who will call round if you cannot. Make sure they have a working phone.
- Encourage them to drink regularly, even when not thirsty, starting a few days before the heat. Offer variety: water, coffee, tea.
- Offer ways to cool down: a water spray, a cool cloth, a shower or a footbath.
- Watch for signs of dehydration, tiredness and blood pressure. Older people can deteriorate quickly.
- Write down the person's most important details and put them where they can be seen, in case of an urgent hospital admission.

**People with a chronic illness and people taking medicines**

- Heat and sunlight can affect how medicines work. Drinking more can also change the concentration in the blood. Discuss this with your doctor in advance.
- Store medicines as the package leaflet says. If it says nothing, store them in a cool, dry place, not in the bathroom.
- When shopping, go to the pharmacy last and then straight home.
- Ask friends or neighbours to call round or phone on hot days.

### Pets and animals

**Water and shade**

- Change the drinking water at least twice a day. Put down extra bowls that cannot tip over. Keep the water cool, not ice-cold.
- Provide shade and a cool place. Move hutches and cages to a cooler spot. A wet cloth over an outdoor hutch helps.
- Livestock need trees or a field shelter. A metal container is unsuitable. A salt lick helps horses and livestock to replace lost minerals.

**Dogs**

- Walk on grass, early in the morning or late in the evening. Hot paving and tarmac can burn the pads of the paws.
- Do not use a muzzle: it prevents panting, which is how a dog cools down.

**Other animals**

- Cats: do not leave wet food out for long. Stroke the coat with wet hands.
- Rabbits and rodents: move the cage to a cooler spot and put down cool stones. Remove leftover food before it goes off.
- Birds: move cages to a cooler spot and shade aviaries.
- Horses: do not ride in the heat of the day. Do not transport animals at high temperatures.

**Heatstroke in animals**

- Signs: the animal is tired or listless, keeps its mouth open and pants heavily. Dogs and cats with a short snout are most at risk.
- Bring the animal to a cool place immediately.
- Wet its underside with lukewarm water or lay it on a cool, damp towel. Do not use ice-cold water: it narrows the blood vessels and traps the heat.
- Give cool water and let it drink a little every few minutes.
- If it does not improve after a few minutes, contact a vet.

## Afterwards

**In case of heatstroke: act immediately**

1. Call 112 if the person loses consciousness, and follow the instructions of the emergency services.
2. Bring the person to a cool place.
3. Cool them down immediately: spray or pour water over the skin and fan them at the same time. Ice packs in the armpits, the neck and the groin help.
4. Do not give them anything to drink.
5. Immersion in water cools fastest, but is not recommended for older people, children and people with an underlying condition.

**After the heatwave**

- Consult your GP if you continue to feel unwell.
- Keep drinking enough and get enough rest.

## Do not

- Do not leave anyone, nor any animal, in a parked car.
- Do not wait until you are thirsty to drink.
- Do not use alcohol or sugary drinks to quench your thirst.
- Do not do heavy sport or work in the heat of the day.
- Do not give anyone with heatstroke anything to drink.
- Do not cool animals with ice-cold water and do not walk dogs on hot tarmac.
- Do not store medicines in a hot car or in the damp bathroom.
