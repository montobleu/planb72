## Recognise

In an earthquake the ground shakes or trembles. Aftershocks can follow the first shock, sometimes much later.

**Earthquakes in Belgium.** Belgium lies in a so-called stable zone. Even so, larger earthquakes have caused damage here in the past. The Royal Observatory of Belgium mentions, among others, the earthquakes of 1382 and 1449 in the North Sea, 1580 in the Channel, 1692 in Verviers, 1756 in Düren (Germany), 1938 in Zulzeke-Nukerke and 1992 in Roermond (the Netherlands). On average, Belgium has an earthquake of magnitude 5 or more about once every 40 years, and an earthquake of magnitude 6 or more about once every 315 years.

**The greatest danger** is damage to buildings or their collapse. Falling objects, glass, gas leaks, damaged electrical installations and fire are also a risk. The Belgian risk analysis considers a strong earthquake unlikely.

The Royal Observatory monitors seismic activity continuously and alerts the competent services when an earthquake is felt. The municipality, the province or the National Crisis Center then work with their general emergency plans.

Source: [Royal Observatory: FAQ](https://seismologie.be/en/frequently-asked-questions) · [Royal Observatory](https://seismologie.be/en) · [National Crisis Center](https://crisiscenter.be/en/risks-belgium/natural-risks/earthquake)

## Prepare

**At home.**

- Anchor heavy furniture to the wall so that it cannot topple over: bookcases, cupboards, fridge, water heater, TV.
- Hang mirrors and frames securely.
- Do not hang heavy objects above your bed.
- Put heavy and fragile items on low shelves.
- Know where to shut off the gas, water and electricity.
- Check whether your home is in a risk zone. For new buildings: apply Eurocode 8, adapted to your region. This European standard has been in force since 2011 and sets out how buildings must withstand earthquakes.

**With your family.**

- Agree how you will protect yourselves, where you will meet and how you will find each other again.
- Practise taking cover together. Take part in or follow drills.
- Know the official risk information and the safety instructions.

**Emergency kit.** Keep a kit ready with a battery-powered radio, a torch, drinking water, personal documents, essential medication, blankets, spare clothing, tinned food and a first aid kit. The National Crisis Center recommends being self-sufficient for at least 3 days. Ready.gov adds a whistle to call for help.

## During

Protect yourself immediately.

### At home

**Best option: take cover under a sturdy piece of furniture.**

1. Drop to your hands and knees.
2. Crawl under a sturdy table, a desk or a bed.
3. Protect your head and neck with one arm and hand.
4. Hold on to the furniture with your other hand. If it moves, move with it.
5. Stay like this until the shaking stops.

**No sturdy furniture nearby.** Sit or crouch against an interior wall, away from windows. Bend forward to protect your upper body and hold your head and neck with both arms. The Royal Observatory also mentions a load-bearing wall or a pillar as a place to shelter.

**If you are in bed.** Turn onto your stomach and cover your head and neck with a pillow.

**If you are close to an exit.** The National Crisis Center says: if there is an exit nearby, go safely outside to an open space, away from buildings. The Royal Observatory and the American Ready.gov advise staying where you are and not running outside. If you are unsure whether the way out is safe, stay inside and take cover.

**Always.**

- Stay away from windows, mirrors and glass.
- Do not stand in a doorway.
- Do not light a fire.
- Afterwards, listen to the radio for instructions.

### Outdoors or on the move

- Stay outdoors. Go to an open space.
- Stay away from buildings, trees, street lights, power lines and anything that can collapse, such as bridges, cornices and roofs.
- If you are in a public place, shelter somewhere you will not get caught in a crowd.
- Drop down, protect your head and stay like that until the shaking stops.
- If you are at the coast, move as far away from the sea as possible to higher ground. Also stay away from the coast for a long time after the earthquake. Most tsunamis are caused by earthquakes under the seabed.

### In the car

- Stop in a safe place without blocking the road. Leave room for the emergency services.
- Apply the handbrake.
- Stay in the vehicle until the shaking stops.
- Listen to the radio.
- If power lines are lying on the vehicle, stay inside and wait for the emergency services.

### At work or at school

- Take cover in the same way as at home: under a sturdy desk or a sturdy table, or against an interior wall.
- Keep the people you are responsible for informed.
- Never use the lift to leave the building. Take the stairs.
- Practise with colleagues beforehand how to protect yourselves.

### Children

- Do not collect children from school. The school looks after them.
- Teach children beforehand how to protect themselves under a table and hold on.
- Children do not help with clearing up after the earthquake.

### Vulnerable people

- **With a walking stick:** drop down and take cover, or sit on a chair or bed and cover your head and neck with both hands. Keep the stick with you.
- **With a rollator:** lock the wheels if possible, carefully sit as low as possible, cover your head and neck and hold on.
- **In a wheelchair:** lock the wheels, cover your head and neck and hold on.
- After the earthquake, help your neighbours, especially older people and people with a disability, if you can do so safely.
- People with asthma, lung disease or a weakened immune system should preferably not enter buildings with leaks or visible mould.

### Pets and animals

- Protect yourself first. Your own safety comes first.
- If you have to leave your home after the earthquake, take your pet with you if possible. Small pets are sometimes allowed in a reception centre. Follow the official instructions.
- If your animal has to stay behind, leave water and food for at least 24 hours and tell the emergency services how many animals there are and where they hide.

## Afterwards

**First minutes.**

- Expect aftershocks. If you feel one, take cover again and hold on.
- Check whether you are injured yourself. Help others if you can. Call 112 if there are injured people.
- Do not light a fire, do not smoke and avoid sparks.
- If the building is damaged, go outside and away from the building. Do not go back in.
- Always take the stairs, never the lift.

**If you are trapped under rubble.**

- Send a text message or tap on a pipe or wall so that rescuers can find you.
- Cover your mouth with your clothing against dust.
- Use a whistle instead of shouting.

**Gas, water and electricity.**

- Pipes and cables may be damaged. Check the gas, water and electricity.
- If you smell a leak: shut off the supply, open doors and windows, leave the building and alert the authorities.
- If you can return home, shut off the gas and electricity to prevent leaks.
- Have the chimney checked. A damaged chimney can let carbon monoxide (CO) into the house.

**Your home.**

- Check your home for structural damage. If it seems unsafe, do not go in and ask an expert for advice, such as the fire service or a civil engineer.
- Take photos of the damage for your insurance.
- When clearing up, wear protective clothing and sturdy shoes. Do not move heavy rubble alone. Wear a face mask if there is mould.

**Communication.**

- Follow the instructions from the radio and the authorities.
- Only phone in a real emergency. Send a text message instead: that often works better and saves your battery.
- Report dangerous situations to the authorities.
- Offer your help to the emergency services.
- If you felt the earthquake, fill in the survey on seismologie.be. This helps the emergency services.

## Do not

- Do not run outside in panic during the shaking if the way out is not safe.
- Do not stand in a doorway.
- Do not use a lift.
- Do not light a fire, candle or cigarette after the earthquake.
- Do not go back into a damaged building.
- Do not collect children from school.
- Do not phone unless necessary.
- Do not go to the coast after a strong earthquake.
